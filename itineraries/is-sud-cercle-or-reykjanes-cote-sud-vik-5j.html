✨ Highlights
  • Þingvellir: Parliament founded in 930, between two tectonic plates
  • Strokkur: a jet reaching 15 to 20 m every 6 to 10 minutes
  • Gullfoss: two drops of 11 and 21 m in a 32 m canyon
  • Seljalandsfoss: a trail behind a 60 m waterfall
  • Reynisfjara: black sand beach and basalt columns, dangerous waves
  • Blue Lagoon: geothermal water at around 38 °C near the airport
☀️ Weather

Best time to visit: June through August, with temperatures of 9 to 15 °C in Reykjavík in July and nearly 20 hours of daylight in June. In January, expect -2 to 3 °C; in December, daylight lasts only 4 to 5 hours, and storms sometimes close roads, so allow extra time each day. Vík is Iceland’s rainiest coastal town, with around 2,250 mm of rain per year: keep a waterproof jacket handy in every season.

📋 Notes

In winter, daylight lasts only 4 to 5 hours in December, storms sometimes close roads (check umferdin.is every morning), and the northern lights appear from September to mid-April. Another eruption remains possible at any time near Grindavík: the day before day 5, check vedur.is and umferdin.is for access to Grindavík, Routes 43 and 427, and the Blue Lagoon.

Reykjavik

🛏️ Suggested base : Reykjavik
★★★★☆
1

You can’t miss Hallgrímskirkja: at 74.5 m tall, it’s Iceland’s largest church. Architect Guðjón Samúelsson designed its facade with inspiration from columnar basalt, the volcanic rock columns you’ll see at Reynisfjara. In front of the entrance, the statue of explorer Leif Erikson was a gift from the United States in 1930 to mark the Icelandic Parliament’s thousandth anniversary. Take the elevator up the tower (1,500 ISK): in winter, it’s open from 10:00 a.m. to 4:45 p.m.

⏱ 60 min
2

In 2008, Iceland’s financial crisis halted construction of Harpa, the concert hall by the harbor, which had begun a year earlier. The government then decided to fund its completion, and the hall opened in May 2011. Its colorful glass facade, designed in collaboration with artist Olafur Eliasson, also echoes basalt formations. In 2013, it received the European Union’s Mies van der Rohe Award for architecture. Allow 15 minutes to walk here from Hallgrímskirkja.

⏱ 45 min
3

It’s often mistaken for a Viking ship, but Sólfarið, the “Sun Voyager,” is no such thing. Its creator, sculptor Jón Gunnar Árnason, envisioned it as a dream boat and a tribute to the sun, symbolizing hope and freedom. He died in 1989, a year before this steel sculpture was unveiled on August 18, 1990. It stands beside Sæbraut, a few minutes’ walk from Harpa: come at sunset.

Thingvellir

🛏️ Suggested base : Hvolsvollur · 72 km
★★★★☆
1

In 930, Icelanders founded the Alþingi, their Parliament, at Þingvellir, meeting in the open air. The assembly met here every year until 1798. The site has been a UNESCO World Heritage Site since 2004. Descend into Almannagjá, the park’s largest fissure, to Lögberg, the “Law Rock,” where laws were recited before the assembly. Its exact location remains uncertain: a flagpole marks one possible site. Parking costs 1,000 ISK per car.

⏱ 75 min
2

Þingvellir National Park occupies the rift where two tectonic plates—the immense slabs of Earth’s crust that carry the continents—are pulling apart. The North American and Eurasian plates move away from each other here by about 2 cm per year. At the end of Almannagjá, the Öxará River plunges into the fissure, forming Öxarárfoss waterfall. Farther on, beside the lake, the Silfra fissure holds crystal-clear water at 2 to 4 °C, where tour operators lead dives in dry suits.

🛏️ Suggested base : Hvolsvollur · 63 km
★★★★★

The ground steams, the water trembles, then a column shoots skyward: Strokkur, in the Haukadalur valley, erupts every 6 to 10 minutes. Its jet usually reaches 15 or 20 m, sometimes as high as 40 m. 🛏️ Suggested base : Hvolsvollur
★★★★☆
1

You’ll better understand the landscapes you’ll see over the following days after visiting the LAVA Centre in Hvolsvöllur, which opened in 2017 and focuses on Iceland’s volcanoes and earthquakes. Its interactive galleries present eruptions from the last century, with simulated tremors and ash falls. If the skies clear between September and mid-April, head away from Hvolsvöllur’s streetlights to watch for the northern lights. Seljalandsfoss

🛏️ Suggested base : Vik · 53 km
★★★★★
1

Icelanders named it Gljúfrabúi, “she who lives in the canyon”: this 40 m waterfall hides in a cleft in the cliff, a few hundred meters from Seljalandsfoss. A large boulder obscures part of the waterfall. To see it in full, walk along the streambed through the water, wearing waterproof shoes. Be careful in the gorge, where rocks can fall.

⏱ 30 min

Activities

Seljalandsfoss drops about 60 m from an ancient sea cliff. At the end of the last ice age, sea levels were higher, and waves washed against the foot of the Eyjafjöll mountains. As the sea receded, it left this cliff far inland. A trail loops around the waterfall, passing behind the curtain of water, but it may close in winter because of freezing conditions and falling ice: respect the barriers. Wear a waterproof jacket—the spray will soak you in seconds.

Skogafoss

🛏️ Suggested base : Vik · 28 km
★★★★★
1

On sunny days, a rainbow, sometimes a double one, almost always forms in the spray at Skógafoss. This waterfall is 60 m high and 25 m wide. A staircase with 527 steps leads to the top. The Fimmvörðuháls trail starts there, climbing to the pass between the Eyjafjallajökull and Mýrdalsjökull glaciers. Wear sturdy shoes: the spray makes the steps wet.

The treasure of Þrasi, Skógar’s first settler, disappeared behind the waterfall, but the ring from his chest is displayed at Skógar Museum, Skógasafn. Legend has it that local residents pulled it off as the chest slipped from their grasp. The museum brings together everyday objects, turf houses with walls and roofs made of grass-covered earth, and a technology museum. Admission: 3,000 ISK; from September to May, it is open from 10 a.m. to 5 p.m., making it a good place to visit in the late afternoon in winter, when it is already dark.

Vik

🛏️ Suggested base : Vik
★★★★☆
1

If Katla volcano erupted, Vík’s church, Víkurkirkja, would probably be the only building in the village spared by the flood, as it overlooks the sea from its hilltop. Katla has lain dormant beneath Mýrdalsjökull glacier since 1918. By melting the ice, an eruption could send a flash flood as far as the village. Sirens would then warn residents, who might have only 15 minutes to reach safety. Come at sunset for views over the rooftops and the sea.

⏱ 30 min
2

Legend has it that Reynisdrangar, the basalt sea stacks rising from the water at the foot of Mount Reynisfjall, were two trolls pulling a ship. Daybreak caught them by surprise and turned them to stone. You can see them from Vík’s black sand beach. Stay well away from the water, as you should everywhere along this coast, and return early in the morning for soft light on the rocks.

Never turn your back on the sea at Reynisfjara beach. Sneaker waves sometimes surge far up the sand without warning and sweep people away. Dyrholaey

🛏️ Suggested base : Hveragerdi · 122 km
★★★★☆
1

Sailors called it Cape Portland. Icelanders call it Dyrhólaey, “the island of the hill with the door”: the door probably refers to the lava arch rising from the sea in front of the promontory. Standing 120 m high, the promontory is topped by a lighthouse built in 1927. In October 1993, a pilot flew a plane through the arch. Reach the lighthouse by a steep gravel road, or walk up from the lower parking lot, 1.3 km away.

Between late April and mid-August, Atlantic puffins, small seabirds with colorful beaks, nest on the cliffs of Dyrhólaey. Look for them near the upper viewpoint during permitted access hours: in spring, parts of the reserve close to protect nesting birds, particularly from 7 p.m. to 9 a.m. between May and late June. Check the posted restrictions before visiting early in the morning or in the evening. They nest in the tall grass: stay on marked trails and keep well away from the edge, as wind gusts are unpredictable here.

Activities

The more the glacier melts, the trickier the approach becomes: Sólheimajökull, a glacier tongue descending from the Mýrdalsjökull ice cap, retreats by about 60 m a year. A lagoon has formed at its foot. In July 2026, the Icelandic Meteorological Office reported that the ice ridge leading to the glacier was thinning and that rocks were falling along the trail. From the parking lot at the end of Route 221, walk 15 to 20 minutes to the viewpoint, without stepping onto the ice or lingering below the slopes.

Seltun

🛏️ Suggested base : Keflavik · 27 km
★★★★☆
1

A rotten-egg smell, jets of steam, and bubbling mud: at Seltún, the geothermal area of Krýsuvík, the ground is scorching hot. Sulfur colors the slopes yellow and orange. A boardwalk with platforms runs through the site: stay on it, as boiling water may be hidden beneath the ground. In 1999, a nearby borehole caused an explosion that opened a new crater. In freezing weather, the boards become slippery.

⏱ 45 min
2

Locals say that a worm-shaped monster as big as a whale lives in Lake Kleifarvatn. Covering 10 km² and reaching a depth of about 97 m, it is the largest lake on the Reykjanes Peninsula. After two strong earthquakes in 2000, its water level dropped, perhaps because fissures had opened in the lake bed. The water has risen again since then. Route 42 follows its western shore, with pullouts where you can stop.

Gunnuhver

🛏️ Suggested base : Keflavik · 21 km
★★★★☆
1

Brimketill is best admired from its viewing platform: this small pool was carved into the lava by waves, west of Grindavík. Legend has it that the giantess Oddný used to bathe here. The site is unsupervised, and waves sometimes reach the parking lot. Do not climb down onto the rocks, and never swim here.

⏱ 20 min
2

Gunnuhver means “Gunna’s hot spring.” According to legend, the ghost of a woman named Gunna haunted the area about 400 years ago, until a pastor lured her into the spring. Its main mud pool, about 20 m wide, is said to be Iceland’s largest. Steam emissions have increased considerably since geothermal production began nearby in 2006. Stay on the two viewing ramps.

⏱ 30 min
3

Iceland’s first lighthouse, built in 1878 on the cliff of Valahnúkur, was damaged and then demolished with explosives in 1908. Its replacement, the 26 m tall Reykjanesviti, lights the headland from the nearby hill of Bæjarfell. Climb Valahnúkur for views of the coast. Its cliff is made of tuff, a rock formed when lava cooled suddenly in water. In strong winds, keep well away from the edge.

⏱ 40 min
4

The Bridge Between Continents, a footbridge about 15 m long installed at Sandvík in 2002, spans a fissure in the Mid-Atlantic Ridge. This ridge marks the line where the Eurasian and North American plates move apart. The bridge does not actually connect Europe to America: it is symbolic. Recent fissures have opened near the site following earthquakes: stay on the marked paths.

Blaa Lonid

🛏️ Suggested base : Keflavik · 14 km
★★★★☆

Activities

The Blue Lagoon is far from a natural lake: it collects water discharged by the Svartsengi geothermal power plant, which has operated since 1976. The first bathing facilities opened here in 1987. This mixture of seawater and freshwater, drawn from 2,000 m underground, arrives at about 38 °C. Silica, a mineral, gives it its milky color and forms white mud on the bottom. Book ahead and apply conditioner before entering: silica makes wet hair stiff.
