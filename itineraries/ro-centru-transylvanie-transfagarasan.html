Bucuresti

★★★☆☆
1

Outsized, chilling, mesmerizing: the Palace of Parliament leaves nobody indifferent. The second-largest administrative building in the world after the Pentagon, it was commissioned by Ceaușescu in the 1980s at the cost of demolishing an entire neighborhood. Of its roughly 1,100 rooms, a large portion remains empty. Book the guided tour in advance and bring photo ID—without it, you'll be turned away.

⏱ 90 min
2

Get lost in the Old Town, Bucharest's historic heart. This maze of lanes miraculously survived both the 1944 bombings and the communist urban plan that wanted to raze a fifth of the city. Grand old palaces, cafés, and bars crowd Lipscani Street. Go in late afternoon when the terraces start to fill up.

⏱ 60 min
3

Tiny amid the tumult, Stavropoleos Church still holds its own against its neighbors. Built in the early eighteenth century, it conceals a courtyard and cloister of rare calm right in the city center. The stone carvings on its façade are worth lingering over. Entry is free, but respect the silence—it's still an active place of worship.

Sinaia

★★★☆☆
1

Built between 1873 and 1914 for King Carol I, Peleș Castle was the first in Europe to have electricity. Its German Neo-Renaissance façade and carved-wood interiors are breathtaking: allow two hours for the full tour. Look for the secret door in the royal library—the guide loves setting that challenge. Visit early; tour groups arrive fast.

⏱ 120 min
2

Smaller than its neighbor, Pelișor Castle wins hearts through intimacy. Queen Marie's residence, it reveals a Golden Chamber and Art Nouveau interiors designed by Romanian artists. The visit is quick but memorable. Buy a combination ticket with Peleș—it's better value.

⏱ 45 min
3

Sinaia Monastery has watched over the resort for more than three centuries. King Carol I summered here before Peleș was finished. The small monastery museum and frescoes merit a twenty-minute detour. Modest dress required: shoulders and knees covered.

Brasov

★★★★☆
1

Pastel façades, crowded terraces, echoing bells: Council Square grabs you the moment you arrive. At its center stands the Council House, a former town hall built around 1420, now a history museum. It's the natural starting point for any Brașov wander. Grab a terrace seat in the morning, before the afternoon rush.

⏱ 45 min
2

Erected between 1385 and 1477, the Black Church is Romania's largest Gothic church. Its nickname comes from the 1689 fire that blackened its walls. Inside, an 1839 organ sports nearly 4,000 pipes alongside a collection of Anatolian carpets. In summer, organ concerts resound several times a week—check the schedule at the entrance.

⏱ 60 min
3

So narrow your shoulders brush the walls, Rope Street is nonetheless a Brașov attraction. Measuring 1.11 to 1.35 meters wide over barely 80 meters, it ranks among Europe's narrowest streets. The detour takes five minutes but always raises a smile. Early morning, you'll have it to yourself.

⏱ 15 min
4

Mount Tâmpa looms over the Old Town from 960 meters, Hollywood-style 'Brașov' sign included. From the summit, the view sweeps across rooftops and the Carpathians. You can climb via a steep trail or take the cable car. Pack good shoes if you're hiking up.

It's one of the best-preserved in southeastern Europe. Arrive at opening to enjoy the quiet before the groups.

Rasnov

★★☆☆☆
1

Bran

★★★★☆
1

Built in the fourteenth century by Saxon merchants to guard the route between Transylvania and Wallachia, Bran Castle invented itself a legend: that of Dracula. The link to Vlad the Impaler is tenuous, but Queen Marie made it her summer residence in the early twentieth century. Four levels, 57 rooms, narrow corridors. Come early or off-season—the interior fills quickly and tips into kitsch.

Viscri

★★★☆☆
1

Viscri Fortified Church hides on a hillock, off to the side of the village. A Saxon church from the twelfth-thirteenth centuries, fortified in the fifteenth against the Ottomans, it's been UNESCO-listed since 1999. From the bell tower, the gaze carries across the hills. Very busy in season—come in the morning to avoid the bulk of the tour buses.

2

Saschiz

★★☆☆☆
1

Built between 1493 and 1496, Saint Stephen's Church of Saschiz raises beside it a clock tower strikingly reminiscent of Sighișoara's. At one of its windows, a wooden figurine christened Bogdan once marked the quarter-hours. The ensemble has been UNESCO-listed since 1999. A half-hour stop suffices, on the road to Sighișoara.

⏱ 30 min
2

If your legs are up to it, climb to the Peasant Fortress on the hill, two kilometers away. This fourteenth-century peasant stronghold served as the first refuge against Tatars and Ottomans before villagers came to prefer the fortified church. Angle towers and a deep well remain visible. The path climbs steeply—closed shoes advised.

Sighisoara

★★★★☆
1

The Clock Tower dominates Sighișoara from its 64 meters, visible from almost everywhere. A former city gate, it has housed the history museum and a wooden-figurine automaton marking the days of the week since 1898. Climb to the gallery for the view over the rooftops. The current mechanism dates from 1964—stop by on the hour to watch it spring to life.

⏱ 45 min
2

In Vlad Dracul's House, two steps from the tower, Vlad Țepeș was supposedly born around 1431, the distant inspiration for Dracula. His father lived there a few years before decamping to Târgoviște. The ground floor houses a restaurant, the upper floor the so-called birth chamber. It's more anecdote than established fact—go mainly for the old frescoes.

⏱ 30 min
3

Climb the Covered Staircase, or Scholars' Stairs, its wooden steps sheltered from the weather. It leads to the Church on the Hill, the Gothic church crowning the summit, and to the old Saxon cemetery. The ascent is shaded, pleasant even in summer. Allow ten minutes at your own pace.

⏱ 30 min
4

Uneven cobbles, ocher and blue houses, the scent of warm stone: Citadel Square is the heart of Sighișoara's citadel. It's one of the last still-inhabited medieval citadels in Europe, ringed by 950 meters of walls and nine guild towers. Settle at a terrace to soak up the atmosphere. In the evening, once the buses leave, the town changes face.

⏱ 45 min

Activities

If you like exploring, follow the circuit of the old guild towers: Tailors, Tinsmiths, Butchers, Shoemakers, Blacksmiths. Each belonged to a craft of the citadel. The rampart circuit takes an hour at a relaxed pace. A free map from the tourist office near the tower will keep you from walking in circles.

Biertan

★★★☆☆
1

If you could see only one fortified church, Biertan would be a serious contender. The first Saxon site inscribed on UNESCO, in 1993, it was the seat of the Lutheran bishopric of Transylvania from 1572 to 1867. Its treasure: the 1515 sacristy door, whose 19-lock mechanism won a prize at the 1900 Paris World's Fair. The painted great altarpiece, the largest in Transylvania, clinches the case. Don't dawdle—it closes in late afternoon.

Medias

★★★★☆
1

Leaning like Pisa, the tower of Saint Margaret's Church intrigues before you even step inside. Nicknamed the Trumpeters' Tower, it rises nearly 68 meters and leans more than two meters. The Gothic church dates from 1488; Vlad Țepeș was briefly imprisoned there in 1476. Climb if access is open—the view over Mediaș is worth the effort; otherwise, content yourself with the Oriental carpets inside.

Sibiu

★★★★☆
1

Vast and luminous, the Great Square stretches nearly 142 meters, lined with Baroque façades. On its west flank, the Brukenthal Palace (1778–1788) houses one of Romania's oldest art museums, opened in 1817. Look up—the almond-shaped dormers make the rooftops seem to watch you. A terrace, a coffee, and the spectacle of the square can fill a morning.

⏱ 60 min
2

The Council Tower, from the thirteenth century, links the Great Square to the Small Square. Its summit offers the finest panorama over Sibiu's rooftops and the distant Carpathians. The climb is short but the staircase tight. Go in late afternoon for the golden light on the Old Town.

⏱ 30 min
3

They call it the Bridge of Lies, and everyone spins their legend: cheating merchants, faithless lovers. The Bridge of Lies is Romania's first cast-iron bridge, thrown between the Great and Small Squares. It doesn't look like much, but everyone stops. The Small Square around it is more intimate, ideal for dinner.

⏱ 20 min
4

Descend to the Lower Town via the cobbled lanes and the staircase at the foot of the Lutheran Cathedral. Sibiu was a fortified citadel—towers and sections of medieval ramparts remain. Strada 9 Mai, one of the oldest streets, merits a look. Get a little lost—that's where the town is quietest.

Sibiel

★★☆☆☆
1

The Glass Icon Museum of Sibiel guards the largest collection of glass icons in Transylvania—more than 600 pieces. Assembled starting in 1969 by priest Zosim Oancea, these naïve peasant paintings blend Orthodox tradition with techniques from the West. The building hides in the churchyard. Check opening hours—midday break almost every day.

Activities

Narrow cobbled lanes, Saxon and Romanian houses, flocks of sheep—you breathe an unspoiled rural Transylvania. In Sibiel, park in the center; spaces are scarce. An hour's pause suffices to taste the atmosphere.

Balea Lac

★★★★☆
1

Emerging from the tunnel, the shock: Bâlea Lake, a glacial lake perched at 2,034 meters, the high point of the Transfăgărășan. Cetatea Poenari

★★★★☆
1

Here stands the true fortress of Vlad the Impaler, not Bran: Poenari Fortress, perched on its rocky spur. Built in the fourteenth century then strengthened by Vlad Țepeș as a lookout against the Ottomans, it served as his secondary residence. Only ruins remain, but the site is gripping. Open daylight hours only—plan your visit before dark.

⏱ 40 min

Activities

Prepare your calves: 1,480 concrete steps lead to Poenari's ruins through a beech forest, a half-hour ascent. From the summit, the view plunges over the Argeș gorges, Vidraru Dam, and the Făgăraș. Bring water and leave early—there's no shade once you're at the top.

Curtea De Arges

★★★☆☆
1

'A jewel,' traveler Paul of Aleppo called it: Curtea de Argeș Monastery, built from 1515 to 1517 by Neagoe Basarab. Its twisted turrets and bronze birds make it a unique masterpiece. A royal necropolis since 1914, it shelters the tombs of Carol I, Ferdinand, and Queen Marie. Modest dress required, and silence—it's an active monastery.

⏱ 60 min
2

Manole's Fountain, just opposite the monastery, marks according to legend the spot where the master builder crashed. Denied his ladders by the prince, Manole made himself wings and fell; a spring supposedly gushed forth there, his tears. The stop takes a minute, but the story clings to the monument. Taste the water if the fountain is running.

⏱ 15 min
3

Erected around 1352 under Basarab I, the Princely Church of Saint Nicholas was the first necropolis of the Wallachian princes. Its murals, among the oldest in the country, cover the interior from floor to ceiling. More sober than the grand monastery, it visits in twenty minutes. Ask to see the fourteenth-century paintings, often in shadow.
