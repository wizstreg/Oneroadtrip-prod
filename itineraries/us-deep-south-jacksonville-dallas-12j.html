Lake City, FL

Suggested base : Lake City, FL
★★☆☆☆
1

Your first surprise after leaving I-10: Ichetucknee's water is so clear you can see turtles breathing three meters below the surface. Eight springs feed a 9.6 km river, designated a National Natural Landmark since 1971. Water rises from the aquifer at a constant 22°C—cold when it is 35 outside, lukewarm in winter. The state bought the site from a phosphate company in 1970 for $1.85 million, and nothing has changed since. Entry $6 per car, tube rental $7.50. Arrive before 10 AM in summer: the park closes when it reaches 750 visitors, which happens quickly on weekends. Plan a round trip from Lake City for this visit.

60 min
2

A must-see, Blue Hole Spring takes a little effort—an 800-meter walk from the North Entrance through a cypress forest. A first-magnitude spring, meaning a flow of more than 2.8 million liters per minute. The hole descends 12 meters and opens into 180 meters of underwater caverns explored by technical divers. For an ordinary swimmer with a mask and snorkel, it is already spectacular: sand bubbles up from the bottom like an inverted fountain. Avoid 2–4 PM, when it is the park's only crowded swimming hole. Plan a round trip from Lake City to the park, followed by the walk from the North Entrance.

75 min
3

If Florida and the Civil War seem incompatible, Olustee Battlefield will prove otherwise—February 20, 1864, 10,000 soldiers engaged in total, a Confederate victory, Florida's largest Civil War battle. The site has remained largely intact, with a 1.6 km trail and interpretive signs. Annual reenactment in February, the largest in the Southeast. Free admission, open 8 AM to sunset. Twenty minutes east of Lake City, worth combining with Ichetucknee if you have a full day.

60 min

Activities

Float down the river in a tube from Dampier's Landing—1.2 km, 45 minutes to one hour of gentle drifting among cypress trees and turtles. The current does the work; you just hold on to your tube. Strictly prohibited: food, alcohol, single-use plastic, ice, and non-reusable bottles—everything is inspected at the entrance. Bring sunscreen (apply it 30 minutes before entering the water to avoid pollution), a reusable water bottle, and water shoes. Plan a round trip from Lake City for this activity.

90 min

Not exactly a culinary hotspot, Lake City—but Halpatter Brewing Company serves local IPAs and decent burgers in a restored 1902 railroad depot. Locals hang out here until nightfall. For something more rustic, The Patio Place runs a classic Southern buffet (fried chicken, collard greens, mac & cheese) for $14—church cafeteria vibe, and that's exactly why you go.

90 min

Tallahassee, FL

Suggested base : Tallahassee, FL
★★★☆☆
1

Florida's capital isn't Miami or Orlando—it's Tallahassee, population 200,000, perched in the middle of forests. Why here? When choosing a capital in 1824, they split the difference between Pensacola and St. Augustine: Tallahassee, midway, won. The current Florida State Capitol stands 22 stories and 105 meters tall, built in 1977 in a controversial style (some call it a “giant stepladder”). Self-guided visit, public access to the 22nd floor, panoramic views over forests stretching to the Gulf. Free admission.

75 min
2

Right out front, the 1845 Old Capitol belongs to another world—white facade, red dome, crossed Confederate and American flags. Restored in 1982 to its exact 1902 appearance, it now houses a free museum on the state's political history. The rotunda preserves its original stained glass. Small but well done—count on 45 minutes, longer if Florida politics (hanging chads, Bush vs Gore in 2000) fascinates you. Closed Sundays.

60 min
3

The most credible reconstruction of a Spanish mission in North America, Mission San Luis brings 1656 back to life—the provincial capital of the Apalachee people at its peak, with 1,500 inhabitants living alongside Franciscan friars. The complex was burned in 1704 by English colonists from South Carolina during a raid. Excavations since 1948 enabled reconstruction of the chief's house (the largest Indigenous structure ever built in the Southeast, 36 meters in diameter), the church, the fort, and the governor's house. Costumed demonstrations on weekends. $5, worth seeing.

120 min
4

Five officially protected routes make up the Canopy Roads—Old St. Augustine, Centerville, Miccosukee… where 200-year-old live oaks form a leafy tunnel over the roadway. Spanish moss hangs in two-meter curtains. Driving along them at sunset with the windows down is exactly how you picture the South. Centerville Road is the most beautiful, a 26 km loop.

60 min
5

Some scenes from “Tarzan Finds a Son!” (1939) with Johnny Weissmuller were filmed at Wakulla Springs, as was “Creature from the Black Lagoon” (1954)—one of the world's largest freshwater springs, with an average flow of 760,000 liters per minute and a depth of 56 meters. Flat-bottomed riverboat cruises continue ($8, 45 minutes)—alligators, manatees, and herons. A 25-minute drive south of downtown.

120 min

Albany, GA

Suggested base : Albany, GA
★★☆☆☆
1

Turquoise-blue water that looks photoshopped: Radium Springs releases 285 million liters daily at a constant 20°C. Nicknamed “Radium” after a 1925 analysis detected small traces—a false alarm that made its reputation. A 1920s casino-hotel burned in 1982, then was finished off by Flint River floods in 1994 and 1998. What remains: an 11-hectare botanical garden with ruins, fountains, and walkways around the basin. Free admission, Thursday through Sunday, 5 km south of downtown.

75 min
2

Housed in the former Mt. Zion Baptist Church, the Albany Civil Rights Institute tells the lesser-known story of the Albany Movement (1961-1962)—Martin Luther King was arrested here twice, and this is one of the few movements he publicly called a failure. Police Chief Laurie Pritchett's strategy (jailing protesters in neighboring counties to avoid media images) successfully stymied nonviolence. Strategic lesson for later actions in Birmingham and Selma. $6, Tuesday-Saturday.

90 min
3

The Flint RiverQuarium is dedicated to this ecosystem—a single central “blue pool”, 4.5 meters deep, recreates the river: Gulf sturgeon, alligators, alligator snapping turtles, and freshwater mussels. $12, designed for families but genuinely interesting if you enjoy nature.

75 min

Activities

Southern buffet for $12 at Pearly's Famous Country Cooking, open since 1976—fried chicken cooked to order, slow-cooked vegetables. Touristy by Albany standards? More like local and packed daily by residents. No-frills dining room, vinyl booths.

Dothan, AL

Suggested base : Dothan, AL
★★★☆☆
1

Welcome to the “Peanut Capital of the World”: half of America's peanuts are harvested within a 160 km radius of Dothan. This identity is proudly displayed—since 2001, over 90 peanut statues decorated by local artists have been scattered throughout downtown. Each stands 1.20 meters tall, painted to a theme (firefighter, police officer, Elvis, doctor, soldier…). Free map at the Visitor Center for hunting them down. It's kitschy, it's unapologetic, budget an hour on foot.

75 min
2

Officially the state agricultural museum, Landmark Park isn't a museum: it's a reconstructed working farm from the early 20th century on 40 hectares—a 1900 farmhouse, one-room schoolhouse, barn, blacksmith shop, and planetarium. Animals roaming free. This is what the region looked like 100 years ago, before industrial peanut farming. $5, 10 km north of town, two full hours.

120 min
3

The world's largest number of military helicopters under one roof: the U.S. Army Aviation Museum at Fort Novosel (formerly Fort Rucker). 160 aircraft, including unique prototypes—Vietnam Hueys, AH-64 Apaches, OH-6 Cayuses. This is where the U.S. Army trains all its helicopter pilots. Free admission, but access is through a military base: ID required, security check, arrive 20 minutes early. 25 km west of Dothan.

120 min

Activities

Buffet for $14 at Old Mill Restaurant, where regulars have had lunch since 1980—fried chicken, ribs, vegetables of the day. For something more contemporary, Poplar Head Mule Co. downtown reimagines regional cuisine: fried chicken with honey-pepper sauce, mac & cheese with local porcini mushrooms.

75 min

Montgomery, AL

Suggested base : Montgomery, AL
★★★☆☆
1

Probably the most important museum built in the United States in twenty years, the Legacy Museum opened April 26, 2018, created by Bryan Stevenson's Equal Justice Initiative, then expanded fivefold in 2021 in a former cotton warehouse where enslaved people were held before sale. The route follows 400 years in four stages: slavery, racial terror and lynchings, Jim Crow segregation, contemporary mass incarceration. First-person testimonies, holograms, statistics. This isn't a comfortable visit—most people leave in tears. Book ahead, $5 for the Museum + Memorial combo ticket.

150 min
2

A 15-minute walk away, the National Memorial for Peace and Justice is the emotional counterpart to the museum—805 suspended corten steel pillars, one for each American county where a racial lynching was documented between 1877 and 1950. Each bears the victims' names. As you walk beneath the pillars, the ground descends: you end with your head tilted back, as if facing hanged bodies. Brutal and necessary architecture. The Washington Post called it "one of the most powerful memorials created in a generation." Included in the combo ticket.

90 min
3

December 1, 1955, 6 PM: Rosa Parks refuses to give up her bus seat, right here. This triggered the Montgomery Bus Boycott (381 days) and the modern Civil Rights Movement. The Rosa Parks Museum is built on this exact location: bus reconstruction, immersive interface, audio archives. $7.50, budget 90 minutes. More didactic than the Legacy Museum, family-friendly.

90 min
4

On Goat Hill, the Alabama State Capitol carries two histories that don't speak to each other—this is where Jefferson Davis took his oath as President of the Confederacy on February 18, 1861 (a bronze star marks the exact spot in front of the portico), and it's also the terminus of the Selma-to-Montgomery March on March 25, 1965, where Martin Luther King delivered "How Long, Not Long" before 25,000 people. Two opposing histories on the same steps. Free tours, closed weekends.

60 min
5

Two blocks from the Capitol, Dexter Avenue King Memorial Baptist Church is the church where Martin Luther King served as pastor from 1954 to 1960—he planned the bus boycott from his basement office. The building dates to 1889, simple light-wood interior. Guided tours $10 by reservation. The basement is preserved exactly as it was, with the original movement murals.

60 min

Selma, AL

Suggested base : Montgomery, AL · 68 km
★★★☆☆
1

March 7, 1965: 600 peaceful marchers for voting rights are charged by state police with clubs and tear gas on the Edmund Pettus Bridge. The footage broadcast that evening changed the nation. A 760-meter steel bridge built in 1940, named after a Confederate general who was Grand Wizard of Alabama's Ku Klux Klan—the irony hasn't escaped anyone and renaming debates continue. To walk across, start from the Selma Interpretive Center at the corner, budget 30 minutes round trip.

60 min
2

At the foot of the bridge on the east side, the National Voting Rights Museum and Institute tells the full context of the Selma to Montgomery March—8 km on March 7 (brutally stopped), 4 km on March 9 (“Turnaround Tuesday”, a voluntary return after prayer), and the complete 87 km march from March 21–25, 1965, under federal protection. Room dedicated to the 17 often-forgotten women leaders (Amelia Boynton, Diane Nash, Marie Foster). $6.50.

75 min
3

Launch point for all three 1965 marches, Brown Chapel AME Church saw Martin Luther King preach several times between the two attempts. Red-brick building from 1908, two-tower facade, modest interior. The congregation remains active, and the pastor gives tours by appointment. In front of the church, the “Foot Soldiers” monument honors the 600 anonymous marchers. Free, 1 km east of the bridge.

45 min

Activities

Pulled-pork sandwich for $8 at Lannie's Bar-B-Q Spot, in business since 1942—Lannie Travis, daughter of the founder, still works there. House barbecue sauce, fried okra as a side. Working-class dining room, a line on Saturdays at lunchtime. Historical detail: Lannie's was also one of the few places where movement leaders could meet discreetly in 1965.

Meridian, MS

Suggested base : Meridian, MS
★★★☆☆
1

An incredible concentration of talent for a state of three million inhabitants: Elvis Presley, B.B. King, Faulkner, Eudora Welty, Oprah Winfrey, Tennessee Williams, Robert Johnson, Morgan Freeman—all born in Mississippi. The Mississippi Arts and Entertainment Experience (The MAX) opened in 2018 to tell this story, a 5,700 m² building combining interactive exhibits and live performances. $15, budget two hours.

120 min
2

The Gustav Dentzel carousel turns here in Highland Park, with its original paintings from 1896. Twenty-eight hand-carved animals (horses, giraffe, deer, tiger), with a wooden pavilion built for it in 1909. Designated a National Historic Landmark, restored for $700,000 between 2014 and 2017. Rides $1, open on weekends from May to September.

45 min
3

Completed in 1929 just before the crash, the Threefoot Building rises 16 stories and 57 meters—it was originally Mississippi's tallest building, and remains the tallest in eastern Mississippi today. Polychrome glazed terra-cotta facade (red, orange, green, white) on the first two levels, brick above. Commissioned by the Threefoot family (Jews of German origin, their name anglicized from Dreyfus), wholesalers ruined by the Depression six months later. Empty for decades, restored as a Marriott Tribute hotel in 2021. You can go up to the lobby for coffee; the interior has retained its original woodwork.

30 min

Activities

Mississippi's oldest continuously operating restaurant, Weidmann's opened in 1870—formerly frequented by train travelers (Meridian was a major rail junction). Original mahogany bar, pressed-tin ceilings. Classic Southern cuisine, comfort food, decent wine list. Budget $35-45 per person.

Laurel, MS

Suggested base : Hattiesburg, MS · 44 km
★★★☆☆
1

If you watch HGTV, you already know Laurel: it's the town from “Home Town” where Erin and Ben Napier have renovated houses since 2016—eight seasons and over 100 restored homes. Before the show, Laurel had dozens of empty buildings in its historic downtown. Today, fans make pilgrimages. The pedestrian downtown packs 80 shops into six blocks: Laurel Mercantile (the Napiers' store), Scotsman Co. (vintage furniture), bookstores, cafes. You can explore it all on foot in 2–3 hours.

90 min
2

An understated Southern treasure, the Lauren Rogers Museum of Art was founded in 1923 by the Rogers family (timber industrialists) in memory of their son who died at 23. Permanent collection: American paintings (Sargent, Whistler, Homer, Cassatt), Dürer prints, the Southeast's largest collection of Native American baskets with 810 pieces. Free admission. The neoclassical marble building is just as worth seeing as the art.

90 min
3

Explore on foot: North 5th Avenue, Magnolia Street, West Oak Street—downtown residential neighborhoods, perfectly restored Victorian houses and Craftsman bungalows, some seen in “Home Town”. The gardens are well tended, the porches occupied in late afternoon. The atmosphere of a real small Southern town, without the tourist fatigue of Charleston or Savannah. Free.

60 min

Activities

Right next to the Napier studio, Pearl's Diner serves breakfast all day—biscuits and gravy, $5 pancakes. Otherwise, Café La Fleur in a renovated old house serves more modern bistro food (mixed salads, gourmet sandwiches). And Slabtown Pizzeria for low-key evenings.

Hattiesburg, MS

Suggested base : Hattiesburg, MS
★★☆☆☆
1

One of the few museums in the world dedicated solely to African American military history is here—housed in a 1942 USO Club reserved for Black soldiers under segregation. Six wars covered, from the Civil War to Iraq. An entire room on the Tuskegee Airmen, African American pilots who flew 1,491 missions without losing a single escorted bomber in Europe. Free admission, open Tuesday–Saturday.

90 min
2

Funny and unexpected, the Hattiesburg Pocket Museum is a 90 cm-wide “museum” in a niche between two buildings on Front Street—miniature exhibitions changed monthly (Barbie dolls, Cold War objects, outsider art miniatures). Free, open 24/7. All around, the historic downtown has gained over 70 murals since 2014 through a municipal program that revitalized the former railroad district.

60 min
3

15 km south, Camp Shelby is the largest National Guard training base in the United States, active since 1917—and its military museum is one of the South's largest. An exceptional exhibition on the 442nd Infantry Regiment, the most decorated Japanese American unit in U.S. military history, trained here in 1943 while the soldiers' families were in internment camps. Free admission, ID check at the base entrance.

90 min

Activities

You bike along the Longleaf Trace—a 66 km greenway built on a former railroad line, connecting Hattiesburg to Prentiss through longleaf pine forest. Flat, paved, shaded. Bike rental at Trace Station in Hattiesburg ($15 for a half-day). The urban section can also be explored on foot without renting a bike.

Natchez, MS

Suggested base : Natchez, MS
★★☆☆☆
1

Unmatched in the South, Natchez is perched on a 60-meter bluff above the Mississippi—600 designated historic antebellum properties, more than any other American city. Why here? Before the Civil War, Natchez had more millionaires (in pre-1860 dollars) per capita than any city in the country except New York. The city surrendered without a fight in 1862, which explains why nothing was destroyed. Walk along the bluff at sunset, with views stretching 50 km. Free.

90 min
2

You'll find the South's strangest mansion on the southeast outskirts: Longwood—octagonal, a 27-meter Oriental dome, six stories. Construction began in 1860 under planter Haller Nutt, but the Northern workers (all abolitionists) fled when war was declared in 1861, abandoning tools and materials inside the house. Only the ground floor was finished. The upper five floors have remained unfinished for 165 years—tools, ladders, and wood shavings exactly as in 1861. Guided tours only, $30, budget 75 minutes. Unsettling.

90 min
3

At the opposite extreme, Stanton Hall downtown is the most opulent antebellum mansion still intact—completed in 1857 by merchant Frederick Stanton at a cost of $83,000 ($3 million today). Drawing rooms with six-meter ceilings, Bohemian crystal chandeliers, French mirrors ordered directly from Paris. The furniture is largely original. $25, one-hour guided tour.

75 min
4

Deliberately understated, Forks of the Road, 2 km northeast of downtown, was a slave market—its activity peaked in 1820–1860, with tens of thousands of people sold here. Today it is an empty plot with a few interpretive panels and a simple memorial. The decision to avoid spectacle is explicitly upheld. An essential place to understand the darker side of the antebellum wealth admired elsewhere in town. Free, accessible 24 hours a day.

45 min
5

Rare testimony of a free African American life in the antebellum South, the William Johnson House preserves the home of "the Barber of Natchez"—a freed Black man, who became a property owner and himself an enslaver, murdered in 1851. His journal kept for 16 years (2,000 pages) is one of the few such writings to survive. Federal site managed by the National Park Service, free admission.

60 min

Activities

Sunset nearly mandatory at Magnolia Grill, beneath the bluff at river level—wooden deck facing the Mississippi, river fish (fried catfish), gumbo, crawfish étouffée. $30-40 per person. For something less touristy, Magnolia Sushi & Grill serves surprisingly well-executed Vietnamese-Southern fusion.

120 min

At twilight, walk along Broadway Street atop the bluff—benches, cast-iron lampposts, views down onto barges passing on the Mississippi. The historic “Under the Hill” below (the former district of saloons, prostitution, and brawls during the steamboat golden age, 1830–1860) is quiet today: three bars remain.

Natchez, MS

Suggested base : Natchez, MS
★★☆☆☆
1

Managed by federal rangers, Melrose Estate is the flagship unit of Natchez National Historical Park—and what makes it unique is that the rangers give as much weight to the history of enslaved people as to that of the owners. A 33-hectare estate, an 1845 Greek Revival mansion, and six original slave cabins preserved and studied. The McMurran family owned 25 enslaved people at the estate, 700 across their five Louisiana plantations—the rangers hide nothing. Free guided tour, 1.5 hours, reserve at the Visitor Center.

120 min
2

An engineering masterpiece of the early 19th century, Auburn House's central hall spiral staircase is built without a central support—copied throughout the South afterward. First known example of Greek Revival architecture in Mississippi (1812), located in Duncan Park. Estate donated to the city in 1911, public park open 24/7 around the house. Interior tour $15.

75 min
3

On Homochitto Street, Dunleith Historic Inn is one of the few mansions with 26 Corinthian columns on four sides—visible in "Showboat" (1951) and "Beauty Queen" (2001). Built in 1856 by Charles Dahlgren, brother of the inventor of the Dahlgren cannon used during the Civil War. Today a luxury hotel (rooms $250-400), but interior tours $25 for non-guests, by reservation.

60 min

Activities

You see the city from the river, as in the steamboat era—a paddlewheel boat cruise from Natchez Landing, two hours for $35 (Riverside Magnolia, local operator). The historical commentary during the cruise is honest about the city's economic underside (cotton trade and slavery). In summer, sunset departures.

120 min

Natchez's oldest standing building, opened in 1789: King's Tavern built from recycled steamboat wood, low ceilings, period fireplaces. Also Mississippi's oldest restaurant. Current cuisine: wood-fired pizzas, salads, wines by the glass. Budget $40-50 per person. Reservations recommended.

Alexandria, LA

Suggested base : Alexandria, LA
★★☆☆☆
1

A practical stop rather than a destination, Alexandria was burned in 1864 by Union forces (General Banks) during the Red River Campaign—90% of antebellum buildings went up in smoke. Kent Plantation House remains, 5 km west: a 1796 Creole farmhouse, one of the few 18th-century structures still standing in central Louisiana. Half-timbered construction, cypress-shingle roof, detached kitchen with outdoor oven. Guided tour $10, one hour.

75 min
2

In an early-20th-century historic bank on the Red River, the Alexandria Museum of Art is a respectable regional museum—Louisiana folk art collection, contemporary Southern paintings, rotating exhibitions. $5, budget one hour. Pair with a lunch break if you're passing through at noon.

60 min

Activities

Crawfish étouffée (in season December–June), grilled boudin, and coleslaw—Critic's Choice has been a local institution since 1986, serving straightforward Cajun-Creole food. The dining room gets noisy at lunchtime, especially on Fridays.

Natchitoches, LA

Suggested base : Natchitoches, LA
★★★☆☆
1

Learn the pronunciation before arriving; locals will correct you otherwise: “NACK-uh-tish”. Natchitoches is the oldest city founded by the French in Louisiana—1714, four years before New Orleans. The historic downtown along the Cane River (actually a former bend of the Red River, now a lake) has 33 blocks of Creole, Spanish, and antebellum houses. Paved Front Street runs along the river for 1 km: wrought iron on the balconies, old lampposts, terraces. This is where “Steel Magnolias” was filmed in 1989 (Julia Roberts, Sally Field, Dolly Parton, Shirley MacLaine), and the town still lives off it.

90 min
2

Exact reconstruction of the 1716 French fort on its original site, Fort St. Jean Baptiste stands on the riverbank—cypress-post palisade, barracks, chapel, powder magazine. Costumed demonstrations on weekends, you learn that the garrison never exceeded 30 soldiers and they survived through trade with the Caddo and Natchitoches peoples. $4, Wednesday-Sunday.

75 min
3

What the Cane River Creole National Historical Park preserves is unique in the world—two Creole plantations 15 km south on Cane River Road: Oakland (1818) and Magnolia (1830). The term “Creole” takes its technical meaning here: descendants of French colonists and their enslaved Africans, forming a free, property-owning caste before the Civil War. Magnolia has 21 original outbuildings, including the only intact mule-powered cotton press in the United States. Free admission, parking at both sites, budget three hours.

180 min
4

Marie Thérèse Coincoin (1742–1816), an enslaved woman born in Natchitoches, freed in 1778 by her French partner Claude Thomas Pierre Metoyer and the mother of ten children, became a landowner and slaveholder. The 911-hectare land grant that would become Melrose Plantation was awarded in 1796 to her son Louis Metoyer, who built the Big House there. The Metoyer family, together with other freed people, formed the Isle Brevelle community; they were the country's wealthiest free family of color in the 19th century. Cook Clementine Hunter, born here around 1887, became a major painter of American naïve art in the 1950s without ever learning to read. Guided tour $12, 30 km south.

90 min

Activities

Taste Natchitoches meat pies, the city's official specialty since 1967 (proclaimed by the Louisiana legislature)—fried turnover stuffed with spiced beef and pork, sold at Lasyone's Restaurant on Second Street since 1967. $4 each, $8 with sides. Texture between a samosa and empanada, family recipe never published.

45 min

If you visit between the Saturday before Thanksgiving and January 6, you will encounter the “Christmas Festival of Lights”—300,000 bulbs illuminating the historic downtown, one of the oldest year-end light displays in the United States, begun in 1927. Outside the season, dinner on the terrace at Mama's Oyster House on Front Street facing the river: gumbo, jambalaya, and fried-oyster po-boys, $30–40 per person.

Shreveport, LA

Suggested base : Shreveport, LA
★★★☆☆
1

October 16, 1954, Shreveport Municipal Auditorium: Elvis Presley takes the stage for his first paid concert, age 18, at the venue for “Louisiana Hayride”, a country radio show broadcast nationally every Saturday. He would return 50 times over 18 months. Hank Williams had debuted there in 1948, Johnny Cash in 1955. Art Deco building from 1929, a 3,200-seat hall still in use. Guided tour $10 by appointment, mornings only.

75 min
2

A surprise of the trip and an absolute must-see: the R.W. Norton Art Gallery is the best-kept secret of Texas and Louisiana—a private collection bequeathed to the city in 1966 by an oil tycoon. Forty hectares of botanical gardens, plus a Frederic Remington collection (135 works, the world's largest collection of this artist of the American West) and works by Charles Russell. A rare-book library with 5,000 old volumes. Free admission.

120 min
3

Both folk art and historical testimony, the Louisiana State Exhibit Museum's 26 dioramas, arranged in a semicircle, were built in the 1930s by the WPA under Roosevelt—illustrating Louisiana life (sugarcane harvesting, bayou fishing, a Creole forge), and restored in 2010. On Greenwood Road. Free, Tuesday–Friday.

60 min

Activities

Pierre Avenue, a little wooden shack: Herby-K's Restaurant has been open since 1936 and the house specialty is found nowhere else—the “Shrimp Buster”, shrimp pounded between two sheets of wax paper to half a millimeter thick, then grilled. $22. Brusque but cordial service, as it should be. A line at lunchtime.

90 min

Jefferson, TX

Suggested base : Jefferson, TX
★★★☆☆
1

Population 1,800 today, versus 30,000 around 1870: Jefferson is Texas's largest inhabited ghost town, once the state's second inland port after Galveston. Big Cypress Bayou allowed steamboat access from the Mississippi via the Red River. In 1873, dynamite blew up a natural logjam 300 km downstream—the water level dropped, steamboats could no longer reach Jefferson, and the city's economy died within a few years. What remains: 56 blocks of intact antebellum buildings, designated historic landmarks. Free walking route.

90 min
2

One of Texas's oldest continuously operating hotels, the Excelsior House opened in 1858—Rutherford Hayes, Ulysses Grant, and Oscar Wilde all slept here. Original woodwork, antique furniture, 1860 canopy bed. Interior tour $5 for non-guests (on request at reception), far better than the modern rooms nearby.

45 min
3

An unreal atmosphere, unique in North America: Caddo Lake is Texas's only natural lake of significant size—formed in the early 19th century by a driftwood logjam on the Red River, exactly the same cause as Jefferson's prosperity. 105 km² of bald cypress draped in Spanish moss, a labyrinth of channels and bayous dotted with duckweed. 25 km east. Canoe rental $25 for two hours, or a guided rowboat tour for $45.

150 min

Activities

An 1860 brick building, a restaurant-saloon with live country music on weekends: Auntie Skinner's Riverboat Club is the classic spot. Otherwise, Riverport BBQ for hickory-smoked barbecue (brisket cooked for 14 hours). Budget $25–35 per person.

Tyler, TX

Suggested base : Tyler, TX
★★★☆☆
1

One-fifth of all roses grown commercially in the United States comes from East Texas counties—a legacy of the early 20th century, when local nurserymen replaced peach trees decimated by the freezes of 1907 with rose bushes. Tyler Municipal Rose Garden, on 5.7 hectares, has 35,000 rose bushes and 500 varieties: North America's largest public rose garden. Peak bloom in May and October. Free, open year-round.

90 min
2

Adjacent to the garden, the Tyler Rose Museum explains why this East Texas sandy soil suits roses (acidic soil, mild climate), how seven million cuttings were shipped nationwide each year in the 1950s, and the annual Texas Rose Festival tradition since 1933—the second weekend in October, 100,000 visitors. $3.50, budget 45 minutes.

45 min
3

If you're here in late March or early April, it's the absolute peak: the Tyler Azalea District showcases 16 km of streets lined with azaleas planted in the 1920s by residents themselves. Self-guided route by car or on foot, map available at the Visitor Center. Off-season, the houses remain beautiful but without the spectacle.

60 min

Activities

Locals' favorite burger since 1979, that's Jucy's Hamburgers—not haute cuisine, but the local institution. Otherwise, Rick's on the Square downtown serves elegant Southern cuisine (steak, Gulf fish) in a former 1916 bank whose marble vault has been preserved.

Paris, TX

Suggested base : Paris, TX
★★★☆☆
1

A good-natured battle among the six U.S. cities named Paris: ours, in Texas, has its 20-meter Eiffel Tower with a 1.5-meter red cowboy hat installed at the top in 1998, as a reply to the Eiffel Tower in Paris, Tennessee, which had dared raise its own to 18 meters. Population 25,000, and yes, this is exactly the kind of thing you photograph on a road trip. Free, accessible 24/7, on Jefferson Road.

30 min
2

Built in 1866–1868 by Samuel Bell Maxey, a former Confederate general (trained at West Point) who became a two-term U.S. senator, the Sam Bell Maxey House is one of the few fully preserved 19th-century Texas homes. High Victorian Italianate style: tall windows with cornices, a portico, and a rooftop belvedere. The family lived there for 99 years, until donating it to the city in 1966. Over 10,000 original objects preserved. Tours $8, Wednesday–Saturday.

60 min
3

The South's most unexpected funerary sculpture: Willet Babcock's grave at Evergreen Cemetery (founded in 1866) is topped by a 4.6-meter monument depicting a Christ-like figure… wearing cowboy boots. Babcock, originally from Ithaca, New York, a former Union lieutenant during the Civil War, owned a furniture and coffin factory in Paris whose ground floor also served as a 1,200-seat opera house. He commissioned his monument from German sculptor Gustave Klein in 1880, eight years before his death. Nicknamed “Jesus in Cowboy Boots”, it's now Texas's most photographed tomb—some question the identification: no beard, a feminine face, and the figure leans on the cross instead of carrying it. Perhaps a mourning angel. Free, sunrise to sunset.

30 min

Activities

A house cheeseburger at Jaxx Gourmet Burgers, or barbecue at Rib Crib, on the Downtown Square (the town's central square, as in all Texas towns). The square itself merits a stroll: Confederate monuments, a fountain, and restored commercial buildings from 1900.

Dallas, TX

Suggested base : Dallas, TX
★★★★☆
1

12:30 PM, November 22, 1963: John F. Kennedy is assassinated here, at Dealey Plaza. A triangle of concrete and grass in the heart of downtown, completely preserved in its 1963 configuration—you see the exact shooting angle, the “grassy knoll” that feeds conspiracy theories, and the crosses painted on Elm Street where the bullets struck. Philip Johnson's JFK Memorial (1970), 200 meters away, is deliberately minimalist: an open nine-meter cube, empty, abstract. Free.

60 min
2

Located on the sixth floor of the Texas School Book Depository, the very floor from which Lee Harvey Oswald fired, the Sixth Floor Museum has preserved intact the corner window from which the shots were fired, with the boxes of books behind it. An honest account of the assassination, the Warren Commission investigation, and conspiracy theories, presented with factual rigor. $18, audio guide included, budget two hours. Avoid weekends: long lines.

120 min
3

One of America's 10 largest museums is in the Arts District: 24,000 works, from the Neolithic period to contemporary art. The Dallas Museum of Art's centerpiece? The Wendy and Emery Reves Collection, furniture and artworks from Villa La Pausa (Coco Chanel's former house in Roquebrune), with its interiors entirely reconstructed. Free admission to the permanent collection.

120 min
4

Picture this: two hectares of lawn, food trucks, pétanque, mini-golf, free concerts—all built in 2012 over a freeway, the Woodall Rodgers Freeway, which runs in a tunnel underneath. Klyde Warren Park is one of America's finest examples of a “deck park”, the city's urban heart. Free.

60 min
5

East of downtown, Deep Ellum has a distinctive history: initially an African American neighborhood in the 1920s where Blind Lemon Jefferson, Robert Johnson, and T-Bone Walker created “Texas blues”. Today, 40 bars with live music, murals every 50 meters (Deep Ellum Mural Project), and restaurants. At night, the atmosphere changes completely: from calm to buzzing within hours.
