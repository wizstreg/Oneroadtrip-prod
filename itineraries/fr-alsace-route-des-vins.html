Strasbourg

★★★★☆
1

142 meters of pink sandstone, a single spire, and the second one missing for six centuries—lack of funding. Strasbourg Cathedral was the world's tallest building from 1647 to 1874, dethroned only by Hamburg's St. Nicholas Church (147 m). Gothic construction began in 1220 on the ruins of an Ottonian cathedral from 1015, dragging on until the spire's completion in 1439. Inside, the 16th-century astronomical clock triggers a parade of figures at noon sharp—arrive twenty minutes early, seating goes fast.

⏱ 90 min
2

Tanners, fishermen, millers—La Petite France, UNESCO-listed with the historic center since 1988, was Strasbourg's working-class district in the Middle Ages. You can still feel it in the architecture: houses on the water, 14th-century covered bridges, functioning locks. This isn't a reconstructed set; people actually lived here. Avoid Rue du Bain-aux-Plantes between 2 and 4 PM in July—tour buses block the entire passage.

⏱ 60 min
3

Three 17th-century patrician houses, interconnected, telling the story of everyday Alsatian life in the 18th and 19th centuries. The detail that sticks: the four-poster beds indebted peasants would display to neighbors during festivals—without ever sleeping in them. Costumes, reconstructed kitchens, farm tools. More immersive than you'd expect from a folk arts museum.

⏱ 75 min

Carved beneath the Civil Hospital, the Historic Cellar of the Strasbourg Hospices has been operating since 1395—it's Alsace's oldest working cellar. It preserves a barrel from 1472 that's never been emptied. Riesling and Gewurztraminer tastings at the end of the visit, with a guide who knows each cask by name. Book ahead in season.

Strasbourg

★★★★☆
1

Urbanist paradox: Strasbourg's most 'Prussian' district is today one of France's finest neoclassical extensions. Neustadt was forcibly built between 1870 and 1918 under German administration—imperial palace, university, grand boulevards—and UNESCO-listed in 2017, thirty years after the old town. Less crowded than La Petite France, and far more pleasant to explore on foot without the masses.

⏱ 45 min
2

Three museums in a single palace—archaeology, fine arts, decorative arts—and royal apartments built to receive Louis XV during his visits. Marie-Antoinette stayed here in 1770 at age 14, en route to Versailles to marry the future Louis XVI. You often enter without queuing, unlike the cathedral a hundred meters away. The decorative arts wing, with its 18th-century Strasbourg faience, is the most overlooked—and the most beautiful.

⏱ 90 min

Activities

Seventy minutes on the Ill, beneath the bridges of La Petite France and past the Palais Rohan façade. Less romantic than it sounds—you pass under low arches, glide along private gardens—but far more useful than a paper map for understanding the city's geography.

Obernai

★★★☆☆
1

The village is unremarkable. But this is where the counter starts: Marlenheim is the official starting point of the Wine Route since 1953. 170 km of vineyards, 67 communes, 51 grands crus—it all begins with this sign. Take five minutes for the photo. Alsatians care about it.

⏱ 20 min
2

We stop in Molsheim not for the wine, but for the Metzig: a former 16th-century butchers' guildhall, Renaissance loggia, belfry. And for Bugatti—Ettore set up here in 1909; the first Type 10 rolled out of his workshop on Rue de la Dohle. The factory no longer exists, but the local museum keeps archives and a few period mechanical parts.

⏱ 40 min
3

Place du Beffroi, Obernai checks all the boxes: a 16th-century six-bucket well, Gothic Chapelles Tower, half-timbered town hall, Sainte-Odile fountain. It's the birthplace of the patron saint of Alsace, which explains the pilgrimage that's been running for twelve centuries—and the abbey 12 km away, up the mountain.

⏱ 50 min
4

At 764 meters, on clear days you can see all the way to the Black Forest. Mont Sainte-Odile Abbey attracts 1.5 million visitors annually—founded in the 7th century, rebuilt multiple times, it still operates as monastic lodging. The pagan wall surrounding it, 10 km long and 1.7 m high, remains unexplained by archaeologists—Celtic? Roman? Merovingian? The debate continues.

⏱ 90 min

Ribeauville

★★★☆☆
1

Twelve meters of Romanesque portal, fantastical animals, human figures, interlacing—Andlau Abbey Church's portal displays a sculptural density rarely seen in Alsace. The church dates back to the 9th century; the crypt houses the tomb of Saint Richardis, wife of Charles the Fat. Andlau is also one of the rare villages to concentrate three grands crus on its territory alone: Kastelberg, Moenchberg, Wiebelsberg.

⏱ 45 min
2

Three medieval gates still standing, ramparts nearly complete—Dambach-la-Ville is one of Alsace's best-preserved fortified towns. Its grand cru Frankstein grows on pink and beige granite that geologists come to study as much as sommeliers. Less frequented village than its neighbors: take advantage.

⏱ 50 min
3

600,000 visitors a year—which gives you an idea of the July queue. Perched at 755 meters since the 12th century, Haut-Koenigsbourg burned in 1633 during the Thirty Years' War, remained in ruins for two and a half centuries, then Wilhelm II had it restored from 1900 to 1908. Result: not entirely medieval, but striking. The view over the Alsatian plain—and on clear days to the Alps—is worth admission. Arrive at opening (9 AM) or after 4 PM.

⏱ 120 min
4

It's here, in this library, that the American continent received its name for the first time. Waldseemüller's Cosmographiae Introductio, published in Saint-Dié in 1507, is preserved here—with the first printed mention of the word 'America.' Beatus Rhenanus's personal library, intact since 1547, was UNESCO-listed in 2011. Paid admission, closed Mondays.

⏱ 60 min

Kaysersberg

★★★★☆
1

Three ruined castles dominate Ribeauvillé from the hillside—Saint-Ulrich, Girsberg, Haut-Ribeaupierre—accessible on foot in 45 minutes from the center. Below, three grands crus (Kirchberg, Osterberg, Geisberg) and a main street where grocery stores and residents still coexist with wine shops. It's not frozen like a museum—and that's its best asset.

⏱ 60 min
2

Touristy, yes. But the 16th-century ramparts are intact, houses date from the 16th to 18th centuries, and the Château des Wurtemberg (1540) houses an unexpected postal museum—medieval communication history, postcards, telegraphs. In July, crowds are dense in the afternoon. Off-season, in November, Riquewihr recovers a silence that hasn't existed for a long time.

⏱ 70 min
3

Just a few streets, but a 15th-century fortified church that stops you cold: cemetery enclosed by a hexagonal wall with bastions, bell tower among the tombs. Since the 16th century, Catholics and Protestants have shared the building on a programmed rotation—each in their corner, at fixed times. The arrangement still holds today.

⏱ 40 min
4

Albert Schweitzer was born here in 1875—his birthplace has become a museum. The medieval ruined castle can be freely visited above the village, with views over glazed tile roofs and the Vosges. The 15th-century fortified bridge spans the Weiss at the town's heart: one of the rare fortified covered bridges still standing in Alsace.

⏱ 75 min

Activities

Estate founded in 1626, eleven generations, one of Alsace's most exported Trimbach houses. Their Riesling Clos Sainte-Hune—a single one-hectare parcel in the Rosacker grand cru—regularly ranks among the world's finest dry Rieslings. Shop open without appointment on weekdays. Limited quantities on the Sainte-Hune.

⏱ 60 min

Since 1976, over 200 white storks are born annually at the Hunawihr center before being released in Alsace and beyond. Free-flight shows twice daily, otter pool, nursery accessible during nesting season (April-August). Far more instructive than storks perched on village chimneys—and less passive.

⏱ 75 min

4 km loop through the Schoenenbourg and Sporen grands crus from Riquewihr, with panels on grape varieties and soils. Flat, accessible to all. In September during harvest, you can smell the must 200 meters from open vats—it's the best time to do this trail.

Colmar

★★★☆☆
1

Nine panels, 2.30 meters high, one of the most violent crucifixions in Western art: tortured bodies, garish colors, expressionism before its time. Grünewald painted the Isenheim Altarpiece between 1512 and 1516 for patients in a hospital treating St. Anthony's fire—the sick recognized themselves in Christ's wounded flesh. Take time with the altarpiece alone before the rest of the collections. Closed Tuesdays.

⏱ 90 min
2

The façade dates from 1537: double-story carved wooden gallery, painted medallions, corner turret. The Pfister House belonged for centuries to bourgeois hatters. Today it's a wine shop on the ground floor; the façade is photographed from Rue des Marchands—morning or evening light, not midday.

⏱ 30 min
3

Tanners' and market gardeners' quarter on the Lauch—16th-18th century façades directly on the canal, some houses still have their water-level doors. The tanners' quarter, with its houses featuring open wooden drying lofts across the entire façade, is less known than Little Venice and more authentic. Do it on foot, early morning before the group influx.

⏱ 60 min
4

Circular layout unique in Alsace: Eguisheim's streets coil concentrically around the 8th-century comital castle. The village was voted France's favorite in 2013—and it's deserved, but expect crowds in July. Often-forgotten detail: it's the birthplace of Pope Leo IX, born here in 1002.

⏱ 60 min
5

Every evening from April to October, at 10 PM, a costumed medieval town crier makes his rounds through Turckheim's streets announcing the hour. The tradition has lasted for centuries—the scene is freely accessible, 45 minutes, departure from the belfry. The 14th-century ramparts with their three monumental gates are among the region's most intact. No reason not to sleep in Colmar and come for the evening.

⏱ 50 min

Thirty minutes on the Lauch from Quai de la Poissonnerie—you pass under low bridges, glide along private gardens, graze façades two meters away. Less romantic than Venice, more honest. Operates April to October.

⏱ 30 min

Two opposing philosophies, ten minutes' drive from each other. Léon Beyer in Eguisheim has produced dry, straight wines since 1580, tailored for the table. Mulhouse

★★★☆☆
1

Less known than its neighbors, Rouffach is all the more pleasant: Place de la République, Gothic collegiate church of Notre-Dame (11th-16th century), remains of the Hohenstaufen imperial palace. A stork nests almost every year on the bell tower—one of the first observed in Alsace according to 12th-century chronicles. Twenty minutes is enough, but it's twenty minutes without crowds.

⏱ 40 min
2

Guebwiller marks the entrance to Florival, the valley of flowers. Its 13th-century Dominican abbey church has been converted into a concert hall—exceptional acoustics, serious classical programming. Nearby, the Romanesque abbey church of Saint-Léger (12th century) features a Rhenish-style portal that specialists compare to Andlau's.

⏱ 45 min
3

Less known than Strasbourg Cathedral, and yet: the western portal of Thann's Collégiale Saint-Thiébaut (14th-16th century) extends 15 meters high with three carved voussoirs, original 15th-century stained glass still in place. It's the masterpiece of Alsatian Gothic—and there's nobody in front of it.

⏱ 60 min
4

150 locomotives across 13,000 m²—the area of two covered football pitches, intact. The 1852 Crampton No. 80 is the oldest preserved French locomotive. Cabins are accessible, platform sets reconstructed, travel accounts displayed. Surprisingly captivating even for non-enthusiasts. Allow 2 hours minimum.

⏱ 120 min
5

450 vehicles, including 123 Bugattis—the world's largest Bugatti collection, secretly assembled over twenty years by the Schlumpf brothers, Alsatian textile industrialists. In 1976, their striking workers discovered the warehouse and forcibly opened it to the public. The collection's history is almost as interesting as the cars themselves.

⏱ 120 min

Thann's Rangen has a 45° slope on black volcanic soil—winemakers carry down the harvest by hand in baskets, as in the 13th century. The wine trail crosses the vineyard for 2 km from Thann, with views over the rooftops below. Steep slope, hiking shoes essential. Free, marked from the center.

⏱ 90 min

Last stop before the industrial plain: might as well mark it. Auberge du Vieux Pressoir in Guebwiller serves Rhine frogs, aged Munster, Sundgau pond duck. Alsatian cuisine without tourist choucroute. Weekend reservations recommended, menus from €35 at lunch.
