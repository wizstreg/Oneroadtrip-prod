✨ Highlights
  • Teotihuacán: the Pirámide del Sol, 65 m tall, and the Calzada de los Muertos, approximately 4 km long
  • Real del Monte: the Mina de Acosta and its 400 m of tunnels, and the English cemetery facing England
  • Huasca de Ocampo: Mexico’s first Pueblo Mágico in 2001 and the basalt columns of the Prismas
  • Tlaxcala: more than 450 m² of murals at the Palacio de Gobierno
  • Puebla: a historic center inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 1987, the Capilla del Rosario, and the Biblioteca Palafoxiana
  • Cholula: the Gran Pirámide and its archaeological tunnels, which remain closed to the public according to INAH
☀️ Weather

Travel between November and April, during the dry season: in Puebla, afternoon temperatures reach 23 to 28 °C, but nighttime temperatures drop to around 5 °C in January. Avoid the rainy season if possible, from mid-May to mid-October. In Real del Monte, at an elevation of 2,712 m, evenings are cooler than in Puebla: bring a sweater.

📋 Notes

Popocatépetl is at Yellow Alert, Phase 2, in November 2026, with a 12 km exclusion zone around the crater: you will admire it from a distance, from Atlixco and Cholula. Drive during daylight hours on toll highways and stay away from suburban municipalities that France Diplomatie advises against visiting, such as Ecatepec, Nezahualcóyotl, and Naucalpan.

⚠️ SAFETY NOTE: While major tourist destinations (Riviera Maya, Puerto Vallarta, etc.) are generally safe with good security, some regions have elevated crime risks due to organized crime. Avoid border areas with high violence rates and check government travel advisories for current regional conditions. Stick to tourist zones and use reputable transportation.

Teotihuacán Mex

🛏️ Suggested base : Real del Monte Hid · 53 km
★★★★★
1

At least four tonnes: that is how much each serpent head projecting from the facade of the Templo de la Serpiente Emplumada, the Temple of the Feathered Serpent, in Teotihuacán’s Ciudadela weighs. These heads, surrounded by feathered collars, alternate with geometric figures with round eyes. The temple dates to around 150 AD. The Ciudadela, a square enclosure measuring 400 m on each side, was not a fortress, despite its Spanish name meaning “citadel.” Arrive when it opens at 8 a.m., before the heat sets in.

⏱ 75 min
2

Nothing prepares you for the sheer bulk of the Pirámide del Sol: 65 m tall, with a base measuring approximately 225 m on each side. The city had been abandoned for centuries when the Aztecs gave it the name Teotihuacán, often translated as “the place where the gods gathered.” Climbing is not permitted: walk around the base instead to appreciate its size. Bring a hat and water, as shade is scarce at the site.

⏱ 50 min
3

Tools, cooking pottery, religious objects: the Museo de la Cultura Teotihuacana displays more than 600 pieces in eight galleries. They illustrate life in a city whose estimated population ranged from 85,000 to more than 150,000. Its heyday was around 250 to 550 AD. Plan to visit this museum in the middle of the day: its galleries offer a break from the sun.

⏱ 60 min
4

If you enjoy painting, set aside 1 hour for the Museo de Murales Teotihuacanos Beatriz de la Fuente. Its nine galleries bring together murals discovered over a century of excavations, along with objects made of stone, ceramic, and obsidian, a black volcanic glass. The museum follows the site’s opening hours, from 8 a.m. to 5 p.m. General admission costs 210 pesos; on Sundays, admission is free for Mexican citizens and residents with proof of status.

⏱ 60 min

Zacatlán Guided Tour walking through the Center

To the north, it leads to the 46 m Pyramid of the Moon. Following the armed attack on April 20, 2026, access to this pyramid was prohibited: admire it from the avenue and ask at the entrance whether the rule has changed. Allow a good hour for the walk.

Real del Monte Hid

🛏️ Suggested base : Real del Monte Hid
★★★☆☆
1

Four hundred meters of mine passage dug into the mountain: that is the route through the Mina de Acosta in Real del Monte, whose official name is Mineral del Monte. This silver mine operated from 1727 to 1985. It is now a museum, where you walk through the tunnel in the miners’ footsteps. Bring a sweater: the town sits at an elevation of 2,712 m. Ask locally about tour times.

⏱ 90 min
2

The most surprising place in Real del Monte is a cemetery: the Panteón Inglés, the English Cemetery, which opened in 1851. Its 755 graves are arranged in rows facing England, the homeland of the miners who came to work here. Only one grave is an exception, and tradition attributes it to the English clown Richard Bell. Walk slowly along the paths: at this elevation, even the slightest climb leaves you breathless.

⏱ 40 min
3

The south tower of the Parroquia de Nuestra Señora del Rosario has a clock added in 1842 thanks to donations from miners. At the time, many of these workers came from England: the first English technicians had arrived in Mexico in 1824. Most came from Cornwall, a mining region in southwestern England. Begin your visit to downtown Real del Monte in the church square, then stroll through the neighboring streets.

⏱ 25 min
4

If you are interested in the miners’ history, step inside the Museo de Medicina Laboral, the museum of occupational medicine. It is housed in the Centro Cultural Nicolás Zavala. The town had its own miners’ hospital as early as 1907, funded jointly by the workers and the Compañía Real del Monte y Pachuca, the company that operated the mines. Allow 40 minutes and visit it on the same day as the Mina de Acosta.

⏱ 40 min
5

In Real del Monte, the local specialty is called a “paste”: it is the Mexican cousin of the pasty, the savory turnover eaten by Cornish miners. The Museo del Paste, which opened in 2012, explains how this potato and ground meat turnover arrived with the English miners. Those same miners also brought soccer to the region. Visit the museum in the late morning, just before heading out to try a paste.

⏱ 40 min

Huasca de Ocampo Hid

🛏️ Suggested base : Real del Monte Hid · 12 km
★★☆☆☆
1

It is hard to believe that nature alone carved the Prismas Basálticos de Santa María Regla, near Huasca de Ocampo. These basalt columns, often with five or six sides, reach heights of 30 to 40 m. They formed as lava flows slowly cooled around 2.5 million years ago: as the rock cooled, it cracked into regular prisms. The German naturalist Alexander von Humboldt drew them in 1803. Admission costs 100 pesos and includes the footbridge, overlooks, and access to the waterfalls.

⏱ 120 min
2

To see where silver was separated from the ore extracted from the mines, visit Hacienda Santa María Regla, next to the prisms. Pedro Romero de Terreros, the wealthy operator of the Real del Monte mines, had it built around 1760 beside the river because flat ground was needed, and none could be found on the slopes of Real del Monte. You can see courtyards made of volcanic stone, a small chapel, tunnels, and La Rosa waterfall. Tours run daily from 8 a.m. to 6 p.m.

⏱ 45 min
3

Many visitors believe it is dedicated to Saint Michael the Archangel, but the Parroquia de San Juan Bautista de Huasca de Ocampo is dedicated to Saint John the Baptist. Augustinian monks from Atotonilco el Grande founded it in the 16th century. In 2001, Huasca became Mexico’s first “Pueblo Mágico,” a national designation recognizing towns with remarkable heritage. Stop by the church late in the day, on your way back from the basalt prisms.

⏱ 20 min
4

Drive about twenty minutes from the entrance to Huasca to reach Peña del Aire, a viewpoint on the edge of a very deep canyon. The view extends toward the Barranca de Metztitlán Biosphere Reserve. The more adventurous cross the void on a bicycle suspended from a steel cable or on a zip line. The site opens daily at 9 a.m. On Hidalgo’s back roads, residents sometimes set up roadblocks to ask for a passage fee: France Diplomatie advises paying, as the amounts are small.

⏱ 50 min

Activities

Turquoise water, rowboats, and the smell of grilling: Bosque de las Truchas, in San Miguel Regla, is a recreation park surrounding a trout lake. You can fish with a rod rented on-site, even if you have no experience. Admission ranges from 30 to 299 pesos depending on the activities you choose, such as a rowboat ride or zip-lining. Grill your catch under the palapas, open-sided shelters set up for cookouts.

⏱ 45 min

Huasca de Ocampo even has a museum dedicated to duendes, the elves of Mexican folktales: the Museo de los Duendes. A visit takes about 45 minutes and costs 40 pesos per person. It is a fun stop with children, to fit in between the basalt prisms and the village center.

Cacaxtla Tla

🛏️ Suggested base : Tlaxcala Tla · 13 km
★★★★☆
1

Nearly 26 m long: that is the size of the Mural de la Batalla, Cacaxtla’s great mural. It depicts warriors dressed in jaguar skins, the victors, facing defeated warriors wearing bird headdresses and jade jewelry. It was painted around 650–700 by the Olmec-Xicalanca, a people whose city flourished between 650 and 900. An enormous roof protects it from sun and rain. The site is open from 9 a.m. to 5 p.m., with last admission at 4 p.m.; foreign visitors pay 210 pesos, and flash photography is prohibited.

⏱ 100 min
2

Next, head to Xochitécatl, 5 km away, an older religious center active mainly between 600 and 100 BC. Its largest structure is the Pirámide de las Flores, where archaeologists found around thirty burials, mostly of children, accompanied by offerings. The site is also known for its female figurines, linked to fertility and rain rituals. Bring a hat and water: you will be walking in full sun.

Tlaxcala Tla

🛏️ Suggested base : Tlaxcala Tla
★★★☆☆
1

The city’s best history lesson is painted on the walls: the murals in the Palacio de Gobierno de Tlaxcala cover more than 450 m². Painter Desiderio Hernández Xochitiotzin began them in 1957 with depictions of Tlaxcala before the Spanish arrived. He continued with the Conquest, then the following centuries, until his health forced him to stop in 2001. The palace houses state government offices, so enter quietly.

⏱ 60 min
2

Orange walls and cobalt-blue talavera tiles: the Parroquia de San José brings color to central Tlaxcala. Talavera is hand-painted earthenware typical of the Puebla region. An earthquake destroyed the church’s dome and roof in 1864; those you see today date from the reconstruction. The Plaza de la Constitución, the central square laid out in 1524, measures 75 m on each side. Start your visit here: the entire center is walkable.

⏱ 40 min
3

Tlaxcala’s monastery, built between 1537 and 1542, is among the first four Franciscan monasteries in the Americas. Its church, now the Catedral de Nuestra Señora de la Asunción, retains its original roof structure of massive wooden beams. Its nave offers a rare example of Mudéjar art in the Americas, a style inherited from Spain’s Muslim artisans and characterized by geometric patterns. The cloister has housed the Museo Regional de Tlaxcala since 1981: allow 30 minutes for its collections.

⏱ 70 min
4

From the outside, the Basílica de Ocotlán stands out for its earthenware tiles and red brick, typical of Tlaxcala’s Baroque architecture. Inside, the statue of the Virgin occupies a Baroque room covered in sculpted stucco, with eight twisted columns. Francisco Miguel Tlayoltehuanitzin created this decoration between 1715 and 1740. This is a pilgrimage site, so keep a low profile during Mass.

⏱ 45 min
5

If you have 1 hour left, step into the Museo de Arte de Tlaxcala, which opened in 2004. The 19th-century building was first a hospital, then a prison, before becoming a museum. Six rooms display the permanent collection, and five host temporary exhibitions. Ask at the front desk which exhibition is currently on view.

⏱ 45 min
6

Finish in Tizatlán at a 16th-century open chapel, meaning an outward-facing chapel from which Mass was celebrated before a crowd gathered outdoors. It was built on the platform of a pyramid that formed part of the palace of Xicohténcatl the Elder, a Tlaxcalan leader. Fragments of murals still depict the baptism of Christ and the Three Wise Men. Tizatlán is outside the historic center, so plan to drive.

Puebla Pue

🛏️ Suggested base : Puebla Pue
★★★★☆
1

Seventy meters: that is the height of the Catedral de Puebla’s two towers, the tallest in Spain’s American colonies at the time. Bishop Juan de Palafox y Mendoza consecrated the cathedral on April 18, 1649, before it was even finished. Legend has it that angels hoisted its great bell, María, which weighs more than 8 tonnes. In reality, Juan Bautista Santiago, an Indigenous master builder, raised it in 1732. Dress appropriately: this is an active church.

⏱ 50 min
2

Gold all the way up to the dome: the Capilla del Rosario, in the church of Santo Domingo, is covered in gilded stucco. This is a sculpted plaster coating covered with 24-karat gold leaf. The chapel was consecrated in 1690, when the friar Diego de Gorozpe dubbed it “the eighth wonder of the world.” Talavera tiles cover the lower walls. It is located at 407 Avenida 5 de Mayo; visit outside service times.

⏱ 40 min
3

Poblanos, the residents of Puebla, named the Casa del Alfeñique after a sweet. Alfeñique is a sugar-and-almond paste, and the house’s facade is covered with mortar ornaments so delicate that they resemble this decorative sugar work. The house was built for Juan Ignacio Morales, a master ironworker. It later became home to the city’s first public museum, which displays more than 1,500 historical objects. Allow 45 minutes.

Puebla Pue

🛏️ Suggested base : Puebla Pue
★★★★☆
1

It is Puebla’s most impressive room: the Biblioteca Palafoxiana, a vaulted gallery 43 m long, completed in 1773. It all began in 1646, when Bishop Juan de Palafox y Mendoza donated 5,000 books to a college on the condition that everyone could read them. UNESCO recognizes it as the first public library in the Americas. Admission costs 48 pesos and is free on Sundays; the library opens at 10 a.m., Tuesday through Sunday.

⏱ 45 min
2

If you could visit only one museum in Puebla, choose the Museo Amparo. It occupies two colonial mansions from the 17th and 18th centuries and has fourteen exhibition rooms. The displays take you from objects made before the Spanish arrived to colonial-era furniture, then contemporary art. Allow at least 1 hour 30 minutes so you do not have to rush.

⏱ 90 min
3

Step inside the Uriarte workshop, which has been making talavera, Puebla’s painted earthenware, since 1824. This earthenware takes its name from Talavera de la Reina, the Spanish town that gave this pottery its style. Everything is handmade, and a single piece can take up to three months of work. Each object bears a serial number, and most are signed by the artisan. Ask at the front desk whether a workshop tour is scheduled that day.

⏱ 60 min
4

On May 5, 1862, around the Fuertes de Loreto y Guadalupe, General Ignacio Zaragoza’s Mexican army defeated the French troops led by the Count of Lorencez. This was the Battle of Puebla. At Fort Loreto, the Museo de la No Intervención occupies the former chapel, among other spaces, and displays cannons, sabers, and documents from the battle. The forts are in the Centro Cívico 5 de Mayo, outside the historic center: take a taxi.

⏱ 75 min

Activities

Puebla residents say mole poblano originated in a convent. According to the story, the nuns of Santa Rosa, anxious about an archbishop’s upcoming visit, mixed chili peppers, spices, and chocolate, then poured the sauce over an old turkey. Other accounts trace it back to the Aztecs, but there is no evidence that they added chocolate. Try mole at lunch, then save room for a cemita, a large sandwich piled with fillings and served on a round sesame seed roll.

Atlixco Pue

🛏️ Suggested base : Cholula Pue · 22 km
★★★☆☆
1

On a clear day, you can see the Popocatépetl volcano from Cerro de San Miguel, the hill in Atlixco. In November 2026, it is under a Yellow Alert, Phase 2, the intermediate level of Mexico’s volcanic alert system: authorities ask people to stay at least 12 km from the crater. It can therefore be admired from a distance. The climb begins at the Escalera Ancha, a broad staircase, and leads to the Capilla de San Miguel Arcángel, a 16th-century chapel. Admission is free; come early, as the morning mist lifts.

⏱ 60 min
2

Atlixco’s Palacio Municipal houses murals by artist Juan Manuel Martínez Caltenco. The city has been designated a Pueblo Mágico since 2015. Before leaving, also take a look at the facade of the Capilla de la Tercera Orden, one of the city’s chapels.

⏱ 30 min

“Atlixco de las Flores” is the city’s nickname, and the viveros, or plant nurseries, in the Cabrera neighborhood explain why. Hundreds of growers cultivate plants and fruit trees here, in greenhouses and open fields. Santa María Tonantzintla Pue

🛏️ Suggested base : Cholula Pue · 4 km
★★☆☆☆
1

Not an inch is left undecorated: inside the church of Santa María Tonantzintla, stucco and paintings cover the walls, vaults, and dome. You can see cherubs, children adorned with feathers, angels with Indigenous faces, and tropical fruits such as coconuts, chili peppers, and mangoes. The name, in Nahuatl, the language of the Aztecs, roughly means “place of our little mother.” This is the village church: enter quietly.

⏱ 30 min
2

Perhaps the most colorful facade in the area belongs to the church of San Francisco Acatepec. It is covered in blue, yellow, black, green, orange, and purple talavera tiles interspersed with red brick. All this ceramic work was handmade. The exact construction date is unknown, but a painting in the church is signed and dated 1730. The two villages are neighbors: visit the two churches one after the other.

Cholula Pue

🛏️ Suggested base : Cholula Pue
★★★★☆
1

A wooded hill crowned with a church? It is actually the Gran Pirámide de Cholula, whose Nahuatl name, Tlachihualtepetl, means “built mountain.” Its base measures about 300 m by 315 m. According to Guinness World Records, it is the world’s largest pyramid by volume, at more than 4.45 million m³, compared with about 2.5 million for the Great Pyramid of Giza. Start at the Patio de los Altares, the former main entrance.

⏱ 70 min
2

Cholula’s finest moment awaits at the top of the pyramid, in front of the Santuario de Nuestra Señora de los Remedios. The Spanish built this church in 1594, above the ancient temple. Lightning has struck it several times, and in the early colonial period, people saw this as the wrath of the ancient rain goddess. Climb up in the late afternoon: in clear weather, Popocatépetl stands out to the west.

⏱ 45 min
3

The Convento de San Gabriel was built on the site of a temple dedicated to Quetzalcóatl, the feathered serpent god. Indigenous workers built it entirely under the direction of the friar Toribio de Alcaraz. On the main square, the parish church of San Pedro has a dome in the Churrigueresque style, a highly ornate Spanish Baroque style, pierced by eight windows. End the day in this square before having dinner in town.

⏱ 55 min

Activities

Explore the Cholula pyramid from the outside: according to INAH, its tunnels remain closed to the public until further notice. Beginning in 1931, archaeologists dug about 8 km of passageways to explore the structure’s interior. Do not plan to go down into these passages: the reopening announced in March 2026 has not been confirmed by the National Institute of Anthropology and History.
