Marseille

★★★★★
1

Start with La Bonne Mère—that's what locals call Notre-Dame-de-la-Garde, and it's the first thing you should do upon arrival. Perched at 154 meters atop a limestone outcrop, this Romano-Byzantine basilica consecrated in 1864 dominates everything. The gilded statue of the Virgin at the summit measures 11.20 meters and weighs nearly 10 tons—it was crafted by the Christofle house and requires regilding every 25 years. Inside, the walls are covered with 1,200 m² of gold-backed mosaics and sailors' ex-votos: suspended ship models, thank-you plaques, and naïve paintings. Go up in the morning for the light and avoid weekends.

⏱ 75 min
2

Head back down to the Old Port and climb up into Le Panier district, Marseille's oldest neighborhood—this is where Greek settlers from Phocaea landed 2,600 years ago. Today, narrow alleys are covered in street art, hidden squares shelter artists' workshops and improvised terraces. Don't look for a precise route: get lost, that's the whole point. Place des Moulins, the district's highest point, makes a good landmark if you lose your bearings.

⏱ 60 min
3

Leaving Le Panier toward the sea, you'll encounter La Major cathedral. Built between 1852 and 1893 in neo-Byzantine style, it's massive—142 meters long, the largest religious building constructed in France during the 19th century. The contrast between white Carrara marble and green Florence stone gives it a striking striped appearance. Glance inside at the choir mosaics, then exit on the sea side for views over the port of La Joliette.

⏱ 40 min
4

End the day with a stroll along the Old Port. It's been Marseille's beating heart for 26 centuries. In the morning, the fish market on quai des Belges is a spectacle—fishermen sell their catch directly from boats, shouting in Provençal. By late afternoon, the terraces take over. The Vieux-Port ombrière, Norman Foster's immense polished steel structure, acts as a mirror reflecting the crowds—it's become one of modern Marseille's symbols.

⏱ 45 min

Activities

Walk up to Notre-Dame-de-la-Garde on foot from the Old Port via Saint-Victor Abbey—one of France's oldest, founded in the 5th century, with crypts and early Christian sarcophagi. Allow 30 minutes uphill. Alternatively, bus 60 departs from the Old Port and drops you at the basilica's foot.

⏱ 45 min

Late afternoon, walk along Corniche Kennedy heading south. It's a 25-minute walk from the Old Port to reach Vallon des Auffes, a tiny fishing harbor wedged between two concrete cliffs. This is postcard Marseille, with colorful pointus (traditional boats) and little bouillabaisse restaurants. The spot is perfect for an aperitif facing the sunset.

Marseille

★★★★★
1

Devote the morning to MuCEM—this concrete cube wrapped in an openwork lattice facing the sea was designed by Rudy Ricciotti. Opened in 2013 when Marseille was European Capital of Culture, it entered the world's 50 most visited museums the following year. Collections cover anthropology, archaeology, and contemporary art around the Mediterranean. Even if exhibitions don't appeal, head to the rooftop terrace: the view over the Old Port and the open sea is free and spectacular.

⏱ 90 min
2

Cross the 130-meter footbridge linking MuCEM to Fort Saint-Jean—suspended above the harbor basin, it offers a vertiginous view of the sea. The fort, built on Louis XIV's orders from 1668 onward, dominates the Old Port entrance from its 20-meter height. It served as much to protect Marseille as to watch over it—cannons were aimed at the city in case of revolt. Free entry, it houses the Garden of Migrations, a 12,000 m² Mediterranean botanical space with lavender, rosemary, and orange trees. Look for the Fanal tower (17th century) that served as a lighthouse and King René's tower (15th century), whose terrace offers plunging views over the port.

⏱ 75 min
3

Return landward via the second footbridge leading directly into Le Panier district. Take rue du Petit-Puits to La Vieille Charité, a former 17th-century hospice built by Pierre Puget around an arcaded courtyard and oval chapel. Today, the building houses the Museum of Mediterranean Archaeology and the Museum of African, Oceanic, and American Indian Arts. Even without entering the museums, the courtyard is magnificent and free.

⏱ 60 min

Activities

If you enjoy contemporary art and alternative spaces, make a detour to Friche Belle de Mai—a former tobacco factory converted into a 45,000 m² cultural center. Galleries, artists' studios, concert hall, rooftop skatepark, restaurants. This is creative Marseille, far from tourist circuits. Take bus 49 from the Old Port.

⏱ 75 min

Late afternoon, head down to Prado beaches or Escale Borély for a seaside walk. Parc Borély is Marseille's largest green space, with its 18th-century château, botanical garden, and lake. The atmosphere is relaxed—joggers, families, pétanque players. Grab a drink on a terrace facing the sea before heading to Cassis the next day.

⏱ 60 min

Cassis

★★★★☆
1

Arrive early in Cassis—parking lots fill up quickly in season. The harbor is charming, with pastel façades, terraces, and aligned pointus. From there, look up toward Cap Canaille to the east: at 394 meters, it's France's highest sea cliff (and some say Europe's). The ochre-red color of the rock at sunset is a spectacle in itself.

⏱ 45 min
2

Head on foot toward Port-Miou calanque, 20 minutes from the center. This long 1,400-meter fjord, bordered by remains of an old limestone quarry operated from the 18th century until 1981, now serves as a natural harbor for hundreds of sailboats. In Provençal, "Port-Miou" means "good harbor"—it's the perfect shelter on windy days.

⏱ 30 min
3

Continue on the GR98 trail to Port-Pin—allow 40 minutes from Port-Miou. You'll recognize it by its small sand and pebble beach framed by Aleppo pines (hence its name). The water is translucent, turquoise—you'd think you were in the Caribbean. If you're there early morning, you'll have the beach almost to yourself.

⏱ 45 min
4

For motivated hikers, push on to the En-Vau belvedere—170 meters above the sea, aligned with the canyon. En-Vau is considered the massif's most beautiful calanque: vertical white cliffs 100 meters high plunge into impossibly blue water. Warning: the descent to the beach is steep and technical (Portalet passage, fixed rope), bring good shoes. Allow 1h30-2h from central Cassis.

If hiking doesn't appeal, board a cruise from Cassis harbor. Boats depart every 30 minutes in season for a calanques tour—3, 5, or 8 calanques depending on the formula. You'll see Port-Miou, Port-Pin, and En-Vau from the sea, which lets you appreciate the cliff heights. Allow 45 min to 2h depending on the route.

⏱ 90 min

Late afternoon, take the Route des Crêtes between Cassis and La Ciotat (D141). Over 15 km, this cliff road winds along the top of the Soubeyranes cliffs with viewpoints offering plunging views over Cassis on one side and La Ciotat bay on the other. Warning: the road closes in strong wind (mistral over 60 km/h). Best time is late afternoon for the light.

La Ciotat

★★★☆☆
1

First, step through the doors of Eden-Théâtre at 25 boulevard Clemenceau. Built in 1889, it's the world's oldest cinema still in operation. The Lumière brothers organized a private screening here as early as September 21, 1895—weeks before Paris—and the first paid public screening took place here on March 21, 1899. The Lumière family spent vacations in La Ciotat at their Clos des Plages château, and the town's streets were where they filmed their first movies, including the famous "Arrival of a Train at La Ciotat Station." The building, listed as a historic monument, was saved and renovated by passionate volunteers. Guided tours on Wednesdays and Saturdays afternoons.

⏱ 45 min
2

Next, head down to La Ciotat's Old Port. The quays are lined with 17th-century houses in colorful façades. At the quay's end, the old Town Hall (1864) houses the Ciotat museum with maritime history and Lumière brothers memorabilia. Did you know pétanque was invented here in 1907 or 1910, depending on sources? A monument commemorates it near the port.

⏱ 45 min
3

Continue on foot to Parc du Mugel, 20 minutes from the port. This 12-hectare botanical park nestles in a red-rock calanque, sheltered by the Bec de l'Aigle. Climb to the belvedere at 82 meters elevation: the view of ochre cliffs plunging into turquoise Mediterranean is probably La Ciotat's finest. The vegetation is lush—pines, eucalyptus, palms. Bring proper shoes for the trails.

⏱ 60 min
4

Right next to Mugel, stop at Figuerolles calanque. Smaller and more intimate than those near Cassis, it's distinguished by strangely shaped rocks sculpted by erosion and crystalline waters. If you're there late afternoon, the grazing light on the red rocks is superb.

⏱ 30 min

Le Castellet

★★☆☆☆
1

Enter Le Castellet village through the Grand Portail, one of only two fortified gates pierced in the ramparts—there's no other access. Listed among France's Most Beautiful Villages, this perched medieval village dominates Bandol vineyards 360°. Sloping lanes climb toward the 15th-century château (now town hall, interior not open to visitors). Marcel Pagnol filmed "The Baker's Wife" here in 1938 with Raimu—you can still see Place de l'Ormeau where scenes were shot.

⏱ 60 min
2

Look for the "Trou de Madame"—an opening in the ramparts, left of the château. It's a discreet but spectacular viewpoint: the entire Brûlat wine plain spreads before you, bordered by olive groves and pine forests, with the white line of the Sainte-Baume massif as backdrop. On clear days, the contrast between golden garrigue and blue sky is striking.

⏱ 20 min
3

Push on to La Cadière-d'Azur, 5 minutes by car. This hilltop village, also labeled a Var Village of Character, is less touristy than Le Castellet but equally charming. Its orientation table offers a panorama over vineyards, the distant sea, and Var hills. Lanes are quiet, stone façades golden—this is Provence in its authentic form.

⏱ 45 min
4

In Le Castellet, take time to browse artists' workshops—potters, ceramicists, painters, leatherworkers. The village has specialized in art crafts, and the Parcours des Arts lets you discover them shop by shop. Saint-Sauveur church, built in 1030 by the Marseille bishopric and oriented toward Jerusalem, deserves a look for its three-arch bell tower and campanile.

⏱ 40 min

Activities

Take advantage of your visit for a tasting at a Bandol AOC wine estate. Vineyards spread at Le Castellet's foot on sunny terraces. Mourvèdre dominates the reds—powerful, tannic wines that age remarkably well. Several estates offer visits and tastings without reservation in off-season, but call ahead in summer.

⏱ 75 min

Late afternoon, drive down to Bandol (15 minutes). The small marina is lined with terraces where you can grab a final drink facing the boats. The Tuesday morning Provençal market is worth the detour if your schedule allows. Bandol is also the departure point for shuttles to the Embiez islands—if you have half a day ahead, the island walk is pleasant.

Aix En Provence

★★★★☆
1

Begin with Cours Mirabeau, 440 meters long and 42 meters wide, lined with century-old plane trees forming a vegetal tunnel overhead. Four fountains punctuate the promenade, including the Mossy Fountain—covered in thick green moss, it's fed by a thermal spring at 18°C. At no. 55, look for Paul Cézanne's childhood home. At the Cours's upper end, the King René fountain shows the good king holding the Muscat grape he introduced to Provence—an 1819 statue by David d'Angers.

⏱ 60 min
2

Dive into the old town north of the Cours. Aix counts over a hundred fountains and dozens of 17th-18th century private mansions—wrought-iron balconies, sculpted doors, atlantes and caryatids. Place d'Albertas, with its fountain framed by Baroque façades, is among the finest. At Place de l'Hôtel de Ville, the Saturday morning flower market is a feast for eyes and nose.

⏱ 60 min
3

Visit Saint-Sauveur Cathedral, an astonishing building mixing five architectural styles from the 5th to 17th centuries: Merovingian baptistery, Romanesque nave, Gothic nave, Romanesque cloister, and Renaissance portal. Nicolas Froment's Burning Bush triptych (1476), in the Gothic nave, is the collection's jewel—ask the guardian to open it if the panels are closed.

⏱ 45 min
4

If you have time and desire for a powerful moment, head to the Camp des Milles Site-Memorial, 8 km southwest of Aix. This former tile factory served as an internment and deportation camp between 1939 and 1942—over 10,000 people were detained here, including artists and intellectuals who left poignant wall murals. The memorial, demanding but necessary, takes about 2h to visit.

Activities

Follow the "In Cézanne's Footsteps" walking route through Aix. Brass studs embedded in the ground guide your steps from Cours Mirabeau to the Lauves Studio, where the painter worked from 1902 until his death in 1906. The studio has been preserved as it was—easels, brushes, skulls and objects he painted in his still lifes. From the terrace, you have views of Mont Sainte-Victoire, which he painted over 80 times. Allow 1h30 total.

⏱ 90 min

End the trip with a calisson d'Aix, the local specialty—an almond and candied melon paste covered with royal icing. You'll find them in downtown confectioneries, some even offer production workshops. If you prefer savory, the Provençal markets (Tuesday, Thursday, Saturday on the Cours) overflow with tapenade, anchoïade, and local goat cheeses.
