The world on vacation

Italians who almost never leave their peninsula. Koreans flooding into Japan. Chinese travellers moving by the hundreds of millions during a single week. Here is the big comparison, backed by the numbers.

How many days off do you get depending on where you live? Do people stay home or go abroad? Where, for how long, on what daily budget? And are they hunting for bargains or for luxury? We combed through the major travel studies of 2025-2026, country by country, to answer these questions.

The result reveals radically different vacation cultures, from the two-night Japanese micro-trip to the weeks-long summer of Gulf families. And one global shift that speaks to us directly: everywhere, the traditional package tour is losing ground to the trip you build yourself. Safe travels.

France: five weeks off, yet they stay home

The French start with one of the best deals in the world: 25 days of paid leave, 11 public holidays, and often extra rest days on top. And yet they don't go far: 63 to 70% of summer holidaymakers stay in France. The sea comes first, Provence, Brittany, the Atlantic coast, Occitanie. When they do cross a border, it's for Spain, Italy, Greece or Tunisia.

The trend is clear: 69% name price as their number one criterion, more than half leave in June or September to pay less, and nearly one holidaymaker in two goes away for a week or less. French vacations mean campsites, rentals and the family house far more than five-star hotels.

Germany: the kings of the package holiday

The legal minimum is 20 days, but most German employees get 28 to 30, plus 9 to 14 public holidays depending on the region. And they do leave: depending on the study, two thirds to nearly 80% of holiday trips are taken abroad. Spain crushes the rankings, ahead of Italy, Turkey, Greece and Austria. Majorca remains a virtual German annex.

The big annual trip lasts 13 days on average and costs €1,636 per person, about €130 a day, an all-time record. The typical profile: the all-inclusive package, booked early, with a real budget but no extravagance. Comfortable, reliable, well-planned mid-range. Very German.

04

United Kingdom: holidays above all else, whatever the cost

The British are entitled to 5.6 weeks of leave, that is 28 days including bank holidays. Their relationship with holidays is almost sentimental: 87% went away at least once in the year, 59% abroad, and 65% say their holiday is the most important moment of the year. Remarkably, it's the last thing they will cut back on: restaurants, cinema and clothes go first.

The winning formula: the beach (nearly one trip in two), one to two weeks, and Spain as the beloved destination, far ahead of the rest. In Spain, a British visitor stays 7.7 days on average and spends €184 a day (2025 data), around €1,420 per stay. One of the highest daily budgets in Europe: on holiday, the Brit doesn't count pennies, he makes up for a year of grey skies.

05

Italy: the sea, the family, and never leaving the country

Italians have around four weeks of leave plus 11 public holidays, and summer remains sacred. But it's one of the most home-loving countries in Europe: depending on the study, 80 to 90% of holidaymakers stay in Italy. Hard to blame them: Puglia, Sardinia, Sicily, Trentino, the Romagna Riviera. The sea takes three quarters of all stays. When they do leave, it's for Spain, Greece, France, and a booming newcomer: Albania, at half the price.

On the budget side, the main holiday costs around €900 to €975 per person, roughly €94 a day. The secret of this thrift: nearly 3 Italians in 10 stay with family or friends, and many in the family house. Free accommodation is a national institution.

Spain: many trips, small budgets

On paper, the Spanish are spoiled: 30 calendar days of leave and 14 public holidays, one of the highest totals in Europe. In reality they travel a lot, but mostly at home: 87% of their trips stay in Spain. No wonder, they live in the continent's number one beach destination.

The budget contrast is striking: €66 a day and €263 per trip within Spain, versus €131 a day and more than €1,000 per trip abroad. Only 13% of trips cross the border, but they absorb 37% of the spending. The typical summer: the coast, the family apartment, and stays often split into several short getaways.

07

Netherlands: Europe's keenest travellers

The legal minimum is 20 days, most get 25 or more, and above all the Dutch receive a bonus that is one of a kind: the vakantiegeld, 8% of annual salary paid out in May, specifically to fund the holidays. A country that pays you to leave. And leave they do: 85 to 88% have holiday plans every year, with an average of 23 days of travel in total.

The country being small, flat and rainy, more than half of all holidays happen abroad: Spain first (a third of holidaymakers), then Germany and France, where the Dutch caravan on the southbound motorway is a monument of summer. Controlled budget: most plan €1,000 to €2,000 per person for the whole year. Campsites and bungalows, and proud of it.

08

United States: the only rich country with no guaranteed leave

It's the global anomaly: no federal law requires a single day of paid leave. Everything is negotiated with the employer. In practice, an American employee starts with 10 or 11 days, reaches 15 after five years, and part of those days go unused. As a result, American vacations are short and intense. The country invented the road trip for a reason: the vast majority of leisure trips stay domestic, Florida, the national parks, California, Las Vegas.

Abroad, the top three: Mexico, the Caribbean, Europe, with Japan rising fast. And when they do go, they spend: the average summer budget exceeds $3,000 per person, and per day the range runs from $120 on a budget to more than $900 in luxury mode. Americans make up for lack of time with money.

09

Canada: the year everything changed

The entitlement is modest for a rich country: 2 weeks of legal leave in most provinces, 3 after a few years of seniority, plus about ten public holidays. For decades, Canadian vacations had one address: the United States. Florida in winter, shopping weekends, Disney. Then came 2025: trade tensions, tariffs, a poisoned political climate. Leisure travel to the United States dropped by a fifth to a third depending on the quarter of 2025.

That money didn't stay under the mattress: domestic tourism is breaking records, and the shift abroad is massive, Mexico first (according to Mexican airport data, Toronto became the number one international route into Cancún, ahead of US cities), then Europe and the Caribbean. A textbook case: geopolitics redrawing a vacation map in a single year.

Brazil: 30 days off, beach first

Thirty calendar days after one year on the job, including at least 14 in a row. One of the most generous regimes in the world. Brazilians have the time to travel, and they mostly travel at home: the beaches of the Nordeste, Rio, Florianópolis, and a huge culture of family tourism. The weak currency makes going abroad expensive, so the favourite foreign destinations are close by: Argentina first, Buenos Aires for shopping, Bariloche for snow, then Uruguay and Chile.

The two big long-haul exceptions: the United States, with Miami and Orlando as shopping-and-theme-park pilgrimages, and Portugal, the European gateway with no language barrier. Style-wise, Brazilians increasingly organise the trip themselves, favour budget accommodation, and put the money into experiences and shopping rather than the hotel.

Japan: the country afraid to take its own vacations

The right exists: 10 days after six months on the job, up to 20 later, plus 16 public holidays. But corporate culture does the rest: according to the Labour Ministry survey, in 2022 employees took only 11 of the 17.6 days they were granted, and despite a recent record the rate barely tops 65%. Taking two weeks in a row is still frowned upon. Hence a unique model: micro-vacations. One or two nights at an onsen, a weekend in Kyoto or Hakone, and everyone leaves at once during Golden Week in May, Obon in August and New Year.

About 70% of Japanese travellers prefer to stay in the country, for safety, price and convenience. Foreign travel is slowly recovering, but the weak yen holds it back: destinations are close and affordable, South Korea, Taiwan, Thailand, with Hawaii as the ultimate dream. Spending stays cautious, trips stay short.

12

China: 6.5 billion trips a year

Legal paid leave is meagre: 5 days with less than ten years of seniority, 15 at most. But the public holidays change everything: the Golden Weeks of Chinese New Year and October each offer a full week when the entire country moves at once. The numbers are dizzying: 6.5 billion domestic trips in 2025, at a modest average spend of around 1,000 yuan per trip, about €125. Abroad, nearly 168 million departures in 2025, two thirds of them to Hong Kong, Macau and Taiwan, then Japan, Thailand, South Korea and Vietnam, and in Europe France, plus Norway and Iceland gone viral on Chinese social media.

And one cliché needs burying: the tour bus behind a flag-waving guide is over. In 2025, 56% of Chinese travellers going abroad organised the trip themselves, versus 27% on a group tour. Among 18-24 year olds, independent travel reaches 71%. The new generation plans on social media, books last minute and, above all, takes the wheel: overseas car rentals jumped 50% over the summer, for road trips of 7 to 8 days, notably in Europe.

13

South Korea: the country that travels the most... next door

About 15 days of leave after one year on the job, plus public holidays. A Japanese-style regime on paper, but the exact opposite behaviour. The numbers are staggering: 29.55 million departures from the country in 2025, an all-time record, for 51 million inhabitants. Relative to population, one of the highest outbound rates in the world.

And the destination is overwhelming: Japan alone drew 9.46 million Korean visitors in 2025, +69% compared to 2019 (a year of the Korean boycott of Japan, so the comparison starts from a low base). The weak yen turned the neighbouring archipelago into a low-cost getaway: a two-hour flight, a few days, shopping, ramen and onsen. Far behind come Vietnam and China. The flip side: a tourism deficit of more than $10 billion a year.

14

India: the giant just getting started

The typical Indian employee has about fifteen days of annual leave, topped up by numerous national and regional holidays. Domestic tourism is colossal, often tied to religious festivals and weddings: pilgrimages, Goa, Kerala, Rajasthan. But the real story is international: around 30 million Indians went abroad over the year, a record, with projections of 80 to 90 million annual trips by 2040.

The star destinations: Dubai, Thailand, Singapore, Vietnam, with a breakthrough towards Europe. In 2023 the average spend was around $1,000 per trip, but the trend is upmarket: 84% of travellers surveyed in 2025 planned to raise their budget by 20 to 50%. India is moving from the budget group tour to the tailor-made trip, and sometimes to true luxury.

15

Australia: everything is far, so they go long

Four weeks of legal leave plus about ten public holidays. Decent, no more. The real Australian issue is geography: the nearest neighbour is a 3-hour flight away, Europe 22. It doesn't stop them at all: 12.6 million trips abroad in 2025, an all-time record, for 27 million inhabitants. The podium: Indonesia, meaning Bali, about 1.7 million Australians a year for a typical 9-day stay, then New Zealand, and Japan, whose visitor numbers have tripled in ten years to the point of overtaking the United States.

The Australian paradox: budgets squeezed by expensive flights, yet ultra-rational choices, with exchange rates and on-the-ground costs as the top criteria for picking a destination. Hence Asia's triumph: far from home, everything is cheap. Domestic tourism remains massive too, with 113 million overnight trips a year.

Gulf states: the world's most luxurious summer

UAE, Saudi Arabia, Qatar, Kuwait: here the key variable isn't labour law. When the thermometer passes 45 degrees in July, people leave, often as an extended family, often for a long time. And they leave with unmatched means: outbound tourism spending averages $2,262 per inhabitant per year in the Gulf, eleven times the world average. Qatar peaks at $6,836 per inhabitant.

In the United Kingdom, the Gulf visitor spends an average of $2,850 per trip; in Switzerland, 73% of their overnight stays are in 4 or 5-star hotels. It's the only market in the world where luxury is not a segment but the norm. The destinations tell the same story: London, Paris, Geneva, and the chic Mediterranean summer, Marbella, Cannes, Nice. A recent trend: Southeast Asia now captures more than a quarter of summer demand.

Road trip or package tour? The great shift

One thread runs through all these countries: everywhere, the traditional organised tour is losing ground to the trip you build yourself. Americans invented the road trip and never looked back. The Chinese, symbols of group tourism ten years ago, are now 56% independent travellers and rent cars for 8-day trips across Europe. Brazilians favour the home-planned trip. Indians are moving from the group circuit to tailor-made.

Two strongholds resist: Germany, where the all-inclusive package to the Mediterranean still reigns, and the United Kingdom, attached to its travel agents. But the underlying trend is global: digital tools everywhere, personalised itineraries, and the freedom of the road as the new ideal. What to remember

Four models emerge. The Mediterranean model (France, Italy, Spain): plenty of days, the home coastline, the family, a tight budget. The travelling north model (Germany, United Kingdom, Netherlands, Australia): go far, go sunny, and protect that budget above everything else. The hurried Asian model (Japan, China, Korea): few days actually taken, short and frequent trips, but diverging behaviours, homebound in Japan, frenetic in Korea, ever freer in China. And the model of extremes: the Gulf embodying pure luxury, India and Brazil on the rise, Canada redrawing its map in a single season.

One certainty to close: wherever you live, nobody gives up on vacations. We shorten, we split, we negotiate, but we go. And more and more often, we go behind the wheel, itinerary in hand. Maybe that's humanity's true common ground.
