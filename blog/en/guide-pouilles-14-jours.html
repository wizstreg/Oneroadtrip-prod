01

The three-base method

This article pulls together the five pieces we wrote about Puglia, all linked further down. We spent fourteen days there, crossing the region from north to south. Here's what stayed with us, what we'd do differently, and every tip we can give you.

We built our stay around three bases, a method we recommend: you drop your bags and radiate out, rather than unpacking every single night. Distance covered: exactly 2,000 km.

  • Alberobello. For exploring the Itria Valley, trulli country, with its white hilltop villages and the Adriatic coast from Polignano a Mare to Monopoli. Matera, technically in Basilicata, is easy to visit from here.
  • Castro Marina. Right down south, for the Salento: Otranto, Lecce, Gallipoli, the sea caves and the beaches on the heel of the boot.
  • Trani. For heading back up into the Terra di Bari and the north: Castel del Monte, Barletta, Bari, and the long sandy beaches of Margherita di Savoia.

04

What we'd change

Alberobello, five nights. We'd drop to four. The location is perfect: you fan out less than an hour in every direction, without ever feeling you're wasting time on the road. But rather than doing Matera as a day trip, we'd have slept there one night, then seen the coast before heading down to Castro.

Castro Marina, six nights. Two too many if you go via Matera. Four would have been enough. The Salento is quick to cover when your base is well placed, and Castro is: Otranto 25 minutes away, Lecce 35 minutes, the Grotta della Poesia three quarters of an hour, Gallipoli 50 minutes.

Trani, three nights. That's the right number, we wouldn't touch it.

The Gargano, zero nights. Our real regret. The two nights saved on Castro deserved to be spent here: the east coast with Vieste, Peschici, the Baia delle Zagare and the trabucchi; inland with the Foresta Umbra, Monte Sant'Angelo and San Giovanni Rotondo; and the Tremiti islands offshore.

05

When to go: our take on high season

We went at the end of July and the first week of August, in other words the worst possible time according to everything you read. Frankly, we didn't find Puglia packed. We drove without trouble, and in most of the towns we were aiming for, there weren't many tourists.

Where they do gather is on the beaches. The lidos and the coves fill up fast, especially from 10am onwards. Inland stays quiet.

Our advice. In the busiest towns (Alberobello, Polignano a Mare, Otranto, Lecce) you won't dodge the crowds. Visit them at the end of the day or in the evening: fewer people, better light, and you escape the afternoon heat that hits 30 to 35 degrees.

An alternative, which contradicts what we've just told you. You can also visit those towns in the early afternoon, precisely because something is happening there at that hour. You'll find things to do and places to eat, whereas plenty of non-touristy towns shut down completely between 1pm and 4pm.

A word on temperatures: mornings and evenings are fairly cool, bring a light layer. It catches you out when you arrive expecting 35 degrees day and night.

06

Frederick II, the ghost of the trip

Travelling around Puglia, you'll keep running into Frederick II of Hohenstaufen (1194-1250), nicknamed Stupor Mundi, the wonder of the world. He founded the towns you drive through, turned the region into a cultural crossroads between East and West, and made it a laboratory of power.

He's everywhere. In Altamura, he refounded the city in 1232 after a revolt. In Gravina, he built the cathedral and the castle. And at Castel del Monte, his esoteric masterpiece, ordered around 1240, which to this day nobody can explain the purpose of.

Frederick II spoke six languages. He welcomed Greek, Arab, Italian and Jewish scholars to his court. He was also a mathematician, an astronomer, and probably the architect of his own monuments. He wrote a treatise on hunting that is still a reference today. And at the same time he was a man of power, brutal, who never flinched at a massacre.

The three towns show his three faces. Altamura and Gravina are where he lived, where he built to last. Castel del Monte is his riddle, his monument to himself, built around the number eight, aligned with the solstices, and closed to every theory. Visiting it, you'll understand that Frederick II resembles no other medieval ruler.

07

The beaches, and the proper use of the Italian siesta

We did six different beaches. Let's be honest, as seasoned travellers, these aren't the most beautiful beaches we've been to. But they give you a break from sightseeing.

Our one tip, if we only get to give one. Go between 1pm and 4pm. It's the hottest part of the day, and above all it's the hour when Italy shuts. Churches and almost every monument pull the shutters down over that window. You may as well be in the water.

What we did ourselves: we kept visiting towns between 1pm and 4pm. That works in a tourist town, where there's always somewhere to walk, drink and eat. Elsewhere you'll have little to do, and you'll even struggle to get fed: many restaurants only open in the evening.

Our ranking of the six beaches

  • 1. Baia dei Turchi, near Otranto.The finest of the lot. Room to breathe, an unspoilt setting, superb water.
  • 2. Punta Prosciutto, north of Porto Cesareo.Free beach where we weren't crammed in, which is rare in the Salento in August.
  • 3. Margherita di Savoia, north of Barletta.Kilometres of sand where you almost feel alone. We finished one day at Beach Dunes, very well kept, with a proper restaurant on site.
  • 4. Lido Sabbia d'Oro, 10 to 15 km south of Capitolo.Nothing exceptional, but sun loungers at 20 € a day instead of the 160 € asked at the Capitolo lidos. The tip came from a security guard, and it was a good one.
  • 5. Castro.Rocks, and it's packed.
  • 6. Pescoluse, the famous "Maldives of the Salento".It's jammed. No little fish, but plenty of little tourists.

Bear in mind that a large part of the Puglian coastline isn't sand: it's rock or cliff, especially between Otranto and Santa Maria di Leuca.

What we didn't do and what always comes up in the rankings: Torre dell'Orso and its Due Sorelle, Torre Sant'Andrea and its limestone arches, the Torre Guaceto reserve north of Brindisi, the Porto Selvaggio park near Nardò, and above all the whole Gargano in the north (Vieste, Baia delle Zagare, the Tremiti islands), which is a trip in its own right.

Prices. Outside Capitolo, south of Monopoli, which is a case of its own, reckon on 20 € (Margherita di Savoia) to 50 € (Trani) for two loungers and a parasol. Public beaches are free, but bring your own parasol and, depending on the spot, your boxing gloves to get a place.

08

Zone 1: around Alberobello, the Itria Valley

The very touristy places

Left, the trulli of the Rione Monti in Alberobello. Right, the Sassi of Matera.
  • Alberobello.The 1,000 trulli of the Rione Monti, packed by day, a different place in the evening once the shops close and the crowd thins out. Opposite, the Rione Aia Piccola and its 400 still-inhabited trulli, with no shops: the UNESCO quarter most people miss.
  • Matera.Technically in Basilicata, an hour away. The cave-dwelling Sassi floor you. A full day minimum, two days and an evening would be ideal, the city lit up at night is a show of its own.
  • Locorotondo.White circular village perched at 410 metres, superb view over the valley. The church of San Giorgio and its 35-metre dome. Parking, on the other hand, is a fight, unlike Alberobello.
  • Ostuni.The città bianca. A maze of white lanes climbing up to the cathedral, the Porta Azzurra, and the olive groves running down to the Adriatic.
  • Monopoli.Old harbour with blue fishing boats, lanes lined with shops, baroque cathedral and Charles V's castle. You cover it quickly.
  • Polignano a Mare.The Lama Monachile cove under its Bourbon bridge, the statue of Modugno with arms wide open to the sea, lines of poetry painted on the walls. Very touristy, and still worth the trip.
Left to right: the Lama Monachile cove in Polignano a Mare, the Porta Azzurra in Ostuni, the Itria Valley seen from Locorotondo.

The pretty places worth the detour

  • Cisternino.One of the most beautiful villages in Italy, an Orange Flag from the Touring Club. Small, quiet, full of flowers, and the capital of bombette grilled in front of you in the butchers' shops in the centre.
  • Fasano.The old centre is called U'mbracchie, the shade: the lanes are so narrow that the sun never gets in, and it stays cool even in August.
  • Martina Franca.One of the biggest towns in the region. Palazzo Ducale with its inner courtyard open free of charge, the basilica of San Martino, an opera festival every summer.
  • Ceglie Messapica.Known as the gastronomic capital of Puglia. Medieval maze, castle at the top, big white square. The right place for dinner.
  • Conversano.Quiet, no tourists. The Acquaviva castle looks down over the town, and its restaurant is good and cheap. The monastery of San Benedetto, whose abbesses ruled over the local clergy, was nicknamed the Monstrum Apuliae.
  • Gioia del Colle.Norman-Swabian castle of Frederick II, one of the finest in the region after Castel del Monte. PDO mozzarella and DOC Primitivo. A town that lives its own life far from tourism.
  • Noci.Between Alberobello and Matera. Half an hour to an hour, just for the atmosphere of the lanes and the small Italian squares.
  • Putignano.The oldest carnival in Europe, running since 1394. Out of season, less charming than its neighbours, but a coffee on the square goes down well before tackling the caves.

The other places

  • Castellana caves.Two hours, three kilometres, the biggest cave knockout of our lives. Guided tour in French. The Grotta Bianca at the far end.
  • Cantina Polvanera (Gioia del Colle).Primitivo tasting, bottles from 6 to 20 €. Take a Polvanera 14 and a 16, the numbers are the alcohol content.
  • Castello Marchione.A five-minute stop near the caves. Former hunting lodge turned events venue. We were the only ones there.
  • Egnazia.The largest Roman city in Puglia, 40 hectares by the sea. We gave up on it: without a guided tour it's ruins with no explanation, and at 35 degrees we headed for the beach.

Zone 2: around Castro Marina, the Salento

The very touristy places

Otranto, the easternmost point of Italy. Its cathedral holds a floor mosaic of 600,000 tesserae, made between 1163 and 1165, with a Tree of Life carried by two elephants.

Allow a full day. The chapel of the 800 martyrs of 1480 and the crypt with its 42 all-different columns are worth the detour on their own.

The beach at Gallipoli, with the church of the Purità on the left.
  • Otranto.The easternmost point of Italy. The cathedral and its mosaic of 600,000 tesserae with its Tree of Life, the chapel of the 800 martyrs of 1480, the crypt with its 42 all-different columns. A full day, we weren't expecting so much.
  • Gallipoli.Old town on its island, Angevin castle, and two visits not to miss: the Frantoio Ipogeo at 2 €, an underground olive mill from the 17th century, and the church of the Purità, dark and almost oppressive, built by the dockers' guild.
  • Castro and Castro Marina.The marina below, the old town on the cliff above. Rocky beach, clear water, and under the cathedral the Sacra Textile exhibition, a vaulted room of church vestments, unexpected and genuinely beautiful.

The big quiet towns

  • Lecce.The Florence of the South. A 4-church ticket at 11 €, the Roman amphitheatre rising up in the middle of Piazza Sant'Oronzo, San Matteo with its rippling Borromini-style façade, the papier-mâché ceiling of Santa Chiara. Bring a power bank, you'll be taking photos on every corner.
  • Nardò.Second historic centre of the province of Lecce, and a surprise on every corner. Piazza Salandra entirely baroque, Fontana del Toro, Norman cathedral and its 12th century Cristo Nero.
  • Brindisi.The Roman columns that marked the end of the Via Appia, the cathedral, the temple of San Giovanni al Sepolcro, closed on Wednesdays. We were the only tourists. More a town to live in than to visit.

The pretty places worth the detour

  • Galatina.Birthplace of the pasticciotto, invented at Ascalone in 1745, still going, eleventh generation. And above all the basilica of Santa Caterina d'Alessandria: second fresco cycle in Italy after Assisi.
  • Maglie.Aldo Moro's home town. The beauty is up high: sculptures, dragons, ironwork on the façades. The 48-metre bell tower is the tallest in the province.
  • Tricase.A fairly big town with shops for shopping. Palazzo Risolo and the mother church on the main square.
  • Tricase Porto.Pretty little fishing harbour with a pleasant beach. Quick lunch at Martinucci, a Salento institution since 1950.
  • Specchia.Medieval 9th century village perched among the olive groves, listed as one of the most beautiful villages in Italy and barely visited. At 4pm in high summer, only the swallows and us were walking the lanes.
  • Santa Cesarea Terme.Spa town on the cliffs, promenade above the Adriatic. Villa Sticchi and its purple dome look like a little mosque set down on the sea.
  • Galatone.The sanctuary of the Crocifisso della Pietà and its legend: the church collapsed in 1682, the oil lamp stayed lit in the rubble. The Chiesa Madre is under restoration.
  • Ugento.Unpretentious. The cathedral was open, plain interior, polychrome marble altar and olive-wood choir stalls.

The places we liked less

  • Casarano.We arrived at half past twelve, everything shut, not a tourist, not a resident. The church of Casaranello, the only real point of interest, only opens from 4pm to 5pm. Give it a miss, unless you're sure of the window.
  • Presicce-Acquarica.Listed as one of the most beautiful villages in Italy on paper. On the ground the village is deserted, the baroque palaces are superb and very run down, and almost everything is shut.
  • Porto Cesareo.A quarter of an hour is enough. Deserted at midday, you struggle to find a restaurant. The upside: when you do find one, you have it to yourself.

The other places

  • Grotta della Poesia (Roca Vecchia), 5 €.A natural pool 30 metres by 18, an unreal turquoise. Swimming banned since 2019, the rock is porous. Its neighbour hides 600 m² of Messapian, Greek and Latin inscriptions.
  • Boat trip from Castro.Three hours, 35 € per person, 9am departure. Grotta Palombara, Grotta Azzurra, and two swims including the Baia di Porto Miggiano.
  • Grotta Zinzulusa, 7 €.Visited on foot, not by boat. 160 metres into the cliff, the Dome 25 metres high, Lake Cocito, and the names of the guano workers written on the rock in 1906 and 1933.

Zone 3: around Trani, the Terra di Bari and the north

The very touristy places

  • Trani.Historic centre restored extremely well without being frozen. The cathedral built on three stacked churches, Barisano's bronze door, the hypogeum at 1 €. The Jewish quarter and the Scolanova synagogue, open 11am to noon only, with its bell and its Star of David on the same façade.
  • Bari Vecchia.The old town of Bari, tourists included. San Sabino cathedral (paid entry), piazza Mercantile and its colonna della Giustizia, the Pigna fountain. Very good, but not a match for the small coastal towns. Two to three hours is enough.

The pretty places worth the detour

  • Barletta.Far more to see than you'd imagine. Santa Maria Maggiore, half Romanesque half Gothic, Charles V's castle, the five-metre bronze Colossus planted in the middle of a shopping street, and the Cantina della Disfida, 14th century cellars where the 1503 challenge was issued.
  • Specchiolla.On the road between Castro and Trani, north of Brindisi. Nothing extraordinary, but the coffee stop by the beach and the view are worth pulling over for.
  • Altamura.Charming old town, its cathedral looming over the main street seen from above, and a small museum. Compulsory: coffee, and buying a traditional loaf from the old ovens in the centre.
  • Gravina.The little Matera, even more remarkable. Plenty to see and the charm works on you straight away.
  • Giovinazzo.The favourite of the last day. Shaded seafront right at water level, an empty and beautiful old town, the sort of place where you'd tell yourself it would be good to live. Close to Bari, ideal for a retreat away from the crowds.
  • Molfetta.Small fishing harbour with a very busy fish market, the two bell towers of San Corrado cathedral framing the façade. Worth doing on the way back to Bari, but it's no Trani.
Left, the ravine of Gravina in Puglia. Right, the harbour of Giovinazzo.

The places we liked less

  • Bisceglie.Co-cathedral with its matronei and its crypt, the Chianca dolmen set back from town. You go for the atmosphere more than for the monuments.
  • Canosa di Puglia.The town badly lacks charm, and any signposting about what there is to see. Only stop if the archaeological parks of San Leucio and San Giovanni interest you.
  • Andria.The old town is a little prettier than Canosa, but everything is shut: ducal palace under repair, cathedral closed. We drove round a while looking for somewhere to eat, and called it a day there.

The other places

  • Castel del Monte.The most enigmatic monument in Puglia, UNESCO listed and engraved on the back of the one-cent coin. Ordered by Frederick II around 1240, built entirely around the number eight, and nobody knows what it was for. You can't drive up: car park below, then shuttle.
  • Villa Comunale in Trani.Our favourite corner, rarely mentioned in guidebooks. Public garden that stays cool when the town bakes, parakeets flying free, a turtle pond, and the cathedral facing you across the harbour. L'Aquae serves drinks there with your feet in the water: 53 € for two loungers, closing at 7pm except on Saturdays.
  • Margherita di Savoia and Beach Dunes.Fine sand, gentle slope into the water, a proper restaurant on site.

Car, ZTL and parking

Renting a car

530 € for two weeks, insurance included. A small SUV is ideal: high enough for the Murgia, small enough for the car parks. Air conditioning is essential in July and August. Rental desks close around 11pm at Bari airport.

The real headache in Puglia
ZTLs, limited traffic zones

Every historic centre has one, and the cameras catch you if you aren't registered.

Rule 1Only enter a ZTL if your hotel is inside it and parking is included in the booking
Rule 2Ask your hotel or your rental company for a permit or an exemption
Rule 3If you get it wrong, pay the fine online within 60 days, 80 € on average
Not payingCosts a great deal more

The towns concerned: Alberobello, Locorotondo, Cisternino, Martina Franca, Ostuni, Polignano a Mare, Monopoli, Brindisi, Lecce, Otranto, Gallipoli, Nardò and Trani.

Parking

  • The pay-and-display machine. You enter your number plate. Two hours free between 1pm and 4pm, then 1 to 2 € an hour. Go looking for the machine: it's never near your car, often 200 metres away, rarely signposted. Use an app like EasyPark to pay remotely and extend.
  • The attended car park. Some towns have nothing else. Reckon on 4 € for 24 hours in Castro Marina, 5 € in Trani.

How we feel about it

We loved Puglia. But we love everything about Italy, which we've criss-crossed from end to end. Its history, its culture, its towns, its climate, its beaches. Puglia is an accessible place where you'll find several wonders, whether you stay two weeks or less.

The unmentionables

Two things we didn't tell in any of the five articles.

The palace of the naked man, in Presicce

Presicce won't leave us with an unforgettable memory. But I see a very handsome palace, and its door ajar. Since there was nothing to visit, I tell myself they wanted to do the tourist a favour. Without being too sure of myself.

So I push the door a little, and I see quite a few boxes off to the side. I push a bit more, and there... a bed straight on the floor, and on it a man. Naked.

I think at top speed: either this is a guide showing round a very particular sort of palace, or we'd better run. I go for option two, shut the door loudly and drag Patricia away, picturing him chasing us stark naked. We made our escape.

Never go into that palace. Ever.

The driving genius, between Gravina and Castel del Monte

Faithful reconstruction of events, according to the driver. Image generated by artificial intelligence.

Another thing I'm very proud of. Coming back from Gravina, we'd eaten well. The drive to Castel del Monte is lovely but very monotonous: fields as far as you can see, a dead straight road. So I'm driving, when I hear madam scream "Darling! Watch out!", which wakes me from the well-earned nap I was having.

And there I see a bend, my speedo at 80, the field a metre away from me. I brake, I turn, the car skids, I counter-steer, and we end up facing the other way having just missed rolling over.

Patricia tells me we came close to death. I tell her I reacted like a god of the road. Our last-second rescue will live forever in our memories. In truth, it was so flat that ending up in the field wouldn't have done much harm. Rolling over would have been more dangerous, mind you. But I keep that thought to myself, because Patou is very proud of herself and of her co-driving.
