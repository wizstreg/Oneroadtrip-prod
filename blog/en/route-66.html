01

Route 66, myth and reality

3,940 kilometres from Chicago to the Pacific, eight states crossed, a century of American history. And a surprise for many: officially, Route 66 no longer exists.

Created in 1926, the "Mother Road" was the great artery of the westward migration and the symbol of freedom on four wheels. But it was decommissioned in 1985, replaced by the modern Interstates. What survives today is a signposted "Historic Route 66", made of preserved sections and stretches that merge with the highway. You don't drive one continuous road: you follow a trace, which makes a good GPS with the waypoints essential.

That's exactly what makes its charm. Along the route survive neon motels, 1950s diners, gas stations frozen in time and kitsch attractions found nowhere else. As it nears its centenary in 2026, Route 66 has never felt more alive. Here are its iconic stops, in travel order from east to west, then three angles to live it to the fullest: where to sleep, where to eat, and why the movie Cars owes it everything.

Illinois and Missouri: the start

It all begins in Chicago, at the corner of Adams Street and Michigan Avenue, under a "Begin Historic Route 66" sign so discreet you have to look for it. This is mile zero. Right away in Illinois the tone is set: the first "Muffler Men", those giant fibreglass advertising figures, and in Pontiac more than twenty murals telling the story of the Route. Then comes Missouri and its passage through St. Louis, marked by the Gateway Arch, the "gateway to the West".

Illinois · The first giant
Gemini Giant

In Wilmington stands the first "Muffler Man" of the route: a 9-metre fibreglass giant holding a Gemini rocket, planted there in the 1960s to draw drivers to a restaurant. It's the archetype of the roadside attraction that built the folklore of the 66.

Chain of Rocks Bridge

This 1929 bridge spanning the Mississippi has a unique quirk: it makes a 22-degree bend right in the middle. Now reserved for pedestrians and cyclists, it's one of the most photogenic spots on the route, and access is free.

Oklahoma: the heart of the Route

Oklahoma holds the greatest number of Route 66 miles, and a fine lineup of icons. Just before you get there, the tiny Kansas stretch (only 21 km) passes through Galena and its old tow truck that inspired a character in the movie Cars. In the state, don't miss the Round Barn in Arcadia, a wooden round barn from 1898, the only one of its kind on the whole Route.

Oklahoma · The concrete icon
Blue Whale of Catoosa

A smiling 24-metre blue whale set at the edge of a pond, built in 1972 by a retiree for his wife's anniversary. You can climb on its back. Now a cult stop, it captures on its own the good-natured, homemade spirit of Route 66.

Era1972, an anniversary gift turned monument
WhereCatoosa, near Tulsa
PriceFree

Oklahoma · The giant bottle
POPS 66

Impossible to miss the 20-metre-tall soda bottle sculpture that marks the entrance to this stop in Arcadia. Inside, more than 700 varieties of soda from around the world. Try an improbable one, you won't find it anywhere else.

04

Texas and New Mexico: neon and desert

In Texas, near Amarillo, stands one of the most famous stops on the whole route: Cadillac Ranch. A little further on, the MidPoint Cafe in Adrian marks the exact middle of the Route, 1,139 miles from each end. Then the journey tips into New Mexico, into the neon, of which Tucumcari is the capital.

Texas · Graffiti encouraged
Cadillac Ranch

Ten Cadillacs buried nose-down in a field near Amarillo, planted there in 1974 by an eccentric patron as a tribute to the evolution of the tail fin. They're covered in constantly renewed layers of graffiti. You have to walk 400 metres into the field from the car park. It's one of the few places in the world where tagging is encouraged.

EraInstalled in 1974
WhereNear Amarillo, Texas
TipBring a spray can, tagging is allowed
PriceFree
New Mexico · The finest neon
Blue Swallow Motel

Arrive at nightfall: the swallow-shaped neon sign, promising "100% Refrigerated Air", is considered the finest lit sign on all of Route 66. This 1939 motor court, listed, has a garage attached to each room, the norm back when travellers kept an eye on their car.

05

Arizona: the preserved stretch

It's in Arizona that Route 66 was saved. In 1987, a Seligman barber, Angel Delgadillo, founded the association that kept the Mother Road from fading into oblivion after its decommissioning. And 130 km north of Flagstaff, a detour is a must, even though it's not on the Route: the Grand Canyon, its 1,800 metres of depth and two billion years of strata.

Arizona · The intact Route
The Seligman-Kingman stretch

The 145 kilometres between Seligman and Kingman are the best-preserved stretch of all Route 66: two lanes, almost no traffic, the same road as in 1955 through the scrub and the ranches. Along the way, the Hackberry General Store, a 1930s gas station frozen in time, period pumps and a red Corvette out front.

Arizona · The Eagles song
Standing on the Corner, Winslow

"Standing on the corner in Winslow, Arizona…": this line from a 1972 Eagles song saved the small town from decline. A street corner with a bronze statue and a mural pays tribute to the song. A five-minute stop, but the tune will stay in your head all day.

06

California: the Mojave desert and the ocean

The final stretch crosses the Mojave desert, the most desolate and most spectacular part of the route. Fill up on fuel and water in Needles: stations are rare over the next 250 km, and it often tops 45°C in summer. In Oro Grande, the Bottle Tree Ranch lines up 200 "trees" of metal pipes crowned with glass bottles that chime in the wind.

California · In the middle of nowhere
Roy's Motel & Café, Amboy

In the very heart of the Mojave, literally in the middle of nowhere, this 1930s motel and its Googie sign form one of the most photographed images of the Route. The site is being renovated for the 2026 centenary. It can top 45°C here in summer: stay cautious.

Era1930s, iconic Googie sign
WhereAmboy, Mojave desert
CautionExtreme heat in summer, few stations around
StatusUnder renovation for the 2026 centenary
California · The end of the road
Santa Monica Pier

The "End of the Trail" sign, put up in 2009, marks the official end of Route 66. You've covered 3,940 km since Chicago, and the Pacific is in front of you. Behind the sign, the Ferris wheel of Pacific Park and the historic 1922 carousel. It's the end of the Mother Road and the start of California.

07

Sleeping on the Route: the neon motels

On Route 66, the lodging is part of the journey. Forget the standardised chains: the experience is sleeping in the period motor courts, those 1930s-to-1950s motels whose neon signs light up one by one at nightfall. Many have been restored and still operate.

The must-stays: the Blue Swallow Motel in Tucumcari (1939), the finest neon on the Route; the Wigwam Motel in San Bernardino, whose 19 rooms are 1949 concrete tipis surrounded by vintage cars; La Posada in Winslow, a former 1929 Harvey House by Mary Colter, often cited as the finest lodging on the route; and the El Rancho in Gallup (1937), which hosted Hollywood stars in town to shoot westerns, each room bearing a star's name.

One tip you'll hear everywhere: these legendary addresses are small and in high demand. To sleep at the Blue Swallow or the Wigwam in peak season, book several weeks ahead, sometimes two months in summer.

08

Eating on the Route: the classic diners

Route 66 is also a culinary journey, state by state, in places that haven't changed in decades. Several even claim to have invented an American dish.

  • The Cozy Dog Drive In (Springfield, Illinois). This is where the corn dog, the sausage in fried cornmeal batter on a stick, was reportedly invented, in 1946. Still run by the same family, walls covered in memorabilia.
  • Chicago deep-dish pizza. The city invented thick-crust pizza in the 1940s. Every local has a favourite spot. It bakes slowly: order well ahead.
  • Ted Drewes (St. Louis). A frozen custard institution since 1929, so thick the server turns the cup upside down to prove it won't fall out.
  • The Snow Cap Drive-In (Seligman, Arizona). Opened in 1953, a temple of kitsch and gags, at the heart of the town that saved the Route.
  • Oklahoma barbecue. Briskets smoked for hours and ribs served on kraft paper, with sweet iced tea: you're in the South.
  • New Mexico green chile. They ask "Red or green?" with every order. Answer "Christmas" to get both sauces.

What these places have in common: you don't go just to eat, but for the decor, the history and the atmosphere. They're as much a part of the journey as the landscapes.

09

Route 66 and the movie Cars

If Route 66 speaks so much to children, it's largely thanks to Pixar's movie Cars. The creators drew directly on the Mother Road and its people to build Radiator Springs, the forgotten little town beside a road no one takes anymore, the heart of the film.

Several real places fed the film. The village of Seligman, in Arizona, and its barber Angel Delgadillo, who fought to save the Route, inspired the spirit and story of Radiator Springs. In Galena, Kansas, an old rusty tow truck served as the model for the tow-truck character. And the Rock Cafe in Stroud, Oklahoma, inspired one of the film's characters; burned down in 2008, it was rebuilt stone by stone by the community.

Driving Route 66 today means finding, life-size, the atmosphere of those backdrops: the neon signs, the abandoned stations, the small towns living in slow motion. For families who love the film, it's an ideal thread from one end of the trip to the other.

The checklist before you go

Route 66 takes a bit of preparation, or you're in for trouble. Here are the points that come up most often from those who've done it.

  • A GPS with the Route 66 waypoints. The historic route isn't continuous: it alternates preserved sections and bits of Interstate. Without landmarks, you miss half the iconic stops or get lost.
  • The right season. Spring (April-May) and autumn (September-October) are ideal, 15 to 25°C. Avoid summer: the Mojave and Arizona top 40°C. In winter, Illinois is harsh, snow and sub-zero temperatures.
  • Fill up before the desert. Between Needles and Barstow, stations are rare over 250 km. Fill up on fuel and water before heading into the Mojave.
  • Book the iconic motels early. Blue Swallow, Wigwam, La Posada: small capacity, high demand. Several weeks ahead, up to two months in summer.
  • Allow enough time. 3,940 km can't be done in a few days if you want to enjoy it. Count on about two weeks for a real trip, without driving all day.
  • One-way car rental. Most people pick the car up in Chicago and drop it in Los Angeles. One-way fees between two far-apart cities can be steep: compare carefully before booking.
  • The international driving permit. To drive in the United States with a foreign licence, an official translation or an international permit is often required. Plan it before departure.

On that last point, the car is the backbone of the trip: compare offers carefully, especially for a one-way Chicago-Los Angeles. You'll find the rental comparator and the detailed day-by-day itinerary at the top of this page.
