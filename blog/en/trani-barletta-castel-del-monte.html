01

From Castro to Trani, our last base

This article closes our 14-day road trip in Puglia. After six nights in Castro Marina and the Salento explored from end to end, we head back north for three days between Trani, the Adriatic coast and the hinterland.

The drive

Allow 2h30 of driving from Castro. We break the trip with a coffee stop at Specchiolla, a small seaside resort north of Brindisi. Nothing extraordinary there, but the view from the edge of the beach is worth the stop and the photo.

Driving. On stretches limited to 90, expect to be tailgated by cars doing 130. Unlike northern Italy, speed cameras are rare down south and it shows in the driving. Let them pass, don't dig your heels in.

Arriving in Trani

We booked the Borgo Beltrani Rooms, right in the centre. Parking sorts itself out easily: a car park 100 metres away, 4 € for 24 hours. That is the kind of detail that changes the end of a trip.

A classic hitch: the hotel only opens at 3pm. So we have lunch at Beltrani 29, the restaurant just below. Good surprise, we went twice during our stay.

Lunch

The setting is superb and the food lives up to it. On the menu: a huge antipasti of mussels, tomatoes and mint. Ravioli with ragù. Sea bream in a pistachio crust.

Enough to start the afternoon without rushing.

02

Trani, a first walk around

A digestivo on the harbour

Next comes a stroll along the harbour. Caffè d'orzo for Patricia, caffè corretto for me.

Caffè d'orzo is a coffee made from roasted barley, caffeine free, very common in Italy: round taste, slightly toasted, served like an espresso. Caffè corretto, literally "corrected coffee", is an espresso with a splash of spirits poured in, usually grappa. I most often take mine with sambuca, the Italian aniseed liqueur: it softens the bitterness of the coffee and rounds off the meal nicely. It is the after-dinner drink par excellence, and nobody will give you a funny look if you order one at noon.

A quick walk is enough to understand Trani. The old centre is extremely well restored, without being frozen in time. The white of the stone, on the facades and on the paving, contrasts with the blue of the sky, which in turn competes with the blue of the sea.

The parish of Santa Chiara

On the way we come across the Parrocchia Santa Chiara, away from the main tourist routes, tucked into the lanes of the old centre.

Its story is tied to the neighbouring monastery, founded by the Poor Clares in the 13th century and for a long time a real economic power in town. In 1799 the French looted it and burned the archives. The church became a parish in 1909, while the monastery ended up as barracks, then as a school.

Inside, bright and welcoming: a wooden crucifix from the 16th century and a high altar from the 18th. Next door, archaeological digs have uncovered thirteen wells that supplied medieval Trani. A short visit, but worth the detour.

The Chiesa di Santa Teresa

A little further on, right by the harbour. Door closed for us. It is the finest example of baroque in town.

The site has had several lives. Two churches came before it: San Leo, probably from the 12th century, of which only a mention in a document from 1171 survives, then San Marco, built in the 15th in honour of the patron saint of Venice, whose colony in Trani was large. The Discalced Carmelites took over the site in 1642. In the 18th, San Marco fell into ruin and it was decided to build bigger. The foundation stone was laid on 13 October 1754, but work only started in 1764: noble families nearby had appealed to the king so as not to lose their view of the harbour.

Inside, the Madonna Addolorata is venerated, and her statue is carried in procession on the night between Maundy Thursday and Good Friday. Churches in Trani are often closed outside services; visitors recommend the Christmas period, when the nativity scenes bring them back to life.

03

Trani cathedral ⭐

We go in through the lower basilica, and the shock is immediate. A forest of columns, low light, a volume you don't expect under the nave. This is the old church of Santa Maria, on which the cathedral was built: the site stacks three churches on top of each other. Then comes the crypt, and back up we go.

Our favourite ⭐
Cattedrale San Nicola Pellegrino

The cathedral is dedicated to San Nicola Pellegrino, a young Greek who died in Trani in 1094 while on pilgrimage. Miracles followed, he was canonised in 1099 and building started straight away. Building at the water's edge and keeping the foundations away from the sea was a bold gamble for the time.

The bronze doorBarisano da Trani, late 12th century
The hypogeum1 € to see the illuminated manuscripts
Trani stoneAlmost white limestone with pink glints

Up there, it is majestic. A very simple majesty, with no showing off, that reminds you how small you are. Trani stone, a local limestone that is almost white with pink glints, lights up the whole building. We take our time looking at the bronze door by Barisano da Trani, late 12th century: 32 bas-relief panels, sacred and secular scenes mixed together. The original is inside, what you see outside is a copy put in place in 2012.

Then we go back down, all the way down this time, to the hypogeum. The air is cool and it revives you: outside it is 35 degrees. For 1 €, you get to see the illuminated manuscripts.

04

The Jewish quarter, the Madonna del Carmine and the Villa Comunale

We wander through the lanes of the Jewish quarter, just behind the harbour. Narrow, winding, they run down from the cathedral towards the sea. It is the kind of place where you walk on without a map, taking corners at random. And despite the width, we still meet Fiat 500s that have managed to squeeze all the way in. We still wonder how they get back out.

The Scolanova synagogue

Another closed door, in the old Jewish quarter between the cathedral and the harbour. Visiting hours are tight, 11am to 12pm only. If you pass through Trani, plan your day around it, otherwise you will see it from outside as we did.

Its story is worth stopping for. Built in the 13th century, it was one of the four synagogues in town. Around 1380, an anti-Jewish wave saw them all turned into churches: this one became Santa Maria in Scolanova. In 1541 the Jews were expelled from the whole of southern Italy. It took until the mid-2000s for the building to be deconsecrated and given back to Jewish worship. It is now run by the Jewish community of Naples.

On the facade, a bell gable topped with a wrought iron Star of David. A bell and a star on the same building, you see that nowhere else. The street names of the quarter have kept the trace of the rest: via La Giudea, via della Giudecca, via Mosè di Trani.

The sanctuary of the Madonna del Carmine

On the other side of the harbour, on piazza Tiepolo, we visit the sanctuary of the Beata Vergine Maria del Carmine. In Trani, everyone calls it "the sailors' church".

Inside, in the right aisle, a Madonna della Fonte, a late Byzantine icon. Legend has it that she arrived in Trani on the back of a dolphin, while the bells of Holy Saturday were ringing. She is the patron saint of sailors and fishermen: her feast day, 16 July, takes over the town for several days.

Our secret spot: the Villa Comunale

Guidebooks rarely mention it, and yet this is where we feel best. The Villa Comunale, Trani's public garden, is a haven of cool under the trees when the town is baking.

You walk there to the song of the parakeets, which live here freely. Statues punctuate the paths, and from the terrace you can see the cathedral opposite, on the other side of the harbour.

The Aquae, feet in the water

For an aperitivo with your feet in the water, the Aquae sits inside the park itself, on the Lido Bella Venezia. The setting is superb, so is the price: 53 € for two sun loungers, and in the back row at that.

Two things to know: it closes at 7pm, except on Saturdays when it runs until 10.30pm, and the loungers go fast. Book ahead if you want a good spot.

Lining up Trani's three lighthouses in a single shot takes some finding of the right angle. This one cost us a few trips back and forth.

In the evening we settle a little further away, facing the sea, at Il Vecchio e il Mare (via Tiepolo, below the Fortino). A plate of antipasti, rosé wine, and the sun setting behind the cathedral, right opposite.

05

Barletta and the Cantina della Disfida

The next day we leave Trani for the northern coast. Barletta is fifteen minutes away. Centuries ago the town was a thriving city. It has kept some very fine reminders of that, and there is far more to see here than you would imagine.

Santa Maria Maggiore

First monument we visit, at the end of via Duomo. The whole town is organised around it, the streets literally turn around the building.

From outside, you can see at once that it is cut in two: the front is Romanesque, the back Gothic, and the two styles live together without getting in each other's way. Work started around 1150 and continued into the 14th century. In the time of the crusades, the church was a stop for pilgrims heading for the Holy Land, which explains its scale.

The underground level alone is worth the visit. The digs have uncovered a 6th century early Christian basilica with its polychrome mosaics, an early medieval church above it, and beneath it all tombs cut into the rock dating from before the Christian era.

Good to know. Only one underground level can be visited, the rest you have to imagine. Inside, don't miss the 12th century ciborium, resting on four small columns whose capitals were carved by craftsmen who came from the East.

The castle

Coming out of the cathedral, you run into the castle, right next door, near the harbour. Massive, square, in pale stone, surrounded by a deep moat and wide gardens that soften the whole thing.

Its present look owes a lot to Charles V. It now houses the municipal library and the civic museum, home to the only known bust of Frederick II and the Sarcophagus of the Apostles, a witness to local Christianity in the 3rd century.

A lesser known detail: the Templars arrested in 1308 were held in its underground rooms before being tried in Brindisi. Visits open at 10am. As for us, we head off to the Colossus.

The Colossus

The statue, more than five metres tall, is impressive. It stands in the middle of a shopping avenue, corso Vittorio Emanuele, in front of the basilica of the Santo Sepolcro. It is a unique bronze: the only colossal statue in that material displayed in the open air in its original version, with no glass case and no museum.

Its identity has been debated for centuries. For a long time it was thought to be the emperor Heraclius. The theory accepted today is more down to earth: during digs in Ravenna in 1231-1232, Frederick II, a collector of antiquities, is said to have had the statue shipped to Puglia. It may have been meant for Foggia, Lucera or Melfi; it stayed here. In 1491 it was planted in front of the basilica, and has not moved since.

A cruel detail: its legs were remade in the Middle Ages, the originals having been melted down to cast two bells.

A tip in passing, useful anywhere you travel. On oneroadtrip.com from your phone, go to the Google Maps tab above the map. There you will find keywords for the things to see. Click the one you are interested in, then the "go there" arrow: Google Maps opens and shows you the route. That is how we found the Colossus.

The basilica of the Santo Sepolcro

Behind the statue. Mass was under way when we passed, so we slipped in quietly. The church owes everything to the crusades: Barletta stood at the crossing of the Adriatic route and the road to Canosa, and its port served as an embarkation point for Jerusalem. A hospital for pilgrims was attached to the building. In 1291, after the fall of Acre and the failure of the last crusade, the patriarch of Jerusalem took refuge there with relics, including a silver stauroteca containing a fragment of the True Cross. The basilica treasury still keeps it.

The Cantina della Disfida ⭐

The Cantina della Disfida

600 metres away, on piazza della Sfida. Self-guided visit, open from 10am. Absolutely not to be missed, as much for the history as for the place itself. The cantina is magnificent, set in the cellars of a 14th century building. At the entrance, an old wrought iron sign announces the Antica Taverna del Sole, the tavern of the sun, so named because the light came in from dawn to dusk.

In 1503, Spanish and French were fighting over southern Italy. Barletta was occupied by the Spanish, who captured a group of Frenchmen including captain Charles de La Motte. A banquet was held in their honour, right here. With Puglian wine helping things along, La Motte let slip that Italian soldiers were cowards and good-for-nothings. The Italian captain Ettore Fieramosca took up the challenge. On 13 February 1503, thirteen Italian knights faced thirteen Frenchmen out in the countryside, between Corato and Andria. The Italians won. The episode became one of the founding stories of Italian national feeling.

Today it is a museum. Tables, benches, stools and tankards recreate the banqueting hall. On the walls, the coats of arms of the twenty-six knights, full suits of armour, Spanish flags, wrought iron lanterns. The site was listed as a national monument in 1937 and inaugurated on 13 February 1938, the anniversary of the fight.

As for the flooded room at the back, we never found out what it was originally for. Nobody there could tell us either. If you know, write to us.

06

Canosa, Andria and the beach

Canosa di Puglia, too hot and pretty dull

The town badly lacks charm, and lacks signposting for what there is to see. Only stop here if archaeological parks interest you. There are two of them.

  • The San Leucio park. On a hill south-east of town, among the olive trees. In 318 BC, the most imposing Italic temple in southern Italy was built here, dedicated to Minerva, to seal the alliance between local princes and Rome. Once the temple had been destroyed, from the late 5th century onwards its stones were reused to raise an early Christian basilica on a central plan, the largest in Puglia. Free entry, guided tours for a fee, antiquarium open since 2008.
  • The San Giovanni park. Right in town. It takes its name from the San Giovanni baptistery, from the 6th century, attributed to Saint Sabinus. It is part of a Christian complex with the basilica of the Saviour and that of Santa Maria.

As for us, it was too hot at midday to venture in. We left without regrets.

Andria, everything closed

Next stop. The old town is a little prettier than Canosa, but everything is closed. The ducal palace is under repair, the cathedral shut, and we go round in circles for a while before finding somewhere to eat. We end up in a nice bar, the Gusto. It is the only one open, but honestly we ate well for very little. And the waitresses are lovely.

We call it a day there. This part of the region lacks charm compared with everything we had done before. Tomorrow we will try west of Trani. This afternoon, it will be the beach.

Off to the sea. We end the day at Beach Dunes, at Margherita di Savoia. Fine sand, a gentle slope into the water, comfortable loungers and a proper restaurant on site. After a morning of stone and heat, it was exactly what we needed.

07

Altamura and its bakery

The next day we head west, towards Altamura. Surprise on the way: Matera is very close, we hadn't realised. The old town is charming, we walk right across it to the cathedral.

Palatine basilica
Cattedrale Santa Maria Assunta

It was Frederick II who wanted it, between 1232 and 1254. He had it built as a palatine basilica, meaning outside the authority of the bishops: it answered only to the king and the pope. There are only four with that status in Puglia.

The earthquake of 1316 brought down a large part of the building, rebuilt under Robert of Anjou. On that occasion, the facade was moved outright from west to east.

The doorwayResting on two lions remade in 1533
The archesTwenty-two scenes from the life of Christ
The MUDIMA5 € entry, diocesan museum

So what you see today mostly dates from the 14th: the rose window, a masterpiece of Puglian sculpture with its fifteen radiating colonnettes, and the doorway, a flood of carvings resting on two lions remade in 1533. On the arches, twenty-two scenes from the life of Christ, from the Annunciation to Pentecost.

Going upstairs: the matronei

A rare thing: you can go upstairs and see the nave from above. These galleries are called the matronei, from the Latin matrona: they once held the women. They run above the aisles, on both sides, with pointed vaults and openings that look inwards on one side and outwards on the other.

It is the most genuinely medieval part of the building, and since 2016 it has housed the MUDIMA, the diocesan museum: goldwork, liturgical ornaments, stone and wood carvings, archives, from the 14th to the 19th century. The visit ends at the bishop's palace, in the Hall of Coats of Arms. Allow 5 € entry.

The town is also home to the Altamura Man, a Neanderthal skeleton found in a nearby cave, but we didn't see it.

Antico Forno Santa Caterina

We walk down the street to the end, and on the way back to the car we stop at the Antico Forno Santa Caterina. Everything looks tempting. The oven gives off enormous heat, the focaccia bake in it and sell like hot cakes, which is exactly the phrase.

Our haul: half a loaf at 1.90 €, a packet of taralli and some frise, 12 € in all. The bread is delicious. As for the frise, we thought they were sweet pastries, they are hard breads. Not our thing.

08

Gravina in Puglia ⭐

Plenty to see here. We start at the tourist office and ask them to stick a OneRoadTrip sticker up. Let us know if you spot it.

Reminder: everything closes at 1pm. Plan your morning accordingly, or you will find yourself standing in front of closed doors.

The gravine are the limestone gashes that cut through the landscape: the town hangs above one of them. A stronghold of the Orsini from 1423 to 1810, it saw the birth of the future pope Benedict XIII.

The ducal chapel

The church of Santa Maria del Suffragio, known as the church of Purgatory, built between 1649 and 1654 at the expense of the dukes of Gravina, the parents of the future pope. Hence its nickname, the ducal chapel.

On the doorway, two reclining skeletons on the tympanum, a daily reminder of death. Inside, a single nave, seven altars, and the tomb of Ferdinando III Orsini, who died of the plague in Naples in 1658. The church is now deconsecrated and used for exhibitions.

Opposite the ducal chapel, the Finya library, one of the oldest in Puglia, with more than eight thousand volumes.

The cathedral

A minor basilica dedicated to Santa Maria Assunta, built in the 11th century and enlarged in the 15th after a fire. Three naves separated by columns whose capitals are all different, and polychrome marble altars from the early 18th.

From the forecourt, the view stretches to the aqueduct bridge that spans the ravine. Gorge scenery, houses carved into the tufa: you could almost be in Matera. Beneath the cathedral, a crypt worth going down for: a Crucifixion at the far end, a Virgin, and a fair few 17th century objects.

The Casa della Cola Cola

Right opposite the cathedral, five small rooms at the top of a steep staircase. The cola cola is a terracotta whistle shaped like a bird, painted in the colours of the town, of which it is the symbol. The smaller it is, the higher and more deafening it sounds.

It is the house of the Loglisci family, who have been making them for generations. The son shows you round, in English and in French. 2.50 € entry, free for under-13s. Early 20th century atmosphere: black and white photographs, dark furniture, flowered floors, and a whole room overflowing with brightly coloured terracotta. You can buy some on the spot, and the place is set in the old Dominican nuns' monastery, built in 1677 by the mother of Benedict XIII.

We eat at Mamma Mia, which overlooks the caves, and it is good. A plunging view over the ravine and the dwellings dug into the tufa, often quite dilapidated.

The aqueduct bridge

We cross it on foot to reach the viewpoint on the other side. It is very beautiful: 37 metres high, 90 long, 5.5 wide, in local tufa, with its twenty-five arches on two levels.

Originally it was a simple bridge, built so that the faithful could cross the torrent and reach the rock sanctuary of the Madonna della Stella, which gave it its name. An earthquake brought it down in 1722. The Orsini then rebuilt it between 1743 and 1778, taking the opportunity to turn it into an aqueduct and bring water from the Sant'Angelo and San Giacomo springs into town. Both fountains are still there, one at each end.

You may already have seen it on screen without knowing: it appears in the 2021 James Bond, No Time to Die, where the editing passes it off as a bridge in Matera.

After a few good photos, we come back towards the cathedral and leave through the Four Fountains quarter, the 1778 monument built to bring water into town. There was a liqueur seller, closed as well. The museums and the other churches would reopen later in the afternoon.

09

Castel del Monte, on the way back from Gravina

The most puzzling monument in Puglia ⭐
Castel del Monte

Perched on an isolated height in the Murge, visible from far away. A UNESCO World Heritage site since 1996, and engraved on the back of the one-cent euro coin.

It was commissioned around 1240 by Frederick II of Swabia, the man they called Stupor Mundi, the wonder of the world. An emperor who spoke six languages, passionate about mathematics, astronomy and philosophy, and who welcomed Greek, Arab, Italian and Jewish scholars to his court. Many believe he drew up the plan himself.

The number eightEight towers, eight facades, eight rooms per floor
Car park5 €, down below, plus 2 € for the bus
Entry10 € per person, 7 € more for the guided tour
PricesNoted in August 2026, they may change

Everything here revolves around the number eight: eight towers, eight facades, eight rooms on the ground floor, eight upstairs, around an octagonal courtyard. The orientation produces precise plays of light at the solstices and equinoxes.

And nobody knows what it was for. No moat, no drawbridge, no stables. Hunting lodge, astronomical observatory, calendar in stone, imperial crown, school of philosophy, esoteric temple: the theories have been piling up for eight centuries without any of them winning out. Frederick II probably never lived there.

What followed is less glorious. Abandoned for a long time, stripped of its marble, it served as a prison and then as shelter for shepherds. The Italian state bought it back in 1876 for 25,000 lire.

Getting there, in practice. You cannot drive up. Car park down below at 5 €, bus tickets to be bought at the souvenir shop, 2 €. The shuttle is erratic: it arrives, it leaves, it keeps you waiting. You can walk up, but twenty minutes in 38 degrees cooled our enthusiasm. Sadly not our body temperature.

Once up there, the reward is not only in the walls: the view stretches over the surrounding plains and all the way to the sea.

10

Last day: from Bisceglie to Bari

Last day in Puglia and in Italy. We head back up the coast to Bari, with a 10pm flight. Plenty of time.

The Chianca dolmen

First visit, a few kilometres from the centre of Bisceglie. It is the best known of Italy's prehistoric monuments, and one of the best preserved in the country.

Three large limestone slabs set upright hold up a fourth, 3.85 metres long, which forms the roof. The whole thing makes a square burial chamber about 1.80 m high, extended by a 7.60 metre entrance corridor, the dromos. Originally the whole structure was covered by a mound.

Human skeletons, pottery and charred animal bones were found here. On the left-hand slab, two small openings, thought to have been used to drain the blood of sacrificial victims.

On the dating, sources differ: some place it in the Neolithic, around 4000-3000 BC, others in the Bronze Age, in the 2nd millennium.

We are the only tourists here. The dolmen rarely appears in guidebooks. Splendid olive trees surround it, and the silence does a lot for the place.

Bisceglie

We park on the main square and walk up to the cathedral, along a street lined by a noble palace.

The co-cathedral of San Pietro Apostolo was begun in 1073 by Peter II, Norman count of Trani, and completed in 1295, the year of its consecration. Puglian Romanesque, three naves, a transept over a crypt, and a fine projecting porch above the main entrance.

Two things not to miss: the choir in solid walnut, whose carved stalls come from the sanctuary of Santa Maria dei Miracoli in Andria, and the crypt, which holds the relics of the town's three protector saints, Maurus, Sergius and Pantaleon, found here in 1167. As in Altamura, you can go up into the matronei and look down on the nave. The climb is not free.

Otherwise, Bisceglie is a place you visit for its atmosphere rather than its monuments. Very shaded lanes, and the daily life of people heading to the market.

Molfetta

A fine little fishing port, with its fish market watched over by the old Italian men who have been sitting there forever. Fish rules here, it is cut up on the spot and you can smell it. Patricia preferred to flee.

We head for the old town and its Duomo Vecchio, the cathedral of San Corrado, recognisable by the two bell towers framing the facade. Built between the 12th and the 13th century, it sits at the water's edge, on the tip of the islet where the original town settled. Its three domes lined up along the nave are a rare signature in Puglia. The beach runs right alongside it: you go from old stones to sand in a few steps.

At 11.45am, the doors of the Duomo Nuovo are closed. Opening hours in Puglia remain a mystery. We carry on through the old town.

Verdict. Molfetta is worth a look, but no more than that. It is no match for Trani. Do it on the way back to Bari, as we did.

Giovinazzo ⭐

We carry on to Giovinazzo and its lungomare, the promenade that runs along the sea below the old town. The historic centre sits on a small headland, and you can walk right round it from the outside, level with the water.

After lunch we wander through the pretty streets of the town. We are alone. We wonder where the tourists who are supposed to be flooding Puglia have gone.

We know perfectly well the cathedral will be closed at 2pm, but strolling in the shade through empty lanes, without a sound, without being pushed along by a crowd, is easily worth one more visit.

We come out at the harbour, its blue and its boats. That is when we tell ourselves it would be good to live around here, in one of these little white houses in the shade, with a terrace opening onto the sea or the harbour.

In this kind of small town, it is not so much what there is to see that counts, it is the atmosphere. We promise ourselves we will come back in winter to see what it is like. Giovinazzo is close to Bari and its airport, and it is the kind of place that deserves some lingering, especially knowing that Italy is trying to attract future retirees to towns in the south.

Bari

We park near piazza Federico II di Svevia, at the foot of the castle, and walk into the old town. The tourists we had been looking for for three days: they are here.

The cathedral of San Sabino can be visited, but entry costs. We have seen so many that we give this one a miss. From outside, it looks beautiful.

For those who do stop: it was founded in 1034 and completed in 1064, when the episcopal see of Canosa was transferred to Bari. The present building dates from the 12th and 13th centuries, Puglian Romanesque, in pale limestone, with a rose window and a doorway topped by two Eastern saints. The 18th century had dressed it in baroque, the restorations of the 1950s brought it back to its Romanesque form.

Two things are worth going down for. The crypt first, which holds the icon of the Virgin Odegitria and the relics of Saint Sabinus, bishop of Canosa and the town's first patron saint. Then the succorpo, an underground level where the remains of the old Byzantine church sit alongside Roman ruins, mosaics and fragments of frescoes included. Allow 3 € for that one. A detail not to miss outside: the trulla, the old 12th century baptistery, later turned into a sacristy, with its twelve-sided plan.

We reach piazza Mercantile. This was the most important square in Bari during the Renaissance: the public market, the arsenal, the customs house and the Palazzo del Sedile, seat of the council of nobles, whose clock tower set the rhythm of town life.

In the centre, a white marble column topped with a sphere, with a stone lion lying at its foot: the column of Justice, which the people of Bari call the infamous column. It was raised around 1546 by the Spanish viceroy Pedro de Toledo, and served as a pillory. Insolvent debtors and bankrupts were tied to it, sitting astride the lion, hands chained to the column, backside bare. The punishment was not aimed at pain but at reputation: in an economy built on a man's word, being displayed there amounted to social death.

The lion itself is far older than the column. It is a reused piece from a Roman tomb, dated between the 1st century BC and the 1st century AD, one of the few Roman traces in the town. On its collar, two Latin words: Custos Iusticie, guardian of justice. Opposite, the Pigna fountain, one of the oldest in Bari.

Before setting off again, we go shopping in the Murat quarter, the 19th century town laid out on a grid, with its right-angled streets and its shopfronts along corso Cavour and via Sparano.

We come back along the seafront promenade, then head for the airport.

Verdict on Bari. Very good, but the small towns we did all along the coast are every bit as worthwhile. Two to three hours are enough to soak up each one.

We get home to Nice at 2am, shattered but happy to find Chiara and Elfie making a fuss of us. They are our cats. The next day, we start planning the next holiday, probably a Guatemala and Belize combination.
