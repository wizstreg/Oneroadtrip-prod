01

Castro Marina, our base in Salento

This article is part of our 14-day road trip through Puglia. If you haven't read the beginning, start with the first article, which covers the Alberobello area over 4 days.

Read part one: 4 days around Alberobello →

For this second stretch of the trip we chose Castro Marina as our base. It's a coastal village with the double advantage of rocky beaches for swimming and a lively old town up the hill where you can go for dinner. From Castro you fan out to the Salento sights each day. All of them are under an hour away. We didn't regret the choice: Castro is charming and lively, and we got two very different moods out of it.

Where we stayed
Hotel degli Ulivi, Castro Marina

It isn't the newest hotel in the area, but we'd strongly recommend it. The rooms are large and functional, and you look out over the whole bay from a big terrace with sun loungers.

  • The view. You wake up to a superb panorama over the bay.
  • The hosts. Genuinely lovely.
  • The location. Central, a 10-minute walk from the beaches and the centre.
  • Breakfast. Substantial, and pleasant on the covered terrace.
  • The price. As charming as the hosts.
  • Parking. Plenty of spaces, and that's worth its weight in gold in Puglia.

A practical note for the whole of southern Puglia. Many monuments, museums and sites close between 1pm and 4pm. Churches keep even more unpredictable hours: even outside the break, you'll often find them shut. Plan your visits accordingly, and keep the afternoon for the beach or a coffee.

02

Brindisi and the end of the Via Appia

Coming down from Alberobello, we chose to stop at Brindisi rather than Lecce, which we'll cover in a future article.

Brindisi is best done on foot. Walk down the Corso Giuseppe Garibaldi for a bit of shopping, then turn left at the end of the avenue to follow the harbour.

You arrive in front of the Roman columns, at the top of the Scalinata Virgiliana, Virgil's staircase.

The city's symbol
The Roman columns

These columns once marked the end point of the Via Appia, the legendary road that linked Rome to Brindisi over nearly 600 km. Originally there were two, twins, standing almost 19 metres tall. One collapsed in 1528: its capital was given to Lecce, where it now sits on the column in Piazza Sant'Oronzo. The surviving one looks over the harbour with its Corinthian capital.

Original heightAround 19 metres
Collapse1528, capital given to Lecce
VirgilSaid to have died here in 19 BC

This is also where Virgil is said to have died in 19 BC, on his way back from Greece. A commemorative plaque marks it.

Piazza del Duomo

Carry on to Piazza del Duomo. The cathedral, consecrated in 1089, was almost entirely destroyed by the 1743 earthquake then rebuilt in baroque style. Inside you can still see fragments of the original mosaic. Opposite, the Francesco Ribezzo Provincial Archaeological Museum traces the history of Brindisi from prehistory to Roman times. Don't miss the Bronzes of Punta del Serrone, Greek sculptures recovered from the sea in 1992.

Palazzo Granafei-Nervegna

Carrying on to the left, you reach Palazzo Granafei-Nervegna. Not much of it is left but you can walk freely into the building housing the ruins. This is where the capital of the second, collapsed Roman column is displayed. Then head back towards the cathedral square.

We move on to the Temple of San Giovanni al Sepolcro, a circular Romanesque church from the 11th century, built by the Crusaders on the model of the Holy Sepulchre in Jerusalem. But careful: closed on Wednesdays. It was Wednesday.

Keep wandering around the small city. There are churches everywhere, sadly often closed, as is the case throughout Puglia.

Our take. Brindisi is a historic, non-touristy city. We were the only tourists walking around. In the evening the promenade must be nicer and the city livelier. It's more a city to live in than to visit.

03

Castro Marina: the beach and the old town at night

We reach Castro in the late afternoon. First thing we do: the beach.

Castro beach

Don't expect fine sand, it's rock here. There are private beaches with loungers and parasols, budget around €30 for the day, but by 4pm they're taken. We do what everyone does: go down the steps, put the towel on the rocks and jump in. The water is crystal clear.

The old town, in the evening

In the evening we head up into the old town. It's charming. Restaurants line the lanes one after another, and the viewpoint gives a superb outlook over the bay.

The medieval castle has been turned into a museum. We ate at the highest-rated restaurant in town, a meat specialist but with vegetarian options.

Sacra Textile, the good surprise

Below the cathedral, the church of SS. Annunziata, don't miss the Sacra Textile exhibition: a small vaulted room displaying extraordinary ecclesiastical garments on mannequins. Chasubles, dalmatics, albs and stoles, in every liturgical colour.

Each piece comes with an explanatory panel. It's unexpected and genuinely beautiful.

04

The Grotta della Poesia, Salento's natural pool

Heading north out of Castro, you follow the coast for 45 minutes to Roca Vecchia, in the municipality of Melendugno, between San Foca and Torre dell'Orso. Large car park, take the right towards the cave. Entry to the archaeological park and the cave: €5 per person in July 2026.

The landscapes are majestic: limestone cliffs, intensely blue sea. The cave itself is magnificent, one of the most photographed spots in Italy.

Unmissable ⭐
The Poesia Grande

There are actually two caves. The Poesia Grande is the one you come to see: a natural pool roughly 30 by 18 metres, carved out of the limestone by the sea over centuries. Its roof collapsed after antiquity, turning the cave into an open-air basin.

You admire it from the cliff edge: the water is an unreal turquoise, the walls drop sheer to around five metres deep.

DimensionsRoughly 30 by 18 metres
DepthAround 5 metres
SwimmingBanned since 2019, the rock is porous
Price€5 per person, archaeological park included

The Poesia Piccola, the real treasure

About sixty metres away, the Poesia Piccola is fenced off and inaccessible. Yet it's the real archaeological treasure: in 1983, archaeologist Cosimo Pagliara found inscriptions on its walls, across more than 600 m², in Messapian, Greek and Latin. Sailors carved prayers there to the god Thaotor, "the protector", before crossing to Greece and Albania. It's one of the most important inscription sanctuaries in the ancient Mediterranean.

The name "Poesia" probably comes from medieval Greek posis, a source of drinking water. The romantic version has a princess bathing there, inspiring the region's poets.

The Roca Vecchia archaeological park, right next door, includes the ruins of a medieval coastal city.

05

The Baia dei Turchi, the finest beach of the trip

On the way to Otranto, a stop at the Baia dei Turchi, the bay of the Turks. The name recalls the Ottoman landing of 1480, when 18,000 Turkish soldiers came ashore on this beach before marching on Otranto and massacring 800 inhabitants who refused to convert. Today the mood is considerably more peaceful.

The beach lies at the end of a path through the pine wood. It's a lovely stretch of fine sand, a change from the rocks of Castro. A large car park takes the many visitors.

We found lettini for €35 for the pair. You can stand a long way out. We lazed and swam for an hour and a half.

On the way back there's somewhere to rinse off at the car park before setting off again. A small thing, but it counts when you're doing beach and sightseeing in the same day.

06

Otranto, gateway to the East

Unmissable ⭐
Otranto

Otranto is the easternmost point of mainland Italy. It's nicknamed the "gateway to the East", and you can feel it: Byzantine, Arab and Norman influences mingle everywhere. It's also the town that inspired Horace Walpole's The Castle of Otranto, the first English gothic novel, in 1764.

ParkingAt the entrance to town, then on foot
EntrancePorta Alfonsina and Torre Alfonsina, 15th century
Time neededA full day, easily
Worth tastingPasticciotti, warm for preference

You park at the entrance to town and follow the seafront, lined with restaurants. The Lungomare degli Eroi is a broad esplanade that acts as a balcony over the sea and gives access to the old town.

The old town

You enter through the Porta Alfonsina, also called Porta Terra, a monumental gate from the 15th century, followed by the imposing Torre Alfonsina. The lanes are full of shops: bags, shoes, costume jewellery, wine and olive oil boutiques, and above all the bakeries selling pasticciotti, the Salento speciality. A small oval shortcrust pastry filled with confectioner's custard, invented in 1745 in Galatina. You eat them warm. We must have eaten a lot, a lot, a lot of them over the trip.

The collapsed church

You carry on to the far end of the old town and come across the ruins of theEx Chiesa dell'Immacolata, an 18th century church that collapsed in 1970 after heavy rain. Baroque altars remain, open to the sky.

A wooden footbridge, the Ponte dell'Immacolata, spans the ruins and leads to the Torre Matta.

The ramparts and the Aragonese Castle

Taking the left of the church, you climb onto the ramparts. The view over the harbour is superb. You cross the footbridge to continue along the walls to the Aragonese Castle. Built in the 15th century under Ferdinand I of Aragon, the castle can be visited for €12. Inside: permanent exhibitions on the history of Otranto and on prehistory, temporary shows, and above all the panoramic terrace at the top with views over the whole bay. Opinions are divided on the interior, some rooms are rather bare, but the view is worth the detour.

Practical note. As everywhere in Puglia, many monuments close between 1pm and 4pm. We found ourselves outside a closed cathedral. Use it as an excuse for a break.

Our coffee tip

L'Ortale Otranto, a grocery with a small shaded garden near the cathedral. Perfect for waiting out the afternoon closure in peace.

It was our secret corner. With, of course, a pasticciotto eaten in the shade.

The cathedral of Santa Maria Annunziata ⭐

Impressive. Built by the Normans in the 11th century, it has been reworked several times but keeps all its power.

The floor is entirely covered by a monumental mosaic made between 1163 and 1165 by the monk Pantaleone. 54 metres long, 28 wide, nearly 600,000 limestone tesserae. An immense Tree of Life, carried by two elephants, fills the whole central nave. Around its branches it's a genuine medieval picture book: biblical scenes (Adam and Eve, Cain and Abel, Noah's Ark, the Tower of Babel), mythological figures (Alexander the Great drawn by two griffins), the zodiac, fantastical animals, and even King Arthur astride a goat fighting a monster. The further you go towards the back of the nave, the more esoteric the imagery becomes.

Look up: the blue and gold coffered ceiling dates from the 17th century.

Behind the altar, the Chapel of the Martyrs holds the bones of the 800 inhabitants of Otranto beheaded by the Ottomans on 14 August 1480 for refusing to convert. They were canonised by Pope Francis in 2013. The stone slab on which they were executed is displayed behind a grille.

The crypt is a spectacle in itself: 42 columns, all different, in marble, porphyry and granite, with Greek, Roman, Byzantine and Arab capitals. It dates from the 11th century. It's often compared to a miniature version of the Great Mosque of Cordoba.

The Basilica of San Pietro

Hidden in the lanes of the old town, this small Byzantine church from the 9th and 10th centuries is one of the finest examples of Byzantine art in Puglia. Greek cross plan, old frescoes in ochre, blue and red tones. The best preserved are The Washing of the Feet and The Last Supper, from the late 9th century. Above the altar arch, an Arabic inscription, a trace of the different civilisations that crossed paths here. Entry €5, diocesan museum included. Mind the hours: 10am-1pm then 4.30pm-7.30pm in summer.

We weren't expecting such richness in a town this size. Otranto easily deserves a full day.

07

Santa Cesarea Terme and its clifftop villas

On the way back to Castro you follow the coast road along the sea. The landscapes are superb: cliffs dropping into deep blue, sea as far as you can see. We stop at Santa Cesarea Terme to walk the promenade overlooking the Adriatic.

Santa Cesarea is a spa town perched on the cliffs, known since the 16th century for its sulphurous waters. Four natural caves, Gattulla, Fetida, Solfurea and Solfatara, feed springs at around 30 °C, reputed to treat skin problems and rheumatism.

Local legend tells of a young woman from Otranto who, fleeing her violent father, took refuge in one of these caves. Her father is said to have pursued her there before being swallowed by flames and the sea. Ever since, the waters have come up laden with sulphur.

What strikes you on arrival are the 19th century villas planted on the cliffs.

The most spectacular is the Villa Sticchi, built between 1894 and 1900 by the engineer Pasquale Ruggieri, who had travelled in Egypt. With its purple dome, horseshoe arches and twisted colonnettes in Lecce stone, it looks like a small mosque set down on the Adriatic. The villa is private and cannot be visited, but the façade is worth the stop. Right next door, the Villa Raffaella, commissioned around 1850 by Baroness Lubelli, is plainer but just as elegant.
