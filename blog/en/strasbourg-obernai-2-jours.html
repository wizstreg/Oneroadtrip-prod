Day 1: Vauban and Petite France

The Vauban Dam

We started with the Vauban Dam (barrage Vauban), at the entrance to Petite France.

A bit of history. In 1681, Strasbourg became part of the kingdom of France. Vauban, Louis XIV's military engineer, drew up the plans for this lock bridge, built by his colleague Jacques Tarade at the end of the 17th century. Its job: in case of attack, close the sluice gates to raise the water and flood the south of the city. After 1870, the Germans added an extra floor.

Since 1966, its roof has been a panoramic terrace open to the public. You go up by an inside staircase, and there is a lift for people with reduced mobility. From the top, the view takes in the Ponts Couverts and their medieval towers, the rooftops of Petite France and the cathedral spire in the distance.

Terrace opening hours. 8 am to 7 pm in September and October, 8:30 am to 4 pm from November to February.

Must-see ⭐
Petite France

Petite France is Strasbourg's postcard picture. Half-timbered houses, canals, flower-covered bridges: it's all here, at the western tip of the Grande Île, the historic heart listed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site since 1988.

In autumn, the trees along the canals take on beautiful colors. It's probably the best time for photos.

What you need to know

Here, the Ill splits into several channels. In the Middle Ages, this corner lived off the water: it was the district of fishermen, millers and tanners. The street names still remind you of it, like rue des Moulins (Mills Street) or rue du Bain-aux-Plantes.

The name has nothing romantic about it. In the 16th century, syphilis patients were treated here in a hospice. At the time, the disease had a nickname: the "French disease". The hospice became "la Petite France", and the name then spread to the whole district. Why here? Because the tanners' quarter already smelled bad, and it was a handy place to isolate whatever people wanted to keep out of the way.

What you'll see

  • The tanners' houses, with their very steep roofs and open lofts: that's where the hides were hung to dry.
  • The Maison des Tanneurs, built in 1572, now a restaurant.
  • The pont du Faisan, a swing bridge that pivots to let boats through.
  • The Ponts Couverts, a former 13th-century defense system, with their watchtowers.
  • Place Benjamin-Zix, in the middle of the district.

The Museum of Modern and Contemporary Art

After Petite France, we crossed the Ill to the Museum of Modern and Contemporary Art, the MAMCS, right opposite the Ponts Couverts and the Vauban Dam.

The building itself catches the eye. Opened in 1998, it was designed by Adrien Fainsilber, the architect of the Cité des sciences in Paris. All glass and metal, it is laid out around a large, bright nave that leads to the galleries.

The collections cover the period from 1870 to the present day. You'll come across a Thinker by Rodin, a whole room devoted to Gustave Doré, a son of Strasbourg, Impressionists, and several works by Jean Arp, another Strasbourg native. The museum also shows works by Picasso, Monet and Kandinsky, including the Music Salon he designed in 1931.

Good to know. The museum has a café-restaurant, the Art Café, with a view over the city.

Dinner at Chez Yvonne

Back to the hotel for a short rest, then dinner at Chez Yvonne, 10 rue du Sanglier, a stone's throw from the cathedral.

It's an institution. This winstub, the traditional Alsatian tavern, has existed since 1873 under the name S'Burjerstuewel. Yvonne Haller took it over in 1956 and gave it her name. Jacques Chirac and Helmut Kohl have dined there. Wood everywhere, bench seats, a warm atmosphere.

Our take. It's not what we liked best. A bit heavy, perhaps. But if you want to see a historic winstub, this is the place. Remember to book.

03

Day 2: from Saint-Paul to the Orangerie

Breakfast at Coffee Stub

The next morning, we headed to Coffee Stub, 12 quai des Pêcheurs, on the banks of the Ill in the Krutenau district. Great breakfasts.

Heads up. It only opens around 8:30 am. No point getting there earlier.

To get there, we walked through pretty streets lined with superb buildings. We passed the Christian pastry shop on rue de l'Outre. Its trompe-l'œil painted facade, on an 18th-century house, is worth the stop on its own. It is repainted every year for the holidays. The business was founded in 1962 and has a second shop on rue Mercière, at the foot of the cathedral.

Saint-Paul church

The café is right next to Saint-Paul church. It's closed in the morning, but from the outside it's impressive. Many visitors actually mistake it for the cathedral.

It was built between 1892 and 1897, when Strasbourg was German, for the city's Protestant garrison. Its two spires rise to 76 meters. It has sixteen doors, one for each military unit stationed in Strasbourg at the time. It stands where two waterways meet, the Ill and the Aar.

Arte's headquarters

After breakfast, off to Orangerie park. We followed the Ill toward the European district. Along the way, we passed Arte's headquarters, 4 quai du Chanoine-Winterer, on the banks of the Ill.

Arte is the Franco-German cultural TV channel. Its name comes from "Association relative à la télévision européenne" (European television association). The treaty founding it was signed on October 2, 1990, the day before German reunification. The channel was created in Strasbourg on April 30, 1991 and broadcast its first evening on May 30, 1992, live from the Strasbourg Opera. It rests on two pillars funded equally, Arte France and Arte Deutschland, and produces its programs in French and German.

The current building has housed all the teams since the early 2000s. The location wasn't chosen at random: right in the European district, it shows off the channel's European calling. The building itself is the result of a collaboration between French and German architects. About 500 people work there every day.

The European Parliament

A little further on, we reached the European Parliament, the Louise Weiss building, on the banks of the Ill.

Our take. It disappointed us. After the half-timbered houses of Petite France and the pink sandstone of the churches, this big, very modern complex of glass and steel feels out of place. Compared with the rest of the city, it lacks charm.

As for its history, it was inaugurated in December 1999 by Jacques Chirac. It is named after Louise Weiss, a journalist and campaigner for women's right to vote, who was the oldest member of the first directly elected European Parliament, in 1979. Its 60-meter tower has a top that looks unfinished: that's deliberate, to suggest a Europe that is still being built.

Strasbourg is the Parliament's official seat. MEPs from the 27 countries meet here twelve times a year, for four-day sessions. The building can be visited with an audio guide; allow about an hour. Bring ID, as there are security checks at the entrance.

Our favorite ⭐
Orangerie park

Just opposite, we finally entered the park. It's really nice, especially in autumn. The trees are changing color, but they still have their leaves. And there are few people around. We were glad we came at this time of year.

At 26 hectares, it's the largest and oldest park in Strasbourg. A persistent legend credits it to Le Nôtre, the gardener of Versailles, but the city's historians doubt it.

In the middle stands the Joséphine pavilion. It was built between 1804 and 1807 to house about 140 orange trees seized from the château de Bouxwiller during the Revolution. That's where the park's name comes from. The pavilion is named after Empress Joséphine, Napoleon's first wife, in memory of her stays in Strasbourg. Destroyed by fire in 1968, it was rebuilt identically, but the orange trees were never replaced. Today it hosts exhibitions.

In 1895, a large exhibition doubled the size of the park. The lake was dug, the Buerehiesel was built, a half-timbered house that became a gourmet restaurant, and a small zoo was opened.

We also spotted a building topped with a very fine nest, probably a stork's. No surprise there: the stork is the emblem of Alsace, and the park is home to a center for reintroducing the species.

We left by way of the lake, dug for the 1895 exhibition. Its waterfall and its geyser-like fountain are worth the detour. And all around, those trees in their autumn colors.

04

Carré d'Or, the cathedral and sweet treats

We walked back down to the Carré d'Or, the shopping district at the foot of the cathedral, between the cathedral and Place Kléber and Place Broglie.

We went along rue des Frères. Quite a few shops, but all closed. Most only open at 1 or 2 pm.

Tip. If you're planning to go shopping, keep it for the afternoon.

Must-see ⭐
Notre-Dame cathedral

We came out in front of the cathedral. We decided to come back around noon for the visit: at 12:30 pm, the automata of the astronomical clock spring into action.

In the meantime, we admired it from the outside. It's built entirely of pink sandstone from the Vosges, and its facade is carved like lace. Victor Hugo described it as a "marvel of the gigantic and the delicate".

Its spire rises to 142 meters. Completed in 1439, it made the cathedral the tallest building in the world for centuries. It is still the second-tallest cathedral in France today, behind Rouen. You'll notice there is only one spire: the second was never built. In its place, a platform 66 meters up, reached by 330 steps, offers a view over the whole city.

  • Platform, April 1 to September 30. 9:30 am to 1 pm and 1:30 pm to 8 pm, last climb at 7:15 pm.
  • Platform, October 1 to March 31. 10 am to 1 pm and 1:30 pm to 6 pm, last climb at 5:15 pm.
  • Prices. €10, or €6 reduced rate (ages 6 to 18, students, with proof). Free for under-6s. Half price with the Strasbourg City Card. Tickets are non-refundable.
  • Tips. Wear good shoes and bring water. The passages are narrow, the climb takes some effort, there are no toilets at the top and animals are not allowed.

A sweet stop on rue des Orfèvres

A stone's throw from the cathedral, rue des Orfèvres is the foodie street of the Carré d'Or. We stopped there for some delicious shopping.

Weiss chocolate shop, at number 7. A small clarification: Weiss isn't Alsatian. It's a company from Saint-Étienne founded in 1882, which still makes its own chocolate, from bean to bar. It has a shop here.

Maison Naegel, right next door at number 9. An institution since 1927, both pastry shop and delicatessen. Its tourte and its pâté en croûte, made to closely guarded recipes, built its reputation, along with its sweet Alsatian specialties.

Specialties to try (or take home)

  • Kougelhopf. The emblematic brioche of Alsace, baked in a crown-shaped mold, with raisins and almonds on top. There's also a savory version, with bacon and walnuts.
  • Tarte au fromage blanc. A light, creamy cheesecake, a classic of Alsatian pastry shops.
  • Streusel. A brioche topped with a sweet crumble.
  • Bretzel. The knot-shaped bread, sprinkled with coarse salt.
  • Alsatian tourte. Puff pastry filled with marinated meat, eaten hot.
  • Gingerbread. A long-standing Alsatian specialty, especially in the run-up to Christmas.
  • Bredele. Small Christmas cookies, in dozens of varieties. You'll mostly find them from late November through December.
  • Mannele. A little brioche man, made for Saint Nicholas Day, on December 6.

Tip. Alsatian pastries are meant to be eaten fresh. Better to buy them from an artisan baker than wrapped in plastic on a stall.

Place Kléber

Before going back to the cathedral, we walked through Place Kléber. It's the largest square in Strasbourg. In the middle stands the statue of General Kléber, the local boy who gave it his name.

During the Christmas market, this is where the Great Christmas Tree stands: 30 meters tall, cut in the Vosges forests. According to the city, it's the tallest Christmas tree in France. At its foot, chalets are run by local charities. The historic market, the Christkindelsmärik, is held on nearby Place Broglie, and has been since 1570.

When we went by, crews were putting up the tree and hanging the lights all around. In 2026, the Christmas market opens on Friday, November 27.

Good to know. On Wednesdays and Fridays, Place Kléber hosts a market with no food stalls, from 7 am to 6 pm. If you're looking for clothes, that's the time to go.

Foie gras, a Strasbourg story

In the streets of the center, we noticed quite a few shops selling foie gras. Surprising, since you tend to associate it with southwest France. But Strasbourg played a key role.

Foie gras itself is much older: traces of it go back to ancient Egypt, some 4,500 years ago. What was born in Strasbourg is pâté de foie gras. Around 1780, Jean-Pierre Clause, cook to the Maréchal de Contades, military governor of Alsace, wrapped a whole foie gras in a pastry crust. Some call it a legend, but the recipe does date from that time. "Strasbourg foie gras pâté" became famous all over Europe.

For a long time, foie gras was seen as an Alsatian specialty. It was only in the mid-20th century that the Southwest took over. A notable difference: in Alsace, the tradition leans toward goose foie gras, while the Southwest mostly works with duck.

The astronomical clock and the cathedral

Around noon, we came back for the astronomical clock. €4 admission. The session starts with a film explaining how it works, before the parade of the apostles at 12:30 pm.

The trick. At that time of day, the cathedral is closed to free visits, roughly from 11:15 am to 12:45 pm. But the clock ticket still lets you in to see it. Two visits for the price of one. Tickets are sold on the day, and entry is through the south portal from about 11:35 am. No show on Sundays and holy days.

Three clocks and a childhood promise

The clock you see today is the cathedral's third.

The first was built around 1352-1354 by a clockmaker whose name has been lost. It's known as the Three Kings clock: on the hour, the Magi came out to greet the Virgin, and a mechanical rooster crowed. It stopped around 1500. Its rooster still exists and is kept in the Museum of Decorative Arts, in the Palais Rohan.

The second was started in 1547, then interrupted. It was completed in 1574 under the direction of the mathematician Conrad Dasypodius, with two Swiss clockmaker brothers, Isaac and Josias Habrecht, and the painter Tobias Stimmer for the decoration. Its large painted 16th-century case is the one we still admire today. But the mechanism eventually stopped, shortly before the Revolution.

The third is the work of Jean-Baptiste Schwilgué, and this is where the legend comes in. Born in Strasbourg in 1776, he grew up a stone's throw from the cathedral. As a child, he kept coming to quiz the caretaker about the silent clock. The story goes that at 11, in 1788, upset by the breakdown, he promised his mother: "I'll make it work, Mama."

He kept his word. Self-taught, he trained as an apprentice with a clockmaker, became a math teacher, an inspector of weights and measures, then a business owner. Between 1838 and 1842, rather than repair the old mechanism, he built a new one, housed in the 16th-century case. His portrait even appears on the clock.

Why 12:30?

The clock follows Strasbourg's solar time. When it shows noon, it's 12:30 by our watches. That's when all the automata come to life. The show unfolds in three parts:

  • The ages of life. The automata represent the stages of life, from child to old man, passing in front of Death.
  • The parade of the apostles. The twelve apostles pass one by one in front of Christ.
  • The rooster's crow. It flaps its wings and crows three times, the last as the twelfth apostle goes by.

Our take. The clock is extraordinary. It's worth a trip to Strasbourg on its own.

The planetary dial and the celestial globe

The clock doesn't just tell the time. It also shows the sky.

The planetary dial is the large face where the planets revolve around the Sun. It shows the six planets closest to the Sun: Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter and Saturn. Each one shows its real position. So Mercury completes its orbit in 88 days, while Saturn takes nearly 30 years. Schwilgué's clock was the one that introduced this modern view, with the Sun at the center.

In front of the clock, the celestial globe shows the starry sky. Schwilgué even built in the precession of the equinoxes: a very slow wobble of the Earth's axis, which takes just under 26,000 years to complete a full turn.

Add to that the calendar, the phases of the Moon and eclipses: a true 19th-century mechanical computer.

The ecclesiastical computus

This is Schwilgué's masterpiece, at the bottom left of the clock. A tangle of gear wheels, visible behind glass.

Its job: to calculate the date of Easter. Not so simple, because Easter has no fixed date. It's the first Sunday after the first full moon of spring. So it falls between March 22 and April 25. And all the other movable feasts depend on Easter: Ascension, Pentecost, Ash Wednesday. The old clock only gave them a hundred years in advance.

Every December 31 at midnight, the computus does its calculations on its own and sets the calendar for the following year, taking leap years into account. Schwilgué had developed a prototype as early as 1816, presented to King Louis XVIII a few years later. This small model, hidden during the war by his descendants, was donated to the cathedral in 2021.

The Pillar of Angels

Right next to the clock, in the same arm of the cathedral, don't miss the Pillar of Angels. It's the cathedral's sculptural masterpiece.

It was carved around 1225 to 1235 by a highly innovative workshop from the west, often compared to the sculptors of Chartres. It rises about 18 meters and tells the story of the Last Judgment in twelve statues, on three levels:

  • At the bottom, the four evangelists, with their symbols.
  • In the middle, four angels blowing trumpets to wake the dead.
  • At the top, Christ the Judge, surrounded by angels carrying the instruments of the Passion. At his feet, small figures rise from their tombs.

What strikes you is the upward momentum and the gentleness of the faces. No hell or damned souls here: this Last Judgment speaks of salvation rather than punishment.

And the cathedral itself?

Its sheer scale is impressive. On the other hand, there are fewer objects and furnishings than in other cathedrals we've visited.

There's a historical reason for that. In the 16th century, Strasbourg turned to the Reformation and the cathedral became Protestant. It then lost a large part of its statues and furnishings. It was only returned to Catholic worship in 1681, after the city became part of France.

We may prefer the outside to the inside, even if the nave overwhelms you with its height and size.

The stained glass windows, however, are splendid. Starting with the great rose window above the entrance, known as the "rose of Erwin", after master Erwin von Steinbach, one of the cathedral's architects, who died in 1318. It measures about 13.6 meters across and has sixteen petals, whereas most Gothic rose windows have twelve. All around, the other windows are well worth a look too: the cathedral holds one of the largest surfaces of medieval stained glass.

The Rohraffe, the automaton that heckled the preacher

Look up at the great organ, hanging above the nave. Its gallery is the oldest surviving one in the world, and its case dates from 1491. It is framed by three articulated statues:

  • Samson, prying open a lion's jaws, under the gallery.
  • The town herald, raising a trumpet to his lips.
  • The Rohraffe, a figure raising his right hand.

These statues moved thanks to cables pulled from the gallery. And an actor could hide under the organ to give them a voice. During services, especially at Pentecost, he answered back to the preacher and called out to the congregation, usually in a comic tone. A way for the people of Strasbourg to remind everyone that the city stood up to its bishop: the statues even wear Strasbourg's colors.

The Palais Rohan, for enthusiasts

Right opposite the cathedral, on Place du Château, stands the Palais Rohan. We didn't visit it, it's not really our thing, but enthusiasts will love it.

It's the former residence of the prince-bishops of Strasbourg, built from 1732 to 1742 to plans by Robert de Cotte, the king's chief architect. Louis XV stayed there in 1744, Marie-Antoinette in 1770, then Napoleon and Joséphine. Today the palace houses three museums: Decorative Arts, Fine Arts and Archaeology. A nod to the clock: the Museum of Decorative Arts is where the rooster from the cathedral's very first clock is kept.

A small antiques market

Heading back down toward the Ill, we came across a small antiques market on Place du Vieux-Marché-aux-Poissons. We were there on a Friday.

Good to know. Strasbourg's flea market is also held on Wednesdays and Saturdays, on rue du Vieil-Hôpital and Place de la Grande-Boucherie, just around the corner.

Lunch, Alsace wines and coffee

Lunch at L'Oignon

We had planned to have lunch at the Maison des Tanneurs, in Petite France. Bad luck: it was unusually closed that Friday.

We fell back on L'Oignon, 4 rue des Moulins, right next door. A neighborhood institution, with a terrace by the water, facing the swing bridge. We ate outside and it was very cold, so we each started with a soup. An onion soup, the house specialty, and a butternut soup for Patricia.

Then a roast duck breast with chestnuts and a cod loin with chanterelles.

Our take: excellent. The bill came to €74.50 for two, or €37.25 per person. For reference, the onion soup is €12, the duck breast €24, the cod €25 and a glass of pinot noir €5.50.

A word on Alsace wines

With the meal, a glass of pinot noir, the only red grape variety in Alsace wines. So-so.

The day before, we had tried a slightly sweet white wine that was much better: a gewurztraminer. We really enjoyed it. It's one of the great Alsace grape varieties, very aromatic, with notes of lychee, rose and spice. "Gewürz" actually means "spice" in German.

Good to know. In Alsace, wines are named after their grape variety. The best known are riesling, gewurztraminer, pinot gris, muscat, sylvaner, pinot blanc and pinot noir.

Coffee break at Cafés du Monde

After lunch, a short coffee break at Cafés du Monde, 116 Grand'Rue, a stone's throw from Petite France. They serve coffees from different origins, and you can also leave with a bag of beans.

Service is by a true enthusiast. Not fast, but a really nice little break.

07

Strasbourg from the water

In the afternoon, we bought tickets for the 4 pm boat tour with Batorama.

How it works

The landing stage is very close to the cathedral, at the foot of the Palais Rohan. Tickets are sold at an orange kiosk with ticket machines. There are then four boarding docks: check yours carefully before joining the line.

  • Duration. About 1 hour 15 minutes.
  • Price. €16 per person full price, €14.70 for ages 13 to 17, €8.90 for ages 4 to 12, free for under-4s.
  • Waiting time. We waited about thirty minutes before the boat left.
  • Commentary. Available in several languages, through individual headphones.

The route

The boat runs along the Grande Île, goes through the Petite France locks, past the Ponts Couverts and the Vauban Dam, then heads to the Neustadt, the German district, and the European district. A good way to see again, from the water, much of what we had visited on foot.

The pont du Corbeau

Among the stories in the commentary is that of the pont du Corbeau (Raven Bridge), right next to the Palais Rohan. Flower-filled and peaceful today, it has a much darker past. From the 14th to the 17th century, it was called the "bridge of torments".

This is where some people sentenced to death were executed, especially those guilty of killing a parent or a child. Locked in a cage, they were plunged into the Ill until they drowned. And the water back then was anything but clear: the river served as the city's sewer, and the condemned ended up drowning in filth.

The Ancienne Douane

Right next door, on the banks of the Ill, is the Ancienne Douane (Old Customs House). Built in 1358, it was one of the largest buildings in the city: goods arriving by river were stored and taxed there. It was destroyed by the 1944 bombings, then rebuilt between 1962 and 1965. Today it houses a restaurant and a covered market.

Toward Petite France

We then headed back up toward Petite France, following the explanations in our headphones. A small highlight: going through a lock. The boat enters the chamber, the gates close, and the water level changes so it can continue on its way.

Around the city by canal

The cruise continues with a loop around Strasbourg along its canals. We passed Saint-Paul church again, which we had seen that morning near Coffee Stub, this time from the water.

The commentary tells us that beyond Saint-Paul there was once no district at all, just fields. Until then, the city fit inside the loop of the Ill. Everything changed in 1871: after the Franco-Prussian War, Alsace was annexed to the German Empire and Strasbourg became the capital of the new territory. The Germans then launched the Neustadt, the "new town", which tripled the size of the city. Wide avenues, official palaces, a university, modern housing with running water and gas: it was a showcase for the Empire. It has been a UNESCO World Heritage Site since 2017.

The European district

The boat then heads to the European district, which we had walked through that morning. From the water, we saw the European Parliament again and discovered its neighbors:

  • The Palais des Droits de l'Homme, seat of the European Court of Human Rights, inaugurated in 1995. It was designed by Richard Rogers, one of the architects of the Centre Pompidou.
  • The Palais de l'Europe, seat of the Council of Europe, facing Orangerie park. Strasbourg was chosen as the Council's seat back in 1949.

Good to know. People often mix the two up, but the Council of Europe has nothing to do with the European Union. It brings together 46 countries and safeguards human rights across the continent. The European Parliament, on the other hand, is an institution of the European Union.

The spire and the Phrygian cap

Another story we learned during the tour: the Phrygian cap that once topped the cathedral spire.

During the Revolution, in 1793, revolutionaries tore down the statues of kings and saints from the cathedral. Some went further and proposed knocking down the spire: being so tall, it supposedly offended the principle of equality. To save it, a workaround was found. In 1794, it was topped with a huge tin Phrygian cap, the symbol of the Revolution. The spire thus became a revolutionary monument, and it escaped demolition. The cap stayed in place until 1802.

A movie night and Mexican dinner

After the boat, we went back to the hotel to rest a little. Then off to the cinema to see a movie.

We then had dinner at a restaurant right next to the cinema, mostly serving Mexican food. It was very busy. Not the best restaurant we've been to.

Day 3: a trip to Obernai

The next morning was a day of protests in France. Rather than stay in Strasbourg, where we had already seen a lot, we headed off to Obernai.

We took the 9:30 am train from Strasbourg station and arrived in Obernai at 10 am. The ticket costs about €7.50 one way (2026 price). Allow about 30 minutes for the journey, plus a 10-minute walk from our hotel: 40 minutes in all. We stayed until 1:30 pm. Our plane was leaving at 5:30 pm, but with the strikes and protests, we preferred to get to the airport early.

The bonus
Obernai

Obernai lies at the foot of Mont Sainte-Odile, about thirty kilometers southwest of Strasbourg. According to tradition, it's the birthplace of Saint Odile, the patron saint of Alsace.

Obernai is really nice, with its half-timbered houses and little shops.

The market square

The heart of the town is the Place du Marché. There you'll find:

  • The belfry, the Kapellturm, towering 60 meters over the town.
  • The Renaissance-style town hall, built between 1523 and 1610.
  • The corn exchange, from 1554. It was originally the butchers' hall: look for the two carved ox heads on the gable.
  • The Sainte-Odile fountain, in the middle of the square.

A few steps away, don't miss the six-bucket well, a Renaissance well from 1579, one of the most beautiful in Alsace.

Good to know. Obernai's market is held on Thursday mornings.

Half-timbered houses: how do they work?

In Obernai, as in Petite France, they're everywhere. But how are they built?

  • A wooden frame. The house rests on a skeleton of joined beams: horizontal beams, vertical posts and diagonal braces that stiffen the whole thing. In Alsace, wood was plentiful, and carpentry was a very common trade.
  • Infill between the beams. The gaps are filled with wattle and daub, a mixture of earth, sand, lime, water and chopped straw. In towns, brick was sometimes used.
  • Upper floors that jut out over the street. Since the posts only run one floor at a time, each level can overhang the one below a little. This is called jettying.
  • Very steep roofs, to shed the snow, covered with small, flat, rounded tiles known as "beaver tail" tiles.

Take a good look at the facades: the beams form patterns. Diamonds, Saint Andrew's crosses, suns, or the "Mann", a stylized little man. According to tradition, some of these patterns were meant to protect the house and those who lived in it. The technique reached its peak in the 18th century.

A stollen for the road

Lots of shops sell good things, and we gave in to a stollen, the typical Christmas cake. It's a dense brioche with candied fruit, raisins and almonds, often with a marzipan center and covered in powdered sugar. It was born in Germany, in Dresden, but it's one of the great Christmas classics in Alsace.

The little train and Mont National

Around 11 am, we took the little tourist train for a ride of about 45 minutes. All along the way, a commentary presents Obernai's sights.

It crosses the town, passes the fountain and the churches, then climbs through the vineyards to Mont National. There's a stop at the top, with a superb view over the whole valley.

On this hill stands the National Memorial to the Forcibly Conscripted, recognizable by its large cross overlooking the town. It honors the "Malgré-nous" (against our will): about 130,000 men from Alsace and Moselle forcibly drafted into the German army during the Second World War, after their region was annexed by Nazi Germany.

From the train, you also discover another side of Obernai: it's an industrial town. It's home to the Kronenbourg brewery, opened in 1969, the largest in France. According to the brewer, it produces the equivalent of one in three beers drunk in France; more recent figures put it closer to a quarter.

The little train finishes its loop through Obernai's narrow streets. You see things you wouldn't have seen otherwise.

Our take: for €9 per person, it's really worth it. We were lucky: the little train stops running on October 31, the very day of our visit. It starts up again for the Christmas market, which is said to be splendid and draws big crowds, then stops again on January 31 and reopens on April 1.

The church of Saints Peter and Paul

Getting off the train, we headed for the main church. Quite majestic, like all the churches in the region.

Its history goes back further than it looks. In the Middle Ages, the site was outside the town walls and belonged to the abbey of Mont Sainte-Odile. A 12th-century Romanesque church was built here first, then replaced by a Gothic church three centuries later. Having become too small, it was completely demolished in 1867. The current church, in neo-Gothic style, was built by the architect Eugène Petiti and inaugurated in 1872.

Inside, a few treasures survived from the old church: a Holy Sepulchre altar from 1504, attributed to the Strasbourg sculptor Veit Wagner, surrounded by 15th-century stained glass from the demolished church, and a Merklin organ from 1881-1882. The heart of Bishop Freppel, bishop of Angers born in Obernai in 1827, is also kept here. Its spires, worn by time, were restored between 2019 and 2021.

Good to know. Allow about 2 hours to visit Obernai, little train included. That's usually enough.

Last treats

Back to Strasbourg by train to pick up our bags. Before leaving, we treated ourselves to one last specialty: an onion tarte flambée at La Fignette, 5 rue de la Vignette, very close to rue du Vieux-Marché-aux-Vins. €11 for the large tart.

Tarte flambée, or flammekueche in Alsatian, is a very thin bread dough topped with cream and onions, and usually bacon. It bakes in a few minutes in a very hot oven, where the flames lick the edges: hence the name. Traditionally, it's shared among several people and eaten with your fingers.

One last treat before leaving, bought at Naegel on rue des Orfèvres, which had become our go-to bakery: a bretzel for Patricia, who stays loyal to savory, and on the sweet side, a langhopf. It's a lesser-known cousin of the kougelhopf: a soft brioche filled with rum-soaked raisins, hazelnuts, walnuts and almonds, baked in an earthenware mold. Straight out of the oven, it's brushed with butter and rolled in cinnamon sugar. A recent specialty, which appeared barely thirty years ago.

Our verdict

Our trip lasted two days, arriving on Thursday afternoon and leaving on Saturday afternoon. That's plenty of time to discover Strasbourg and Obernai.

  • Hotel. €320 for two nights in a King room.
  • Total budget. About €600 for the two of us, not counting flights: hotel, meals, visits and small purchases included.
  • Our favorites. The astronomical clock, Petite France and Orangerie park in autumn.
  • Our top pick. L'Oignon, for an excellent lunch in Petite France.

Strasbourg is one of the most beautiful cities we have ever visited.
