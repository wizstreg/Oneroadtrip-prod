01

Castro Marina, our base for these two days

This article is part of our 14-day road trip through Puglia. If you are joining halfway: we spent 4 days around Alberobello, then 2 days between Brindisi and Otranto. Today we run two days together, the baroque churches of Salento and a boat trip along the cliffs of Castro.

A practical reminder. In southern Puglia a lot of monuments and museums close between 1pm and 4pm. Churches keep even less predictable hours. Our advice is to visit in the morning and late afternoon, and save the dead hours of the afternoon for the beach, a coffee, or the busier tourist towns.

Where we slept
Hotel degli Ulivi, Castro Marina

The same hotel as in the previous article, and we still recommend it just as much. It is not the newest place in the region, but the view when you wake up makes up for it.

  • Breakfast. Generous, and pleasant to eat on the covered terrace.
  • The price. As charming as the hosts, 115 euros a night in the middle of August.
  • Parking. Plenty of spaces, and that is worth gold in Puglia.

02

Maglie, the birthplace of Aldo Moro

We leave Castro around 8am and head north. Forty minutes on the road and we reach Maglie, a small Salento town most tourists drive straight through. Yet this is where Aldo Moro (1916-1978) was born, the Italian prime minister kidnapped and murdered by the Red Brigades in 1978 after 55 days in captivity. The case remains one of the most traumatic episodes in Italian history.

Piazza Aldo Moro

We start the visit on the square that carries his name. Look up, because the beauty of Maglie is above eye level.

The facades are covered with carvings, dragons and wrought-iron lamps. A few clothes and jewellery shops line the square.

The old town

We walk into the historic centre. As is often the case in old Salento towns, it is very quiet. No shops inside, no crowds. The streets are a little wider than elsewhere in the region, which makes it feel airier than tight centres like Ostuni or Castro.

The cathedral

We finish with the Duomo della Presentazione del Signore, built at the end of the 18th century in baroque leccese style on the ruins of two older churches. The Lecce stone facade is carved, with a doorway framed by two cherubs and topped by a coat of arms. Inside, three naves separated by solid pillars. The transept altars and the high altar, carved in Lecce stone by Emanuele Orfano and hand painted, are worth a look. The wooden choir has 80 stalls.

The 48-metre campanile (1686-1690) is the tallest in the whole province of Lecce. Its four upper levels are attributed to Giuseppe Zimbalo, the architect behind the Sant'Oronzo column in Lecce. Remember that name, it will come up all day long.

03

Galatina, the birthplace of the pasticciotto

We carry on to Galatina, twenty kilometres south of Lecce. This is where it all began for the pasticciotto, the emblematic pastry of Salento, a golden shortcrust shell filled with lemon-scented custard.

La Pasticceria Ascalone

The story goes back to 1740, when Nicola Ascalone opened his pastry shop. Five years later his son Andrea, going through lean times, gathered leftover shortcrust scraps and spare custard, pressed them into small copper moulds and baked them. He thought the result was a failure. He gave one to a passer-by who came back for more. "Che pasticcio!", what a mess, he is said to have exclaimed. The name stuck.

The shop is still there, Via Vittorio Emanuele II 17, run by the eleventh generation. A compulsory pilgrimage, and you eat the pasticciotto warm, straight out of the oven, with a coffee.

Order ahead. Open Tuesday to Saturday morning only. When we arrived at 1pm everything had been cleared out, and one man was taking the last 7.

Our favourite ⭐
Basilica of Santa Caterina d'Alessandria

Galatina is worth the detour for this alone. Built in the 14th century, it is a gothic masterpiece whose interior is entirely covered with late 14th-century frescoes. The cycle is the second most important in Italy after the one in San Francesco in Assisi.

Inside, the frescoes cover everything, walls, vaults and columns. That is what makes Galatina a stop you should not skip.

The cloister of the adjoining convent is dotted with late 17th-century frescoes, martyr scenes and allegories of the virtues. The walls reminded the monks of the suffering of the saints.

The old town

The main street leading to the church is lined with art galleries. We walk past the clock tower, now converted into the tourist office.

The historic centre is best walked, a maze of cobbled streets where baroque palazzi, decorated balconies and Lecce stone doorways keep appearing.

Chiesa dei Santi Pietro e Paolo

The mother church of Galatina, dedicated to the town's patron saints. The present building dates from 1621-1633, rebuilt over a 14th-century church where mass was still said in the Greek rite. The baroque Lecce stone facade rises to 36 metres, with statues of saints in the niches. Inside, the vault frescoes are by the Neapolitan painter Vincenzo Paliotti and tell the life of Saint Peter. The polychrome marble altars are worth the detour.

04

Lecce, the Florence of the South

We reach Lecce in the early afternoon. One tip before anything else, bring a power bank. Lecce is a town you look at as much as you walk through, and your phone will not survive it. There is a photo waiting on every corner.

Porta San Biagio

We enter the old town through Porta San Biagio, the southern gate. Rebuilt in 1774, it stands 17.3 metres high.

Columns frame the passage, with the coats of arms of Ferdinand IV and of the town. At the top, the statue of Saint Blaise, bishop, watches over those arriving.

Right opposite the church of San Matteo, look out for the Pollicastro Boutique Hotel, set in the 16th-century Palazzo dei Perroni, Via dei Perroni 14. At the entrance a carved angel holds a ciambella. The rooftop terrace looks out over Porta San Biagio.

  • The combined ticket. 4 churches and cathedrals, 11 € per person in July 2026.
  • With the towers. 21 € per person if you also want to climb the bell towers.
  • Where to buy it. On Piazza del Duomo. The visit starts with the cloister right next to the ticket desk, with its Lecce stone well in the middle.

The Duomo, cathedral of Santa Maria Assunta

Founded in 1144, rebuilt a first time in 1230, then completely rebuilt between 1659 and 1670 by Giuseppe Zimbalo for bishop Luigi Pappacoda.

Its oddity is that it has two facades. The main one is plain, with columns and statues of saints. The side one, facing Piazza del Duomo, is spectacular, designed like a triumphal arch with the statue of Sant'Oronzo on top, flanked by angels. Zimbalo wanted it that way so that nobody would walk onto the square facing a bare wall.

Inside, a Latin cross plan, three naves, 12 altars on top of the high altar. The carved wooden coffered ceiling of 1685 holds paintings by Giuseppe da Brindisi.

Under the transept, the early 16th-century crypt holds 92 Lecce stone columns whose capitals are carved with symbols.

Do not miss the bronze door of the main facade, commissioned by archbishop Ruppi for the Jubilee of 2000 and made by the sculptor Armando Marrocco, born in Galatina in 1939. In the upper part, the risen Christ is shown as master of time (the sun on the left) and of history (the moon on the right). On the panels: the Assumption of the Virgin, the martyrdom of the patron saints, the embrace between John Paul II and archbishop Ruppi during his 1994 visit, Saint Peter's basilica, and the people of God on pilgrimage.

The campanile

We cross the square and go up to the top by lift. Built between 1661 and 1682 by Giuseppe Zimbalo, it reaches 72 metres. The view over Lecce from up there is worth the detour.

Church of San Matteo, the architectural oddity

Built between 1667 and 1700, its facade plays with curves: the lower half swells outwards, the upper half hollows inwards. No other church in Lecce does that. Its architect, Giovanni Andrea Larducci, was Borromini's nephew and drew directly on San Carlo alle Quattro Fontane in Rome. After his death it was Giuseppe Zimbalo again who finished the upper part, pulling it back towards the local style.

Inside, a single elliptical nave lined with chapels. The 12 statues of the apostles by Placido Buffelli (1692) stand between them. The altar of San Matteo is remarkable: twisted columns, a star-strewn blue niche holding a wooden statue of Saint Matthew (1691, by the Neapolitan sculptor Gaetano Patalano). The church also keeps a fresco from 1400 of the nursing Virgin, saved from a demolished church.

Church of Santa Chiara

Founded in 1429, rebuilt between 1687 and 1691 by Giuseppe Cino. Like San Matteo, its facade is convex, inspired by Borromini. An elongated octagonal plan, a single nave, six side chapels with baroque altars and wooden statues brought from Naples.

Above the arches, wrought-iron grilles. That is where the Poor Clare nuns followed mass unseen, coming straight from their convent, which is now the MUST, the historical museum of Lecce.

The nice touch. The ceiling looks like carved wood, as in the other churches. It is papier-mâché. Designed by Mauro Manieri in 1738, it uses the traditional Lecce cartapesta technique, famous all over Europe. From the outside the church looks almost unfinished. From the inside it is a jewel.

Piazza Sant'Oronzo

Heading on to the last church on the ticket, we run into the living heart of Lecce. Right in the middle of the square, a Roman amphitheatre from the 1st and 2nd centuries rises between the modern buildings. It was found by accident in 1901 by workers digging the foundations of a bank. Only a third is visible, the rest sleeps under the surrounding blocks. It could hold around 25,000 spectators for gladiator fights. Access is free.

The Sant'Oronzo column, 29 metres high, dominates the square. Its story is a good one: it comes from one of the two Roman columns that marked the end of the Via Appia in Brindisi, the ones we wrote about in the previous article. Brindisi gave it to Lecce in the 17th century to thank Sant'Oronzo for saving the town from the plague of 1656. The bronze statue on top dates from 1739, the previous one, wood covered with copper, was struck by a meteorite in 1737.

The masterpiece of baroque leccese ⭐
Basilica of Santa Croce

It took 150 years to build (1549-1695) and went through three successive architects: Gabriele Riccardi for the structure and the lower facade, Francesco Antonio Zimbalo and Cesare Penna for the rose window, and Giuseppe Zimbalo for the decoration of the upper part.

The facade is stone lace. The lower part stays plain, almost Renaissance, with six smooth columns. Then your eye climbs and everything explodes: a balustrade held up by grotesque figures, fantastic animals, atlantes. Those bent figures are said to represent the Turks defeated at Lepanto in 1571, and the animals the victorious Catholic powers, the lion of Venice and the she-wolf of Rome. In the centre, a large rose window.

A dark historical detail: the church was built on land confiscated from the Jewish community, expelled from the town at the start of the 16th century.

Sadly we could not see the inside: a wedding was under way, with the couple's car parked right in front of the door. We will tell you what waits for you anyway: a Latin cross plan, a walnut coffered ceiling with a canvas of the Trinity at its centre, 16 baroque altars. The finest is the one of San Francesco da Paola in the left transept (1614), a monumental Lecce stone altarpiece with six columns and scenes from the life of Christ carved by Zimbalo.

What we did not have time for

  • The Roman theatre. 1st and 2nd centuries, just behind Piazza Sant'Oronzo, found in 1929, around 5,000 spectators.
  • The castle of Charles V. A 16th-century fortress built against Ottoman raids, now an exhibition space.
  • Porta Napoli. A triumphal arch from 1548, raised in honour of Charles V.
  • The inside of Santa Croce. Which we missed because of the wedding.
  • The MUST. The historical museum of Lecce, in the former Poor Clare convent behind Santa Chiara.

It is 5pm, we head back to Castro Marina through Porta San Biagio and go and get ready for dinner.

05

The Castro sea caves by boat

After two days of sightseeing we change the pace. This morning it is the sea and relative rest.

We leave at 9am from the harbour of Castro Marina for a 3-hour boat trip, on a small boat with ten of us on board: the caves and two swimming stops, 35 € per person. Two hours would be enough in our view.

Book the boat trip →

We are not the only ones on the water. Several boats run the same circuit along the cliffs.

Grotta Palombara

The first cave. It is 75 metres long, 18 wide and 30 high. Its name comes from the pigeons, palombi in the local dialect, that nest in the limestone hollows where freshwater springs seep through. The steady temperature and the fresh water make it an ideal shelter for birds. It is also called the cave of curtains, because seen from inside the entrance looks like a theatre curtain tied back at the edges. It formed as the upper walls gradually collapsed along two faults.

La Rotonda and the Grotta Azzurra

We pass La Rotonda, which connects to the Grotta Azzurra, reachable only from the sea. It is a small cavity 7 metres high with 15 metres of water beneath. Sunlight comes in through submerged cracks, bounces off the limestone floor and paints the whole cave cobalt blue. The same effect as the famous cave on Capri, only more intimate. Go in the morning for the strongest reflections.

Grotta Zinzulusa, visited on foot

The most important of the Castro caves and one of the finest sea cave sites in Italy. Careful though: you do not visit it by boat but on foot, from a built access on the cliff.

Formed in the Pliocene, it runs 160 metres into the cliff. Its name comes from the Salento dialect word zinzuli, rags: the stalactites hanging from the ceiling look like washing on a line. The guided visit goes through the Corridor of Wonders, then the Crypt, then the great Dome chamber. Right at the back, the Cocito, an underground lake with layered water, fresh on the surface and salt below, home to rare organisms. Palaeolithic remains have been found there, proof that the cave was lived in.

Closed to the public
Grotta Romanelli

You see it from the boat but you do not go in. It is still under study and it is a major site for Italian prehistory. Found in 1900 by Paolo Emilio Stasi, this 35-metre cave proved for the first time that the Upper Palaeolithic had reached Italy, which was disputed at the time.

Recent work by the universities of Rome, Turin and Milan, with the CNR, has pushed the dating back to 350,000 years, earlier than the Neanderthals. Three human skeletons have been found there, tools, hunting scenes scratched on the walls, and fossils of rhinoceros, elephants and hyenas, proof that Salento was once a savannah that turned to tundra after the ice age. The term "Romanellian Palaeolithic" was coined from this cave.

Grotta della Strega

The witch's cave owes its name to a shape said to suggest one. We looked from every angle and we still could not really make her out.

Swimming and an aperitivo

The rest of the trip is pure pleasure: two swimming stops, one of them in the Baia di Porto Miggiano, a wild cove wedged between cliffs in the municipality of Santa Cesarea Terme. It is also known as the beach of the 100 steps, but from the boat you skip the descent. The water is turquoise and the 16th-century watchtower keeps guard above.

Three easy hours, surrounded by Italian voices, and an aperitivo before heading back. The perfect break after the intensity of the days before.

06

Tricase and Specchia in the afternoon

Tricase Porto

After the boat we head south. Tricase Porto is a pretty little fishing harbour with a pleasant beach.

Tricase town

We drive back up into the town of Tricase. It is fairly large, with a good number of shops if you feel like browsing. On the main square, the 16th-century Palazzo Risolo and the mother church are worth a look. We stop for a quick lunch at Martinucci, the big Salento pastry house founded in 1950 by Rocco Martinucci and Annunziata Panese. Today it has 28 outlets as far as Rome and Milan, but it all started here in Salento. Pasticciotti, rustici, ice cream: you eat fast and well.

Our favourite ⭐
Specchia

Fifteen minutes from Tricase, listed among the borghi più belli d'Italia. A medieval village from the 9th century, perched on a hill among the olive trees. The historic centre is a maze of cobbled lanes, white houses, vaulted passages and small flowered courtyards.

We visit the church and get lost in the lanes. At 4pm in high summer, only the swallows and us are out on the streets. It is exactly the kind of place that appears in no guidebook and stays with you. Back to Castro Marina for our third evening.

07

Castro Marina, our base camp

Castro comes in two halves: Castro Marina down at the water, and the old town up on the cliff. The Hotel degli Ulivi sits exactly in between, 8 minutes on foot from the marina and 8 from the old town. It is the ideal spot. In the evening we walk up for dinner.

The beach. No sand here, it is rock. Private beaches with sunbeds at around 30 € a day, and free access points where you lay your towel on the stone. The water is crystal clear.

The old town in the evening. It is charming. Restaurants follow one after another in the lanes, and the belvedere gives a superb view over the bay. The medieval castle has been turned into a museum.

Under the cathedral, in the church of SS. Annunziata, do not miss the Sacra Textile exhibition, extraordinary church vestments shown in a small vaulted room. We tried the best rated restaurant in town, a meat specialist but with vegetarian options.

Castro is an ideal base for exploring Salento: 40 minutes from Lecce, 30 from Otranto, 15 from Tricase, and the harbour is where the boat trips to the caves start.
