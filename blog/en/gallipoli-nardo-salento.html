01

Castro Marina, our base for these two days

This article is part of our 14-day road trip through Puglia. If you are joining us halfway: we spent 4 days around Alberobello, 2 days between Brindisi and Otranto, then 2 days of caves and baroque out of Castro. Today, two big days: the Ionian coast, the villages at the tip of the Salento, and an unexpected rival to Lecce.

02

Gallipoli, the pearl of the Ionian Sea

Leaving Castro at 9am, about an hour on the road. We reach Gallipoli around 10am.

Parking. Use the big car park by the harbour, not the small one at the entrance to the old town.

From the car park, the view opens onto the harbour, the fishing boats, and at the far end the bridge across to the old town. That is the Ponte Papa Giovanni Paolo II, lined with small stalls. You can see the fort and the old warehouse on the right. Arcades at the entrance shelter souvenir shops. Gallipoli hesitates between fish and tourism, and in summer tourism has won.

Before you cross the bridge, look for the Fontana Greca on the square. It is a Renaissance fountain, long considered one of the oldest in Italy.

The Angevin castle

Just past the bridge, the Castello Angioino guards the entrance to the old town. The core dates from the 13th century, built by Charles I of Anjou. In 1535, the military architect Evangelista Menga modernised it with four large corner bastions and a fine entrance gate in local calcarenite.

In 1557, the castle was sold to the Genoese Squarciafico family, who had the inner chapel decorated with frescoes by Gianserio Strafella. Reopened to the public in 2014, it hosts exhibitions in the galleries linking the bastions.

According to some historians, the Spanish painter José de Ribera (1591-1652), known as "lo Spagnoletto", was born in this castle.

The old town

You step into the maze of alleyways. It is charming. You pass the Palazzo Ravenna, a 19th century neoclassical palace designed by Gregorio Consiglio, now turned into a 5-star hotel. Sant'Agata cathedral is open but a service is under way, so we only walk through.

The alleys are full of little shops, flower-filled balconies and washing hung between the façades. Gallipoli lives up to its name, "beautiful city" in Greek.

Our favourite ⭐
The Frantoio Ipogeo, the underground mill

This is the surprise of Gallipoli. Beneath the Palazzo Granafei, a fully restored 17th century underground olive oil mill, covering 200 m². It is the only one of the town's 35 underground mills to have been restored.

Highly recommended, allow 30 minutes.

AddressVia Antonietta De Pace
Price2 € per person, audio guide available
Area200 m² beneath the Palazzo Granafei
Time needed30 minutes

The story is fascinating. From the 16th to the 19th century, Gallipoli was the world capital of lamp oil. Its olive oil, too strong for cooking, was used to light the great cities of Europe: Paris, London, Berlin, Amsterdam, Stockholm, Oslo. The oil ran into wells dug out of the rock. The waste water was used to make soap.

Working conditions were harsh. The workers and the donkeys lived underground for nine months, in alternating shifts. One space was set aside for the donkeys that turned the millstone. All of it in permanent darkness and damp.

A panel retraces Gallipoli's foreign trade: 12 foreign vice-consulates were active in the town until 1923, including Austria, Denmark, France, England, the Ottoman Empire, the Netherlands, Portugal, Prussia, Russia, Spain and Sweden-Norway. It gives you an idea of how important this small town was in international trade.

Right next to the mill, a local wine shop. We look around, we do not taste, it is still morning. Shipping costs about 40 € for 12 bottles, so no.

Our biggest favourite ⭐
Chiesa Santa Maria della Purità

This is the church we liked best of the whole trip. It feels like a pagan sanctuary, and it would fit right into the esoteric routes on OneRoadTrip.

Walking through the Palazzo Granafei towards the sea, you come out onto a lovely sandy beach. Just before it, on the left, the church (1662-1665), built by the dockworkers' confraternity.

EntryFree

The interior is overloaded: not one empty square centimetre. Canvases cover the walls from floor to ceiling. On the vault, scenes from the Apocalypse, with a demon coiled in the corners.

Large biblical canvases signed Liborio Riccio, Judith and Holofernes, David and Goliath. The high altar canvas is attributed to Luca Giordano. The floor is Vietri majolica.

In one corner, a mannequin dressed in a white robe with a pointed hood and a red belt. It is the confraternity's penitent's habit, inherited from the 17th century Spanish tradition and worn during the Holy Week processions. A very realistic, almost morbid Christ on the cross and a grieving Virgin in cartapesta complete the picture.

It looks like a black church. The atmosphere is unsettling, almost oppressive.

The verdict. After two hours of visiting, we head off. Gallipoli is very charming and deserves half a day. The Frantoio Ipogeo and the Purità are the two things not to miss.

03

Casarano, Ugento and Presicce

Casarano, ghost town

We stop at Casarano, about twenty minutes south of Gallipoli. It is half past twelve. Everything is closed. Not a tourist in the street, and not a local either.

The town can still be walked even when it is closed. We walk a kilometre to the Chiesa di Santa Maria della Croce, known as Casaranello, a small 5th century early Christian church famous for its mosaics and its 9th century Byzantine frescoes. It is one of the oldest Christian monuments in Puglia.

Mind the opening hours. In July 2026, the church only opens from 4pm to 5pm. The door was shut when we arrived. If you do not know that slot, you will make the trip for nothing. Apart from this church there is nothing special to see. We would advise you to move on, unless you are sure of turning up at the right moment.

Ugento, unpretentious

We carry on to Ugento, an unpretentious little town ten minutes from Casarano.

The Santa Maria Assunta cathedral is open, so we go in. The current building dates from 1718, built thanks to a bequest from bishop Antonio Carafa on the ruins of a Gothic church destroyed by the Turks in 1537.

It was consecrated in 1745. The neoclassical façade, added in 1855, is dominated by four Ionic columns supporting a triangular pediment.

The interior is plain compared with the baroque churches we have seen over the past few days. A single Latin cross nave. The polychrome marble altar from 1740 and the rococo olive wood choir are the highlights. Several altars follow one another along the side walls, one of them with a canvas by Paolo Finoglio. Ugento is a diocesan seat, which explains the size of the cathedral for a town this small.

  • The castle. 13th century, Norman, now a 5-star hotel but open to visits on request.
  • The Archaeological Museum. Ugento's Messapian past, in the former Franciscan convent.
  • La Cripta del Crocifisso. A chapel carved into the rock, on the road to Melissano.

Presicce-Acquarica

We drive on to Presicce, listed among the most beautiful villages in Italy and merged with Acquarica del Capo, around 10,000 inhabitants in total, 1,500 of them in the old centre. On paper, it sounds tempting.

On the ground, it is more mixed. The village is deserted, probably because we arrive around 1pm in crushing heat. Better seen in the evening. Careful: there are two Piazza del Popolo. Follow the signs for the Palazzo Ducale to reach the right one, the square the centre is built around. The Palazzo Ducale quarter is a little prettier, but overall the baroque palaces are superb and very run down. A lot of things closed. Not the finest village we have seen in Puglia.

Chiesa Parrocchiale Sant'Andrea Apostolo. Built between 1776 and 1781 by the architect Saverio Negro. Plain baroque façade, Latin cross interior, 9 altars, stucco. The high altar is in Neapolitan polychrome marble, a gift from king Francis I thanks to Michele Arditi, a Presicce native and founder of the Naples museum. The 27.60-metre Renaissance bell tower dominates the square. Canvases by Catalano, Riccio, Tiso and Lillo. The adjoining Chiesa dei Morti, from 1575, is closed.

The 27.60-metre Renaissance bell tower dominates the square. The Palazzo Ducale, for its part, is closed.

Canvases by Catalano, Riccio, Tiso and Lillo. The adjoining Chiesa dei Morti, from 1575, is closed as well.

Acquarica del Capo. The Castello di Acquarica del Capo, turned into a museum, is closed to visitors. The 17th century Chiesa San Carlo Borromeo is worth a look. Built by baron Fabrizio Guarino after a miraculous recovery in Naples: two naves, Lecce stone, a baroque altar dedicated to the patron saint, an 18th century pipe organ. It is one of the first churches in southern Italy dedicated to San Carlo Borromeo, canonised in 1610.

04

Marina di Pescoluse, the Maldives of the Salento

End of the day at the beach. Marina di Pescoluse: 4 km of sand, warm translucent sea. The nickname "Maldives of the Salento" is well earned. The sand is beautiful, the water is perfect.

The catch: 40,000 holidaymakers, and spots have to be earned. You have to fight to lay down your towel.

Back to Castro along the coast road, with superb sea views. It takes longer than going inland, but we do not regret the detour.

05

Galatone, a sanctuary and some frustrations

We leave Castro heading west. First stop: Galatone, a small town about twenty minutes from Nardò.

Santuario del Santissimo Crocifisso della Pietà

The sanctuary is worth the detour. Baroque façade, well-kept interior. It is a place of pilgrimage built around an image of the Crucifix. In 1682 the building collapsed. But the oil lamp in front of the Crucifix stayed lit in the rubble. Local people saw it as a divine sign and rebuilt the church around that miraculous image.

The interior is well kept, and as soon as you step inside you understand why the sanctuary is still an active place of pilgrimage. Everything is arranged around the image of the Crucifix that survived the collapse of 1682.

Chiesa Madre. Closed. Under works since 2024, for about two years, with no indication of a reopening date. It is frustrating: in these small villages the churches are often the only heritage gems, and they are closed half the time.

Leonardo da Vinci Museum. In the Palazzo Marchesale, a museum devoted to Leonardo's machines, 5 €. Complicated hours: weekends from 5.30pm to 8.30pm, on weekdays by phone, and someone comes within 30 minutes. There is also a vintage radio museum, by phone only. A slightly homespun set-up.

06

Nardò, Lecce's rival

Nardò is the second historic centre of the province of Lecce. And it shows. Surprises turn up at every street corner.

Castello Acquaviva d'Aragona

We arrive straight at the castle, which guards the entrance to the old town. Very handsome. Built at the end of the 15th century by Belisario I Acquaviva in 1497, after the Turkish attack on Otranto in 1480 that had traumatised the whole region. Square plan, four corner towers including the "Torre dell'innamorato". The present façade dates from the 19th century, when the Personè family added rusticated facing and a balcony. The town hall bought the castle in 1933. A young man offers guided tours of the underground rooms. We did not do it: a castle is still a castle.

Giardino Botanico Storico

Right beside the castle, a botanical garden with free access. A small bar lets you have a coffee in the shade before starting the visit. We follow on with the Chiesa San Giuseppe, a few steps away, a quick stop.

The botanical garden can be visited freely, in the shade of the trees. It is the ideal breather before tackling the old centre, especially in the middle of the day.

Piazza Antonio Salandra

The heart of Nardò, considered one of the finest squares in the Salento. Entirely baroque, rebuilt after the devastating earthquake of 1743, magnitude 7.1, 150 dead out of 5,000 inhabitants. Arches, balconies, loggias, rococo doorways, all in Lecce stone.

Here you find the Palazzo della Pretura, the town hall, the 17th century Sedile topped by the statue of San Gregorio Armeno, protector of the town, and the 18th century church of San Trifone, dedicated to the saint said to have freed the town from an invasion of wasps.

In the middle of the square, the Guglia dell'Immacolata from 1769, a 19-metre baroque pyramidal column raised by the survivors of the earthquake. Under restoration when we passed through: you cannot see a thing.

Fontana del Toro

Set against the wall of the church of San Domenico, the Fontana del Toro from 1930 is one of the symbols of the town. Sculpted by Michele Gaballo, a local artist, it was inaugurated to celebrate the arrival of running water in Nardò via the Puglia Aqueduct. A young bull digs at the ground with its hoof: according to legend, Nardò was founded where a bull made water spring from the rock. The medallion bears the motto Tauri non Bovi, bulls, not oxen. Restored in 2021.

The Chiesa di San Domenico, for its part, is closed.

The façade of San Domenico is worth a look even with the door shut: it is against its wall that the Fontana del Toro stands, and the two have been facing each other for almost a century.

The highlight of Nardò ⭐
Cattedrale Maria SS. Assunta

We come upon it turning a corner. Superb. Founded in 1088 by the Normans, a national monument since 1879, a minor basilica since 1980. Baroque façade redone in 1725 by the architect Ferdinando Sanfelice, then restored between 1892 and 1899 to bring back its original Romanesque look.

The whole cathedral can be visited: you can go behind the altar and reach a few furnished rooms. Take your time.

The baroque façade was redone in 1725 by Ferdinando Sanfelice, then restored between 1892 and 1899 to give it back its original Romanesque look. That mixture is what gives the cathedral its unclassifiable air.

The interior: three naves separated by pointed arches, wooden ceiling. The walls and columns are covered with frescoes from the 13th to the 16th century, stories of saint Nicholas and saint Augustine.

On the left, halfway down the nave, the Cristo Nero, a 12th century cedar wood crucifix, darkened by time.

Opposite the cathedral, the Museo Diocesano, which we did not visit. A few hundred metres away, a nameless church next to the post office: closed. Fifty metres further on, the Chiesa Santa Teresa: closed too. The usual story in the Salento.

The verdict. A small town whose old centre amply deserves the detour. In the battle with Lecce, Nardò lost, but surprises turn up at every street corner. Allow 1 to 3 hours depending on whether you visit the castle and the museum. OneRoadTrip rating: 3 out of 5, though the town would deserve 4 out of 5.

07

Porto Cesareo and Punta Prosciutto

We push on to Porto Cesareo, a seaside resort on the Ionian coast. The centre takes a quarter of an hour to see. Facing the harbour, about a hundred metres offshore, the Isola Grande, known as rabbit island: you can walk over at low tide. It is probably livelier in the evening, but at midday it is deserted. Finding a restaurant is hard work, though there is an upside: once you find one, you have it to yourself.

Punta Prosciutto, the good beach

On to the beach at Punta Prosciutto, between Torre Castiglione and Punta Grossa. Far less crowded than Pescoluse the day before, and very pretty. We could put up our parasol without a fight and stay two hours reading quietly.

08

The Grotta Zinzulusa, this time on foot

We head back to Castro to visit the Grotta Zinzulusa on foot. We had seen it from the boat, but visiting from land is a different thing. You go down a staircase cut into the cliff to the mouth of the cave, right at water level. Inside, a walkway takes you about 150 metres in.

The stalactites and stalagmites are spectacular. The name comes from the local dialect word zinzuli, rags, because the limestone formations hanging from the vault look like tatters strung up.

The great chamber, called the Duomo, rises to 25 metres high. This is where hundreds of bats once lived. Their droppings, guano, built up over the centuries into a layer 7 metres thick in places. You could walk on it. On the walls you can still make out inscriptions: the workers hired to clear the guano, from 1906 and again in 1933, wrote their names and the dates straight onto the rock using the guano itself, dark and thick. Used as fertiliser, it was extracted over several decades. Those marks are part of the cave's history.

The cave also holds a small underground lake, the Cocito, and rare animal species adapted to total darkness. 7 € adult, 3 € child. Allow 30 minutes for the visit.

Castro Marina, the verdict

End of our stay in Castro, tomorrow we leave for Bari. In five days from this base we have covered the region from end to end: Lecce, Otranto, the caves, Gallipoli, Nardò, the tip of the Salento down to Pescoluse. Everything is less than an hour away. A little more time in Lecce would have been better, the city deserves it. Castro remains an ideal base for exploring the Salento.
