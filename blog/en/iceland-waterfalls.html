01

Why Iceland is waterfall country

There is no official count, but the island easily holds several dozen major waterfalls, and many more once you include the small unnamed ones.

This abundance is no accident. Sitting in the middle of the North Atlantic, Iceland takes in huge amounts of rain and snow. That water feeds rivers running down young, steep volcanic terrain, often topped up by glacial melt. The result: water falls everywhere.

Every waterfall has its own character. Some are wide and powerful, others tall and slender, others again framed by basalt columns or hidden deep in a canyon. This guide sorts them by region, in the order you would meet them driving the island on Route 1, the famous Ring Road, so you can link them up without pointless detours.

02

The South: the Route 1 classics

This is the most visited region in the country, and for good reason: two of Iceland's most famous waterfalls stand a few kilometres apart, both visible from the road, about two hours from Reykjavík.

South · The most photographed
Seljalandsfoss

Its signature: you can walk derrière the curtain of water. About sixty metres high, it can be circled entirely on a footpath, giving a rare view from the cave behind the falls. Bring a raincoat, the spray soaks you fast.

HauteurAbout 60 metres
AccèsRight on Route 1, paid parking at the base
À côtéGljúfrabúi, hidden in a rock cleft a few minutes' walk away and often missed
AstuceThe path behind the falls can be closed or icy in winter
South · The wall of water
Skógafoss

An almost perfect curtain of water, 60 metres high and 25 wide, fed by two glaciers. On sunny days the spray nearly always throws up a rainbow. A staircase of several hundred steps climbs to a platform at the top, with views over the coast.

DimensionsAbout 60 metres de haut, 25 de large
Accès500 metres off Route 1, next to the village of Skógar
Sur placeFree parking, café, toilets and a campsite nearby
LégendeA Viking chest is said to be hidden behind the falls
03

The Golden Circle: Gullfoss

The Golden Circle is Iceland's busiest route, a short loop out of Reykjavík linking Geysir, Þingvellir park and Gullfoss. It is often the first waterfall visitors ever see.

South-West · The golden falls
Gullfoss

Its name means « golden falls », after the rainbow that often hangs in its spray. It drops in two tiers set at right angles to each other, about 32 metres high and 70 wide in total, before rushing into a narrow gorge. The noise as you approach is striking.

DimensionsAbout 32 metres high, 70 wide, two tiers
AccèsRoute 35, easy even in winter, under 2 hours from Reykjavík
Point cléPart of the Golden Circle, right next to Geysir
AstuceTwo viewpoints, one from above, one close to the falls
04

The South-East: Svartifoss and the basalt columns

Carrying on east along Route 1 brings you to the Skaftafell area, inside Vatnajökull National Park. Here the star is not the force of the falls but their setting.

South-East · The black falls
Svartifoss

Its name means « the black falls ». It is not especially tall, but it is framed by striking basalt columns dark hexagonal shapes formed as lava cooled slowly. The contrast between white water and black rock is like nothing else on the island. You have to earn it: reaching the falls means a short hike from Skaftafell.

ParticularitéFramed by hexagonal basalt columns
AccèsOn foot from the Skaftafell visitor centre, allow for the walk
CadreVatnajökull National Park
AstuceProper shoes, the path climbs
05

The North: Goðafoss and Dettifoss

The north of the island, between Akureyri and Lake Mývatn, holds two waterfalls of opposite temperament: one elegant and balanced, the other raw and deafening.

North · The waterfall of the gods
Goðafoss

« The waterfall of the gods ». The name comes from the year 1000: when Iceland adopted Christianity, a chieftain is said to have thrown his pagan idols into the water here. At 30 metres wide and 12 high, it is neither the tallest nor the most powerful, but its horseshoe shape and turquoise water make it one of the most balanced.

DimensionsAbout 12 metres high, 30 wide, horseshoe-shaped
AccèsRight on Route 1, between Akureyri and Lake Mývatn
AstuceReachable from both banks, with different views on each side
Sur placeCar park two minutes' walk away
North-East · Europe's most powerful
Dettifoss

A monster. At 44 metres high and 100 metres wide, Dettifoss is reckoned to be the most powerful waterfall in Europe by volume. Set deep in the Jökulsá canyon, it impresses from a distance through sheer grey mass and the roar it puts out. Two banks give access, each with a different viewpoint.

Dimensions44 metres high, 100 wide
RéputationEurope's most powerful by volume
AccèsWest bank (road 862) or east bank (864), paths can be slippery
AstuceThe west bank is generally the easier option
06

The Highlands: Háifoss

Inland, where roads turn into gravel tracks, hide waterfalls far taller than the coastal stars. Háifoss is the clearest example.

Highlands · Among the tallest
Háifoss

At roughly 122 metres, Háifoss is one of the tallest waterfalls in Iceland. It drops into a dramatic canyon, usually alongside its neighbour Granni. Access is by gravel track and calls for a suitable vehicle, but the view repays the effort.

HauteurAbout 122 metres, among the tallest in the country
AccèsGravel track in the Þjórsárdalur valley, suitable vehicle advised
VoisineGranni, right beside it, makes a pair
SaisonTrack often impassable outside summer, check before setting off
07

The Westfjords: Dynjandi

The least visited region in the country, well off Route 1, keeps its most impressive prize for those who make the detour.

Westfjords · The hidden prize
Dynjandi

Also known as Fjallfoss, Dynjandi is the largest waterfall in the Westfjords. It is not a single drop but a series of tiers flaring outwards as it descends, like a bridal veil, across nearly 100 metres. Its name means « the thunderous one ». It is often called the finest waterfall in the country.

DimensionsClose to 100 metres, in a widening fan
RégionWestfjords (Vestfirðir), away from the Ring Road
AccèsShort uphill path from the car park
À savoirThe detour is long, but this is the wildest region in Iceland
08

When to go and how to link them up

Waterfalls can be seen all year, but the experience changes completely with the season. In été (June to August), daylight barely ends, glacial melt keeps the flow strong, and every Highland track is open: this is the only window for Háifoss or the interior. In hiver, some falls freeze partway, the setting turns spectacular, but side roads and tracks are often shut and paths get icy.

The routing logic is simple: most of the major waterfalls sit along Route 1, the ring road that circles the island. A full loop links the South (Seljalandsfoss, Skógafoss), the South-East (Svartifoss), then the North (Goðafoss, Dettifoss). The Golden Circle (Gullfoss) works as a short return trip from Reykjavík. Only Dynjandi, in the Westfjords, and Háifoss, in the Highlands, call for a genuine detour.
