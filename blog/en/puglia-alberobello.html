01

When to visit Puglia and how to plan it

This article is the first in a series covering our 14-day road trip through Puglia, the heel of the Italian boot. This first leg starts from Alberobello, our base for four days of sightseeing.

Puglia was one of the last corners of Italy we hadn't done. We went in the last week of July. Weather-wise, pack a light jacket for mornings and evenings, it gets chilly. Afternoons, on the other hand, run between 30 and 35 degrees.

People say it's packed in summer. Not really, at least not in the last week of July. Some of the villages we're about to describe were completely empty, especially in the early afternoon. It makes sense: a lot of places close between 1pm and 4pm. Churches and cathedrals are often shut too, even outside the siesta, which is unusual compared with the rest of Italy. If you're going for the beaches, though, bring your boxing gloves. The public beaches are heaving, especially at weekends.

  • When. Last week of July for us. June and September are more comfortable.
  • Base. Alberobello, central and lively in the evening.
  • Length. 4 full days for this loop, without rushing.
  • Car. Essential. Everything happens on small roads between villages.

02

Alberobello and its trulli: what to see, where to stay

We land in Bari fairly late and pick up the car around 11pm. Careful, the rental desks close at 11pm: let them know if you're arriving later, they'll charge you for the wait. An hour's drive and we reach Alberobello.

We'd picked a room in a trullo, slightly outside town but only five minutes away. You get parking, you're close to the centre, and you're well looked after without the downsides. We paid, in July 2026: 612 euros for 5 nights.

Where we stayed
Dimora Sumerano, Alberobello

A converted trullo five minutes from the centre, with parking. 612 euros for 5 nights in July 2026. Hotel bookings are how we fund the site, and they don't cost you a cent more.

The place
Alberobello

Around 1,500 trulli, drystone houses topped with a conical roof, spread across two UNESCO-listed districts. It's central, it's lively in the evening, and there's no shortage of restaurants. Two of our evenings here went to exploring the town, one on each side.

Rione MontiOver 1,000 trulli, shops and restaurants
Rione Aia PiccolaAround 400 trulli, still lived in
Best timeEvening, once the coaches have left
ParkingEasy, the fields have become car parks

Rione Monti, the spectacular side

Start with Rione Monti, the most striking district. Over 1,000 trulli lined up along sloping lanes, their conical roofs crowned with pinnacles and painted with symbols: stars, hearts, signs meant to ward off the evil eye. Most are now craft shops, cafés and restaurants.

Don't miss the Trullo Siamese, two joined domes with a novelistic backstory, and the church of Sant'Antonio, built entirely as a trullo in the 1920s. At the top, the Trullo Sovrano is the only two-storey trullo in Italy, built in the 18th century. By day it's packed. In the evening the mood changes completely: the shops close, the crowd thins out, and the lanes get their charm back.

Rione Aia Piccola, the authentic side

On the other side of the main road, Rione Aia Piccola gathers around 400 trulli, most of them still lived in. No shops, no restaurants, no crowds. This is the UNESCO-listed district plenty of visitors miss. You wander between white houses fronted by little gardens with fig trees and dwarf olives, and climb up to the cathedral at the top of the district. From the Santa Lucia viewpoint, the view across to Rione Monti is superb.

The central promenade

Between the two districts, the central promenade has a stage with music playing almost every evening. That's where Alberobello beats after dark.

Our tip. Visit Alberobello in the evening. You dodge the heat, you dodge the tour coaches, and you get the music. As for parking, every farmer in the area has turned their field into a paying car park, so you'll have no trouble.

03

Visiting Matera and its Sassi (day 1)

Up around 8am. We start with the furthest stops. We could have gone straight to Matera, since the town deserves a full day even at a quick pace. But we stopped at Noci on the way.

Noci

A small town between Alberobello and Matera. You go for a taste of old Italian town life. No shops, few restaurants, just lanes and small squares that drop you straight into it. Allow half an hour to an hour to walk this little medieval town.

Unmissable ⭐ · Outside Puglia
Matera

One of the most astonishing towns in southern Italy. Its Sassi, two cave districts carved into the limestone, form an urban landscape unique in the world, a UNESCO World Heritage Site since 1993. Inhabited since prehistory, these caves housed farming communities until the 1950s, when the government forcibly evacuated the 15,000 people living there in conditions judged unfit.

Since then the town has seen a spectacular revival: the caves have become hotels, restaurants and museums. In 2019, Matera was European Capital of Culture. The contrast between past poverty and present elegance is striking. You may have seen it without knowing: Mel Gibson filmed The Passion of the Christ here (2004), and it's the setting for the opening of No Time to Die (2021).

ParkingParcheggio Piazza Cesare Firrao, covered, 146 spaces, around €1.50 an hour
200 m awayFrom the entrance to the old town
Time neededA full day
BringGood shoes, it climbs and drops constantly

Carrying on from Noci, you cross open fields. Not much to see on the way. We hadn't hired a guide. It was fine, but we'd suggest going for a guided tour: the town is so rich that you'll learn far more than from a paper guidebook.

Book a guided tour of Matera →

Right at the entrance: a lived-in Sasso

As soon as we entered the Sassi, we found a family showing visitors round their cave dwelling. For 5 euros a head, you see where the people of Matera lived, where they stored their wine and kept their animals. These case grotta are the best way to understand life back then. You'll come across several in the lanes of Sasso Barisano, look for the "Casa Grotta" signs.

The route, step by step

Piazza Vittorio Veneto. Start here. It's the main square of the modern town, with the Ferdinandea fountain from the 19th century, the Palazzo dell'Annunziata (a former convent turned library) and the church of San Domenico. From the Guerricchio viewpoint at the edge of the square, you get your first panorama over Sasso Barisano and the Civita with its cathedral. Beneath the square, don't miss the Palombaro Lungo : the largest excavated cistern in Europe. A 30-minute guided tour for €3. This is where you grasp the rainwater collection system, one of the reasons UNESCO listed the Sassi.

Via San Biagio. From the square, take this busy street. It leads to the church of San Giovanni Battista, one of the finest examples of Romanesque architecture in town.

Via delle Beccherie. Carry on along this old merchant street that climbs to Piazza Duomo. Matera's cathedral, built in the 13th century, sits on the highest point of the Civita, the oldest district. Its Romanesque façade is plain, but inside there are restored frescoes and gilded details. From the square, the view drops away over Sasso Barisano.

Down into Sasso Barisano. Head back down from the cathedral into Sasso Barisano, the better restored of the two districts. The lanes are a maze of stairs, passageways and terraces. You'll find the rock church of San Pietro Barisano, its bell tower rising between the rocks. The interior is carved entirely out of tufa. Below the church, a crypt was once used to bury priests.

Crossing to Sasso Caveoso. A walkway follows the Gravina ravine and links the two Sassi. It's the most spectacular stretch in town. Sasso Caveoso is rougher, less restored, with many caves still visible.

Chiesa Santa Maria de Idris. Perched on a rock above Sasso Caveoso, this small rock church is one of Matera's symbols. The medieval frescoes inside are worth the detour. A combined "3 chiese" ticket covers Santa Maria de Idris, Santa Lucia alle Malve and San Pietro Barisano.

Chiesa San Pietro Caveoso. At the bottom of Sasso Caveoso, this church from the 13th century looks straight onto the ravine. From its square the panorama is striking: caves stack up above you, and opposite, the Parco della Murgia Materana unrolls its cliffs.

Back up to the viewpoints. Climb back up the Sasso Caveoso steps to Piazzetta Pascoli, whose viewpoint takes in both Sassi and the ravine. It's the most famous vantage point in town.

Tip. Stop by the tourist office and pick up a map with three walking routes depending on how much time you have. Allow a full day to cover it properly.

Stay a night?

We went for the day from Alberobello, but to make the most of it, two days and an evening would be ideal. The town lit up at night is a spectacle in itself.

An exceptional address
MOYSEION Matera, sleeping in Magna Graecia

If you want to make the trip memorable, this one stands apart. The MOYSEION is a 16-room hotel-museum set in the caves of Sasso Barisano. The idea: let guests live Magna Graecia, the era when southern Italy was colonised by the Greeks.

The highlight is the Water Sanctuary: a two-level thermal grotto inspired by Hellenistic complexes, with heated pools, mosaics and statues of deities. Every afternoon a guided ritual based on the myth of Demeter and Persephone takes place there. The team isn't made of hoteliers but of archaeologists, musicians and dancers, and the project was developed under archaeological supervision.

Adults only, from 16 upwards. Rates start around €149 a night, breakfast, rituals and sanctuary access included. It's expensive, but you're not sleeping in a hotel, you're sleeping in an experience. We haven't tried it ourselves, we're flagging it because it's out of the ordinary.

04

The gravine villages around Matera

If you're staying longer in the area, several villages in the gravine are worth the detour.

  • Castellaneta (7 km from Casalrotto). The old centre perches on the edge of one of the deepest gravine in Puglia, with a drop of nearly 150 metres. Birthplace of Rudolph Valentino, the silent film star: a museum devoted to him occupies the former convent of Santa Chiara.
  • Casalrotto (2 km from Castellaneta). A small cave hamlet with Byzantine frescoes in its rock churches. A stop for lovers of medieval religious art.
  • Mottola (9 km). Another capital of the gravine, known for its rock churches decorated with frescoes from the 11th to the 14th centuries. The church of San Nicola is nicknamed the “Sistine Chapel of cave civilisation” for the Last Judgement painted on its walls.
  • Massafra (23 km). A town split in two by the San Marco gravina. A medieval castle, churches cut into the rock with Byzantine frescoes, and the curious "pharmacy of the Magus Greguro", a cave where a healer prepared his remedies in the Middle Ages.

Our choice. We only stopped at Massafra on the way back. But after Matera, with tiredness setting in and the gap in scale between what we'd just seen and what was in front of us, we didn't linger. Back to Alberobello.

05

Monopoli and Polignano a Mare: beaches and cliffs (day 2)

Fasano

On the road to the Monopoli beaches, a stop at Fasano. Puglia has several kinds of village: the very touristy ones, Alberobello, Locorotondo, and the more authentic ones. Fasano belongs to the latter.

Few shops, few restaurants. You visit it with your eyes up, on the façades and the flowers along them. We had a coffee on a small square, surrounded by locals discovering the tourist lost in their old town.

The old centre is called U'mbracchie in dialect, from the Latin umbraculum, shade. The lanes are so narrow the sun never reaches them. The result: even in high summer it stays cool. At the heart of the maze, Piazza Ciaia with its Clock Tower and the Palazzo del Balì (now the town hall) opens onto two courtyards lined with palaces. And if you push on to the Chiesa Matrice di San Giovanni Battista, its tufa façade from the 17th century and its 28-metre baroque bell tower are worth a look: it's built on the foundations of a temple from the 14th century.

Fasano Zoosafari. For families, this is the second-largest wildlife park in Europe. A 6 km drive-through circuit among 1,700 animals of 200 species: giraffes, lions, elephants and zebras roaming free between century-old olive trees.

Open since 1973, the park also includes Fasanolandia (rides and shows). Allow a full day. Adults around €30 to €34, children aged 4 to 10 around €25, free under 3. Arrive early: the animals are more active and the queues shorter.

Beach at Monopoli: Capitolo

After Fasano, we were meant to stop at Egnazia, the archaeological site just before Monopoli. It's the largest Roman city in Puglia: 30 centuries of history, Messapian walls still standing, the flagstones of the Via Traiana rutted by carts, a forum, an amphitheatre, basilicas and a museum holding the famous "gnathia" pottery made here 2,500 years ago. The site covers 40 hectares by the sea, entry around €3. But old stones under 35 degrees isn't really our thing. And looking closer, you realise you need a guided tour for it to mean anything, otherwise they're ruins with no explanation. We headed for the beaches.

Off to Capitolo, Monopoli's seaside strip. Coming in on the Fasano road, we try the left side: packed rocks. U-turn. On the right are the beach clubs. We stop at a well-rated one: two loungers and a parasol, 160 euros for the day. The club is empty, the public beach is full. You work out why pretty fast. A friendly security guard tells us to go 10 to 15 km further, to the Lido Sabbia d'Oro : private beach, busy but not jammed, loungers at 20 euros a day. A short reading break in the shade and a swim. Two hours is plenty.

Monopoli

After the beach, a quarter of an hour's drive and we reach Monopoli around 1pm, perfect for lunch. Right at the entrance to town we come across Munz Munz biOstrot, a small organic slow food bistro with mamma at the stove and her daughter waiting tables. Short menu, local produce, vegetarian options and homemade dishes. We recommend it. We didn't regret it once we'd seen the restaurants in the centre.

Monopoli, whose name comes from the Greek monos polis (unique city), is one of the coast's tourist villages. Every lane is lined with small shops and small restaurants. It's crowded, and you cover it quickly. You wander the white streets, get lost, come out at the old harbour with its blue boats, head back towards the centre, have a coffee, browse a few shops for the cool air, then move on.

If you have time, don't miss the cathedral of Maria Santissima della Madia, a fine example of Pugliese baroque (18th century), and the castle of Charles V, built in the 16th century to protect the town from the Turks. From its ramparts, the view over the harbour and the sea is superb. Piazza Garibaldi, lined with cafés and historic buildings, is the town's lively heart.

The image of Puglia
Polignano a Mare

The first part of the centre is very touristy: you get lost in the white lanes and emerge above the beach of Lama Monachile, the most photographed cove in Puglia. Wedged between two limestone cliffs, with white pebbles and turquoise water, it's spanned by the Ponte Borbonico, a stone bridge that crosses the void and gives a plunging view over the cove.

Red Bull Cliff DivingEvery summer (August or September), dives from 27 metres
When to comeJune or September to avoid the crowds
To seeArco Marchesale, Terrazza Santo Stefano
DetailLines of poetry painted on the walls and stairways

You walk, you reach the cliff edge, you see the houses opposite, you take photos, you head back into the village. It's charming, well worth the visit despite the tourism everywhere.

You cross the bridge and it's far quieter on the other side. You follow the cliff and arrive at the foot of an imposing statue: that of Domenico Modugno, the singer of "Volare" (Nel blu dipinto di blu), born here on 9 January 1928.

The 3-metre bronze statue, sculpted by Hermann Mejer and unveiled in 2009, shows him with arms open to the sea. One of the most photographed spots in Puglia.

We finish the day around 5pm and head back to rest before setting off for Locorotondo.

06

The Castellana Caves and the Murge plateau (day 3)

Putignano

We start the day at Putignano, a few minutes from the caves. It's a small village up on the Murgia, known above all for its carnival, one of the oldest in Europe (since 1394). Outside that season, the old centre is a maze of limestone lanes, arches and white houses. Less charming than the villages of the previous days, but it lets you have a coffee on one of the squares before tackling the caves.

The highlight of the trip ⭐
The Castellana Caves

Right at the entrance it's an instant wow: the first chamber is enormous, with a fabulous opening above your heads. The biggest jolt we've had in all our cave visits.

Time needed2 hours for about 3 kilometres
LanguagesGerman, Italian, French, Spanish, English
AltitudeOver 210 metres above sea level
Humidity99% in the White Cave

Inside, stalactites balance everywhere. You learn that these vast cavities are among the safest places in an earthquake: the void cancels out the movement. The caves now sit more than 210 metres above sea level, but they were originally underwater. It isn't that the water dropped: the tectonic plates lifted the land over millions of years.

The full tour lasts two hours for about three kilometres. After an hour and a half of scrambling through the various chambers, you reach the White Cave, and you'll have guessed where the name comes from. The fifty metres of rock above strip the seeping water of its impurities and give the stalactites and stalagmites their white colour.

Careful. It's crowded at that point, it's where the different tour groups cross. Humidity reaches 99%.

Castello Marchione

A few kilometres from the caves, a stop at Castello Marchione. It's a former hunting lodge turned function venue for weddings and christenings.

We were on our own, so we got to see the reception hall. A pleasant five-minute stop, and the photo is worth it.

Conversano

A typical small town, quiet, no tourists. We had lunch at the castle restaurant: cheap and good.

The Acquaviva d'Aragona castle dominates the town from Piazza della Concordia. Home to the counts of Conversano for seven centuries, it now houses a picture gallery. Right next door, the cathedral of Santa Maria Assunta is a fine example of Pugliese Romanesque architecture (12th century). Don't miss the monastery of San Benedetto either, founded in 957, whose abbesses held temporal and spiritual power over the local clergy until the 19th century, a case so rare it earned the nickname Monstrum Apuliae, the marvel of Puglia.

Despite the frequently closed churches, Conversano is worth visiting for the calm of its lanes and the absence of crowds.

Cantina Polvanera

After Conversano, we head to Cantina Polvanera for a Primitivo tasting. Very good. Bottles run from 6 to 20 euros.

We'd suggest taking a Polvanera 14 and a Polvanera 16 : the numbers match the alcohol content. The higher the number, the more concentrated and powerful the wine.

Gioia del Colle

Last stop of the day. Gioia del Colle, whose name means “joy of the hill”, is a quiet little town perched on the Murge plateau, halfway between Bari and Taranto. Its Norman-Swabian castle, built in the 9th century then enlarged by Frederick II around 1230, is one of the finest in the region after Castel del Monte. Inside, an archaeological museum displays finds from nearby Monte Sannace. The town is also known for its PDO mozzarella and its Primitivo DOC, though our boot was already full.

We liked the quiet feel of this town going about its life far from tourism. The lanes around the castle are pleasant, the shaded squares invite you to sit down. Back to the hotel around 5pm, then our first evening in Locorotondo.

07

Ostuni, Cisternino and Martina Franca: the white villages (day 4)

Martina Franca

One of the largest towns in the region, and one of the least touristy compared with the previous ones. The lanes run into one another and open onto handsome squares lined with baroque palaces.

You soon reach Piazza Roma where the vast Palazzo Ducalestands, built in 1668 for the Caracciolo family. The inner courtyard is free to enter: frescoes from the 18th century, painted ceilings, columns.

Directly opposite, the Basilica of San Martino imposes its carved baroque façade. Getting lost in the streets, you come across fine wrought-iron balconies, tufa stone façades, and shaded little squares where locals take their coffee. Martina Franca hosts a well-known opera festival every summer.

Cisternino ⭐

Cisternino is one of the most beautiful villages in Italy, awarded the Touring Club's Orange Flag. Handsome white buildings, lanes full of flowers, balconies spilling over with geraniums and bougainvillea. It's small, it's quiet, it's charming.

Piazza Vittorio Emanuele is the heart of the village, with its Norman tower and thechurch of San Nicola. From the Piazza Garibaldi viewpoint, the view over the Itria valley and its trulli is magnificent.

Worth tasting. Cisternino is the capital of bombette : pork rolls stuffed with cheese, grilled in front of you in the butcher-grill houses of the centre. You settle into the shop, choose your meat, and eat at formica tables.

La città bianca ⭐
Ostuni

Ostuni is the white town. And it's superb. The whole old town climbs, a maze of white lanes rising towards the cathedral. From the ramparts and the viewpoints, you watch the olive trees run down to the Adriatic.

Ceglie Messapica

We finish at Ceglie Messapica, nicknamed the “gastronomic capital of Puglia”. The village was founded by the Messapians in the 8th century BC.

You get lost in the medieval maze and come out on a large white square, most likely Piazza Plebiscito. A medieval castle crowns the top of the village. If you're after a good restaurant, this is the place.

08

Locorotondo and the Itria valley after dark

Two of our evenings went to Locorotondo, a few kilometres from Alberobello. The mood is different. No trulli everywhere here. The village, perched at 410 metres, gives a superb view over the Itria valley, its vineyards and olive groves. Its name comes from the Latin locus rotundus, the round place, describing the circular plan of its old centre.

There aren't many lanes, they're white, with shops and restaurants. It's charming. Locorotondo is listed among the most beautiful villages in Italy and holds the Touring Club's Orange Flag for the harmony of its forms.

Parking is a fight. Unlike Alberobello where the fields have become car parks, here you have to battle for a space.

At the highest point of the old centre, thechurch of San Giorgio dominates the village. Built in the 18th century in a style between baroque and neoclassical, its 35-metre dome is visible from across the Itria valley. The interior is spacious, with richly decorated altars and frescoes in the side chapels. Right next door, don't miss the small Chiesa di San Nicola : its plain white façade gives nothing away about the beauty of the frescoes covering its walls and vault.

  • Villa Comunale. The public garden, ideal in late afternoon: views over the trulli, vines and olive groves of the Itria valley.
  • Via Nardelli. Nicknamed the village "lungomare" because it follows the ridge like a seafront promenade, except that here it's countryside below.
  • Piazza Vittorio Emanuele viewpoint. Surrounded by handsome buildings from the 19th century, with views across the whole plain.
  • The wine. Locorotondo produces an excellent DOC white (Verdeca and Bianco d'Alessano), fresh and dry, with citrus and white flower aromas. Worth tasting on the spot.
