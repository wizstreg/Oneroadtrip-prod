Nice Hinterland Circuit: Perched Villages and Mountain Trails

Perched villages, wild bison, the Valley of Wonders, and the Maeght Foundation: 10 days in the Nice hinterland away from the crowds.

Leave the coast behind and discover the Nice hinterland: medieval villages clinging to cliffs, gorges carved through limestone, a safari in southern France's only wild bison reserve, and Bronze Age rock carvings in the Valley of Wonders. This 10-day circuit from Mougins to Nice reveals a radically different Côte d'Azur—just a few kilometers from Cannes's palace hotels.

Key highlights (4)
  • Gourdon: village perched at 760 m with Le Nôtre gardens, panorama spanning 80 km of coastline
  • Monts d'Azur Reserve: 700 hectares of European wildlife at 1,200 m altitude near Grasse
  • Valley of Wonders: 50,000 Bronze Age rock carvings on the slopes of Mont Bégo
  • Tourrettes-sur-Loup: France's only territory where Victoria violets are still cultivated as a main activity

Mougins

The old village of Mougins sits perched at 260 meters above sea level between Cannes and Grasse, its narrow lanes spiraling around the bell tower—a defensive design inherited from medieval times. Explore on foot in the morning before restaurants open and terraces fill: the flower-covered facades, fountains, and art galleries belong entirely to you. At the village entrance, a monumental 500-kg sculpture of Picasso's head greets visitors—a reminder that the cubist master lived here from 1961 to 1973 at Mas Notre-Dame-de-Vie, until his death on April 8, 1973.

The Mougins Photography Centre occupies the former presbytery since 2020, continuing the legacy of the André Villers Photography Museum and preserving its photographic collection. André Villers was Picasso's friend and portraitist—he photographed the master throughout his Mougins years and received as a gift Picasso's famous Rolleiflex camera, which is displayed here. Temporary exhibitions rotate regularly—check the program before visiting.

If you're passionate about art, head to FAMM (Women Artists Museum Mougins), Europe's first private museum entirely dedicated to female artists: works span from the 17th century to present day. The building is recent, the collection ambitious. Meanwhile, the more than 30 galleries and art studios scattered through the lanes make this a true gallery-village.

From April to October, climb to the top of the bell tower for a 360° view from over 300 meters altitude: Cannes Bay, the Lérins Islands, the Grasse hills, and the snow-capped Pre-Alps in the background. It's a panorama that Mougins locals simply call "the view"—which says everything. In summer, nighttime tower visits are organized.

→

The "Étoiles de Mougins" festival, created in 2006 to honor chef Roger Vergé who opened his Moulin de Mougins in 1969 and brought the village to 7 Michelin stars by 1992, takes place every September. Demonstrations, workshops, and culinary competitions animate the lanes for an entire weekend. If you're here during this period, book several months ahead—the event sells out very early.

→

Stroll around the Fontmerle pond in Valmasque park (427 hectares of preserved nature between Mougins and Sophia Antipolis): in summer, blooming lotus flowers create an unexpected spectacle that few visitors know about. A marked trail circles the pond in an easy hour's walk.

→

Mougins gastronomy is no myth—even the village's more accessible establishments work with quality local produce. Take time for lunch on a terrace in the lanes before leaving: the sunshine cuisine dear to Roger Vergé, blending Provençal and Mediterranean influences, has left a lasting imprint on every restaurant in the village.

Grasse, the world capital of perfume since the 17th century, was inscribed on UNESCO's intangible cultural heritage list in 2018 for its savoir-faire. The old town unfolds around stepped lanes and shaded squares—start from Cours Honoré Cresp and climb toward the cathedral, losing yourself in the alleyways. Houses from the 17th and 18th centuries stand alongside 19th-century mansions—the architecture tells the story of a prosperous town.

The International Perfume Museum is the world's largest dedicated to fragrance: created in 1989 and renovated in 2008, its 3,500 m² trace 4,000 years of perfume, cosmetics, and makeup history, from ancient Egypt to today. The multi-sensory journey with over a hundred essences to smell is a unique experience—allow at least 1.5 hours. Admission is affordable and free on the first Sunday of the month from October to April.

Visit one of the historic perfumeries—Galimard founded in 1747 (the oldest), Fragonard (1926), or Molinard (1849): copper stills, enfleurage techniques, creation laboratories, and fragrant boutiques. Tours are free at all three houses. If you're at the Saturday morning market on Cours Honoré Cresp, the stalls of perfumed soaps, olive oils, and regional products are worth the visit alone.

The Cathedral of Notre-Dame du Puy preserves remarkable paintings within its 12th-century Romanesque walls: a Rubens, a Fragonard (The Washing of the Feet), and works by Louis Bréa. It's one of the rare cathedrals where you see masters of this quality without glass cases or barriers—simply push open the door.

→

Take a perfume creation workshop at one of the Grasse houses: guided by a professional "nose," you choose from dozens of essences to compose your personal fragrance. Sessions last between 1 and 2 hours depending on the formula—book ahead as groups are limited. You leave with your personalized bottle.

→

Ten kilometers from Grasse, the MIP Gardens at Mouans-Sartoux are a living conservatory of 700 perfume plant species: May roses, jasmine, tuberose, violets—the same flowers that supply Grasse perfumeries. Labeled "Remarkable Garden" since 2022. If you visit in spring, the flowering rose fields are spectacular.

The Monts d'Azur Biological Reserve offers an experience unmatched anywhere in France: 700 hectares on a plateau at 1,200 meters altitude, where European bison, Przewalski's horses, elk, deer, and wild boar roam in complete freedom. This project was launched in 2006 by veterinarian Patrice Longour, inspired by African reserves—he wanted to recreate on this plateau the "guild" of large European herbivores as it existed several millennia ago. Over 500 species coexist here, including some twenty protected by international conventions. The reserve is 40 minutes from Grasse and 1 hour from Nice—a total surprise for anyone arriving from the coast.

The village of Thorenc itself is a quiet mountain hamlet at 1,250 meters altitude, typical of the Grasse backcountry. Take advantage of your visit to walk along the meadows and forests surrounding the plateau—the contrast with the Côte d'Azur an hour's drive away is striking. In autumn, the deer's roar echoes through the valley.

→

The guided walking safari (1h45) or horse-drawn carriage tour (1h15) is mandatory to enter the reserve: booking is essential as visitor numbers are strictly limited to avoid disturbing the animals. In autumn, the rutting safari lasts 2.5 hours and takes place in the evening—males answer each other across the forest, ears pricked. A naturalist guide accompanies each group. Bring warm clothing even in summer—at 1,200 m, temperatures remain cool.

→

Eco-lodges are available within the reserve for total immersion nights: you observe animals at dusk and dawn from your accommodation, right in the middle of bison territory. It's one of the Alpes-Maritimes' most unusual experiences—book well in advance, spots are rare.

Gourdon is one of France's most spectacular perched villages: clinging at 760 meters to a rocky spur above the Loup gorges, it's part of the "Most Beautiful Villages of France" network. Its origins date back to the 9th century, when the counts of Provence fortified this promontory. Queen Victoria herself made the detour on March 28, 1891—the central square still bears her name. If you arrive early in the morning, you'll have the lanes to yourself before day-trippers invade the village.

Gourdon Castle, an 11th-17th century fortress rebuilt in 1610, impresses with its cylindrical towers and Renaissance gateway. The castle is privately owned and not open for tours, but the French formal gardens designed by André Le Nôtre—the same landscape architect who worked at Versailles—are accessible from April to September. The centuries-old boxwoods trimmed into topiaries, the terraces suspended above the gorges, and the view of the Mediterranean from this balcony are breathtaking.

From Place Victoria, the panorama stretches 80 kilometers without interruption—from Nice to Théoule, Cap d'Antibes, the Lérins Islands, and the Estérel massif. On exceptionally clear days, you can even make out the first contours of Corsica. Look for the orientation table to identify each summit. Paragliders regularly take off from Gourdon—the spectacle from the ground is already dizzying.

Gourdon's lanes are animated by artisan workshops: blown glass, perfumed soaps, hill honey, nougat, and gingerbread. The "Gourdouillat," a local cake with almonds and honey, is sold in the village shops. Don't leave without trying a piece.

→

The Chemin du Paradis (Path of Paradise) connects the hamlet of Pont-du-Loup below to Gourdon village: this former mule trail still used until 1900 climbs 500 meters elevation through Mediterranean vegetation. The name is a promise—the view at the summit rewards the effort. Allow 2 to 2.5 hours round trip from Pont-du-Loup.

→

Drive down to the Loup gorges for the Saut du Loup and Courmes waterfalls: the river has carved natural basins called "giants' kettles" into the limestone. Trails run along the gorges between Pont-du-Loup and Bramafan. In summer, canyoning enthusiasts launch into the pools—local outfitters offer half-day guided trips.

Tourrettes-sur-Loup, nicknamed the "City of Violets," is a medieval village perched on a rocky spur above two valleys. Pass through one of the two access gates to discover the semicircular Grand Rue: the 15th-century stone houses press against each other as if protecting themselves from the void, their flower-covered facades adorned with geranium planters. Populated since prehistoric times, the village is first mentioned under the name "castrum de Torretis" in the 11th century. The current 15th-century castle, built by the Villeneuve family, now houses the town hall.

The Bastide aux Violettes is both museum and working farm: here you discover the history of the Victoria violet, cultivated in Tourrettes since the 1880s to supply Grasse perfumeries. The last three producer families still grow organically on terraced plots. The visit is open year-round, but come from November to March to see the flowers in full bloom—their fragrance is delicate, almost ethereal.

From the orientation table below the village, the view embraces the Cassan viaduct, the valleys, and the Mediterranean coastline. The table also indicates the Pre-Alps peaks to the north. Take a few minutes here—it's one of the quietest and least-known viewpoints in the Nice hinterland.

→

The first weekend of March, the Violet Festival transforms the village into a flower parade: this institution born in 1952 on the initiative of Scottish artist Victor Linton draws nearly 10,000 visitors. Floats covered with fresh violets parade through the lanes, the flower battle takes place on Place de la Libération, and the producers' market overflows with violet specialties. If you're here during this period, arrive early—the village entrance quickly becomes congested.

→

Taste the violet specialties offered in village shops: crystallized flowers coated in sugar, violet syrup, eau de toilette, and perfumed soaps and creams. The crystallized violet flower confection is a fragile and delicate local delicacy—they keep only a few weeks.

→

The medieval village hosts numerous art craftspeople: potters, ceramists, weavers, jewelers, and sculptors fill the lanes. Tourrettes has always attracted artists—the movement accelerated in the 1950s. The small Saturday morning market on Place du Scourédou is an opportunity to discover local creators.

Saint-Paul-de-Vence is one of the Côte d'Azur's best-preserved medieval villages: its 16th-century ramparts, commissioned by François I after he rewarded the village for its resistance against Charles V in 1536, enclose 300 meters of Rue Grande lined with art galleries and blonde stone facades. Arrive early—Rue Grande becomes difficult to navigate after 10am in summer. In the village cemetery, look for Marc Chagall's grave, alley C #152: the painter lived his final years here and died in 1985.

The 17th-century Chapel of the White Penitents was entirely decorated by Belgian artist Jean-Michel Folon between 1997 and 2008: floor mosaics, stained glass, frescoes with winged figures, and a monumental bronze sculpture transform this small space into a poetic world suspended between sky and sea. It's an absolute surprise in this medieval setting—don't miss it.

Walk the Chemin de Ronde on the ramparts for plunging views over the village rooftops and hinterland hills. From the 16th-century bastion, the panorama embraces the Baou chain and Mont Chauve. Below, Place du Général de Gaulle comes alive under centuries-old plane trees: pétanque matches here are legendary—Montand, Ventura, and Lino Ventura regularly competed here in the 1960s.

→

The Maeght Foundation, inaugurated on July 28, 1964, by André Malraux, is France's first private art foundation and one of Europe's most important collections with 13,000 works. Miró, Giacometti, Chagall, and Braque designed the foundation's spaces themselves with Catalan architect Josep Lluís Sert—the Giacometti courtyard, Miró labyrinth, and Chagall's mural mosaics are part of the building. Allow at least 2 hours and book ahead in summer.

→

Head to Tourrettes-sur-Loup 15 minutes away if you missed it on Day 5. Otherwise, Saint-Paul's art galleries deserve a return visit in the evening when the light changes and the lanes empty a bit—some artists keep their studios open until 7pm.

Saint-Jeannet is a perched village nestled at the foot of the Baou—a Provençal word designating a steep rock with a flat summit—about twenty kilometers from Nice. The Queirard quarter, the village's oldest, unfolds its typical "calades": sloping lanes interrupted by steps to combat erosion, a characteristic inherited from Roman roads. The village was long a land of vines and wine; the Rasse family perpetuates this tradition on the route des Sausses toward Gattières.

From the village Panorama, the view extends from Col de Braüs on the Italian border to the Estérel mountains in the west—on clear days, Corsica can be distinguished on the horizon. Artists began settling here in the 1950s, drawn by this tranquility and these views. Georges Ribemont-Dessaignes, Dada poet, lived here from 1944 to 1974 and brought his friends—Tristan Tzara and Jacques Prévert stayed in Saint-Jeannet.

→

The hike to the Baou de Saint-Jeannet is the area's essential excursion: 6 km round trip from the village parking lot for 400 meters positive elevation gain. The summit, at 802 meters altitude, offers a 360° panorama over the Côte d'Azur, Mercantour, and Italy—one of the region's finest panoramic views. The Baou is also a renowned climbing site: dozens of routes have been opened in its limestone cliffs. Leave early in the morning, parking fills quickly in summer.

→

If you prefer a longer circuit, the Castellet trail passes by the ruins of a 14th-century fortified sheepfold and the Baou de la Gaude, with its remains of a Ligurian enclosure and a centuries-old oak measuring 6.7 meters in circumference. Allow 5 hours for the complete loop.

→

Descending back to the village after your hike, refresh yourself at the village fountains—several shade the lanes. Artisans offer herb ravioli to take away: a local specialty not to be missed if you have access to a kitchen tonight.

The Alpha Centre "Le Temps du Loup" (The Time of the Wolf) is the only facility of its kind in France: three natural habitats allow you to observe semi-wild wolves at feeding time. Wolves naturally returned to Mercantour in the 1990s from Italy—the Centre helps explain the biology and behavior of these predators at the heart of a still-fierce debate between herders and naturalists. Book ahead, especially in high season.

Saint-Martin-Vésubie, nicknamed "the little Switzerland of Nice," is Mercantour's tourist capital at 1,000 meters altitude. Wander the alpine village lanes with their wooden chalets, murmuring fountains, and the canal running down the middle of the main street. The village is the gateway to 3,600 hectares of larch forests and alpine pastures, with peaks exceeding 3,000 meters.

If you have time, push on to the Sanctuary of the Madone de Fenestre at 1,903 meters altitude: accessible via a winding road from Saint-Martin (generally open June to October), this medieval pilgrimage site offers a spectacular view of Gélas, the Alpes-Maritimes' highest point at 3,143 meters. The road itself is a spectacle—don't rush.

→

Hike to Lac du Boréon from the hamlet of the same name at 1,473 meters: clear blue waters in a larch cirque, marmots on the rocks, ibex on the ridges. 6 km round trip for a gentle 2-hour climb. Don't leave without checking the weather—afternoon thunderstorms are frequent in summer in Mercantour.

→

Outfitters offer canyoning or aqua-hiking in the crystal gorges around Saint-Martin: jumps, natural slides, and rappels in 8-10°C pools. Family outings are accessible from age 8; expert formulas descend technical gorges. The water is cold year-round.

The Nice-Tende-Cuneo line is one of France's most spectacular: built between 1906 and 1928 through gorges, viaducts, and alpine tunnels, the "Train des Merveilles" (Train of Wonders) connects coast to peaks in 2 hours. If you're coming from Saint-Martin-Vésubie by car, you can leave your vehicle in Tende and take the train for the return to Nice tomorrow—a different way to complete the circuit.

The Museum of Wonders is the essential gateway to the Valley of Wonders: designed by architect Jean-Michel Wilmotte, it presents archaeological collections over 1,200 m² with faithful casts of Bronze Age rock carvings from Mont Bégo dating to 2,000-1,500 BC. Over 50,000 carvings have been recorded—bulls, praying figures, agricultural tools—a unique open-air museum in Europe.

The medieval village of Tende rises in an amphitheater on the hillside: houses 4 to 6 stories tall in local green schist, topped with lauze stone roofs, crowd the paved lanes. Tende only became part of France in 1947—Italian identity remains present in architecture and cuisine. The Collegiate Church of Notre-Dame de l'Assomption, with its 11th-century Romanesque bell tower and 17th-century baroque interior, testifies to the past wealth of the Salt Route.

→

The Valley of Wonders is the major excursion from Tende: leave early from the hamlet of Castérino (1,550 m), gateway to the site. The carvings are freely accessible but a certified guide is strongly recommended to identify them. Snow makes the site inaccessible before early July. Allow a full day—the landscape of alpine lakes and boulder fields, with chamois and marmots, is worth as much as the carvings.

→

If weather or season don't permit the Valley of Wonders, the Fontanalba Valley offers a more accessible alternative with its own rock carvings and alpine lakes. Local guides offer custom trips from Tende.

Nice completes this circuit beautifully: capital of the Côte d'Azur inscribed on UNESCO's World Heritage list in July 2021 as a "winter resort town of the Riviera," it concentrates decades of tourism history in its Belle Époque facades. Start at Cours Saleya and its morning flower market—peonies, roses, anemones, and mimosas depending on season—before restaurants deploy their terraces.

Old Nice (Vieux-Nice) is a labyrinth of ochre and yellow lanes with Genoese and Piedmontese accents, UNESCO-listed since 2021. Place Rossetti with its baroque Sainte-Réparate cathedral and café terraces, the Cours Saleya markets, shaded lanes, and stairs climbing to Castle Hill compose a neighborhood you can wander for hours without tiring.

On Cimiez hill, the Matisse Museum inaugurated in 1963 occupies a 17th-century Genoese villa in the heart of an olive grove: over 600 works trace the painter's journey from his first 1890 drawings to his late-life cut-out gouaches. Matisse came to Nice for the first time in December 1917, drawn by the light, and never left. In the neighboring Franciscan monastery cemetery rest Matisse, Raoul Dufy, and Roger Martin du Gard.

The Marc Chagall National Museum, a stone's throw from Cimiez, houses the artist's largest public collection: 17 monumental canvases of the Biblical Message, stained glass, and mosaics constitute a ensemble commissioned and gifted by Chagall himself to the French state in 1966. The auditorium features Chagall stained glass—if a concert is scheduled the evening of your visit, the setting is unique.

→

Climb Castle Hill—the elevator is free from Quai Rauba Capéu. The 19-hectare park offers 360° panoramas over the Bay of Angels and Old Nice, an artificial waterfall, and ruins of the castle razed by the Savoyards in 1706. Late afternoon, the light on the old town rooftops is particularly beautiful.

→

The Promenade des Anglais, created between 1822 and 1824 with financing from wintering British aristocrats, stretches 7 kilometers along the Bay of Angels. End your circuit in the evening by settling into one of the blue chairs facing the sea—they were designed by craftsman Charles Tordo in 1948 and have become Nice's symbol, even displayed at MAMAC.

→

For your final dinner, dive into Nice cuisine: golden socca sizzling on the griddle, pissaladière with onions and anchovies, herb ravioli, Nice-style daube. Old Nice concentrates dozens of "Cuisine Nissarde" certified restaurants in its lanes—a cuisine distinct from Provençal, inherited from the County of Savoy.
