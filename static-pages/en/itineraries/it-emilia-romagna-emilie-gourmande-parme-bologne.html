Gourmet Emilia: From Parma to Bologna Through the Hills

A 6-day gourmet road trip through Italy's Food Valley: Parma, Modena, Bologna, Brisighella, and Dozza, where UNESCO cathedrals meet traditional balsamic vinegar and medieval hilltop villages.

Emilia-Romagna is Italy's Food Valley, and this 6-day itinerary takes you through its most delicious treasures. From Parma, capital of prosciutto and Parmesan, to Bologna with its 40 km of UNESCO-listed porticoes, passing through Modena's balsamic vinegar cellars and the perched medieval villages of Brisighella and Dozza, every stop blends Romanesque heritage, tastings, and the Italian art of living.

Key highlights (5)
  • Parma: 11th-century Romanesque cathedral and the 1618 wooden Teatro Farnese that could seat 4,000 spectators
  • Modena: cathedral-Ghirlandina-Piazza Grande UNESCO ensemble and traditional balsamic vinegar aged 12 to 25 years
  • Bologna: 40 km of UNESCO porticoes, university founded in 1088, and leaning medieval towers
  • Brisighella: perched village ranked among Italy's most beautiful, with the unique 12th-century Via degli Asini
  • Dozza: open-air museum village with over 100 painted murals and regional wine cellar in the Rocca Sforzesca

Begin your discovery of Parma at Piazza del Duomo, one of Italy's most beautiful squares. The cathedral, built between 1060 and 1073 in pure Romanesque style, welcomes you with its simple facade in pale stone. Look up inside to discover the dome painted by Correggio in the early 16th century—an Assumption of the Virgin so breathtaking it seems to open onto the heavens themselves. Entry is free, so take your time soaking it all in.

Right beside the cathedral stands the octagonal baptistery in pink Verona marble, the work of Benedetto Antelami, begun in 1196. This monument bridges Romanesque and Gothic styles and ranks among Italy's finest baptisteries. Step inside: the frescoes covering the walls and dome are remarkable, and the light filtering through the windows creates an extraordinary atmosphere. Allow about 30 minutes; a combined ticket includes the adjacent diocesan museum.

Head next to Palazzo della Pilotta, just a hundred meters from the main square. This vast 16th-century complex, built by the Farnese family, houses the National Gallery, the archaeological museum, and above all the Teatro Farnese. Built in 1618 by Giovan Battista Aleotti on orders from Duke Ranuccio I, this entirely wooden theater could seat 4,000 spectators—in a city that had only 20,000 residents. It was used just nine times over a century. Almost completely destroyed by bombing on May 13, 1944, it was reconstructed identically in the 1950s. It's the world's first theater with a permanent proscenium arch. They even staged mock naval battles here thanks to a system that could flood the pit.

Don't miss the Basilica of Santa Maria della Steccata, erected in 1521. This Renaissance church shelters an image of the Virgin venerated for centuries in Parma. You'll also see frescoes by Parmigianino in the apse. Entry is free and the visit is quick—allow 20 minutes.

Finish with a stroll through Piazza Garibaldi, the lively heart of the historic center. Entirely paved, it's bordered by the Palazzo del Comune and the Governor's Palace with its clock tower. This is where locals gather for aperitivo. From here, Via Farini—a pedestrian shopping street—leads you to the Teatro Regio, built in 1829 on the initiative of Marie Louise of Austria, Napoleon's wife and Duchess of Parma.

→

Parma is the capital of Italy's Food Valley, and you can't leave town without tasting its two treasures: Parmigiano Reggiano and Prosciutto di Parma. Tour operators offer combined visits to the city outskirts to discover a cheese dairy and a ham curing facility—allow half a day. Alternatively, head to the covered market or gourmet food shops in the center for a more flexible tasting. Pair everything with a glass of local Lambrusco.

💡 Book cheese dairy visits in advance, especially during high season
→

Treat yourself to a walk in the Ducal Park, Parma's grand Italian-style garden located just across the Torrente Parma. Its tree-lined paths, fountains, and small lake make it the perfect spot to digest after all those tastings. The Ducal Palace, once Marie Louise's residence, can be viewed from outside—it now houses the Carabinieri headquarters.

💡 Free access, lovely in late afternoon

Leaving Parma, stop in Reggio Emilia, the city that gave birth to the Italian flag. Head straight to Piazza Prampolini, the city's heart, where the cathedral and town hall stand. Inside this communal palace is the Sala del Tricolore, where on January 7, 1797, representatives from Reggio, Modena, Bologna, and Ferrara adopted the green-white-red flag as the emblem of the Cispadane Republic—forerunner of unified Italy. The room, designed by architect Lodovico Bolognini in 1774, is free to visit. The small adjacent Tricolore Museum traces the flag's history with period documents.

A few steps from Piazza Prampolini, step into the Basilica della Ghiara, one of the region's finest Baroque buildings. Built between 1597 and 1619, it preserves remarkable frescoes and a miraculous image of the Virgin. The contrast with the Romanesque austerity of the neighboring cathedral is striking. Allow 20 minutes.

Arriving in Modena by midday, make your way to Piazza Grande. Modena's cathedral, begun in 1099, is a masterpiece of Lombard Romanesque art, inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List since 1997 along with its bell tower, the Ghirlandina, and the square itself. Inside, don't miss the richly sculpted 13th-century rood screen that separates the nave from the elevated choir above the crypt—quite rare in Italian churches. Cathedral entry is free.

The Torre Ghirlandina, the cathedral's bell tower, rises 86 meters and dominates all of Modena. If you have the energy to climb, the view over the city's terracotta rooftops and surrounding hills is worth the effort. Allow 30 minutes for the ascent and descent.

The Palazzo Ducale, imposing residence of the Este dukes who ruled Modena from 1598 to 1859, sits just a few minutes' walk away. The palace now houses the Military Academy—you can't visit inside, but the facade and courtyard merit a detour. The Este family had made Modena a refined little capital after being forced to leave Ferrara.

→

Modena is the capital of traditional balsamic vinegar PDO, a product that bears no resemblance to the industrial balsamic vinegar sold in supermarkets. Here, grape must (from Trebbiano and Lambrusco) is slowly cooked then aged for 12 to 25 years in batteries of small barrels made from oak, cherry, juniper, and mulberry, stored in house attics. Local producers open their doors to reveal this fascinating process. Book ahead—it's an experience you won't forget.

💡 Booking essential, especially in high season. Some acetaie are in the historic center
→

For lunch or late afternoon, visit the Mercato Albinelli, Modena's historic covered market open since 1931. Under its beautiful halls, you'll find stalls selling cured meats, cheeses, fresh pasta, and of course balsamic vinegar. You can buy on the spot and sit down to eat—a very local way to discover Modenese gastronomy. Try the tortellini, the region's specialty.

💡 Open mornings and early afternoon, closed Sundays

Before leaving Modena province, devote the morning to the Ferrari Museum in Maranello, located 20 km south of Modena. Opened in 1990, this museum traces the history of the Scuderia and the Prancing Horse through legendary Formula 1 cars, prototypes, and GTs. You can also try a driving simulator. If you're a true enthusiast, combine it with the Enzo Ferrari Museum in central Modena, housed in the founder's birthplace—a combined ticket lets you visit both within 48 hours.

Arriving in Bologna by early afternoon, head to Piazza Maggiore, the city's heart. The Basilica of San Petronio dominates the square with its deliberately unfinished facade—construction began in 1390 and it remains the world's fifth-largest church. Inside, you'll find a meridian line traced on the floor in 1655 by astronomer Cassini, stretching 66 meters long. Entry is free.

The Two Towers are Bologna's symbol. The Torre degli Asinelli, rising 97 meters, is Italy's tallest leaning medieval tower still standing. Climb its 498 steps: the panorama over Bologna's red rooftops, the hills, and on clear days the Alps is exceptional. Beside it, the Torre Garisenda, smaller at 48 meters, leans even more than the Tower of Pisa—it's not accessible for safety reasons. In the 12th century, Bologna had over a hundred such towers; about twenty remain.

→

Bologna is the only city in the world where you can walk for kilometers without ever being exposed to rain or sun. Its porticoes, inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 2021, extend over 40 km in the historic center. Let yourself wander beneath these arcades, from Via dell'Indipendenza to Strada Maggiore. For the more athletic, the San Luca portico climbs 3.8 km and 666 arches up to the sanctuary perched on the hill—allow 1h30 round trip.

💡 The San Luca climb is more pleasant in late afternoon when the heat subsides
→

Bologna earned the nickname "la Grassa" (the fat one) for good reason. Lose yourself in the Quadrilatero, the old medieval guild quarter, now a permanent market where the aromas of cheeses, cured meats, and fresh fish mingle. Taste tortellini in brodo—tiny pasta parcels served in capon broth—and mortadella Bologna IGP, far more refined than the deli meat you might know as "bologna." Pair it all with a glass of Pignoletto, the local sparkling white wine.

💡 The Quadrilatero is just steps from Piazza Maggiore; visit mornings for the best products

Bologna deserves a second day. Start at Palazzo dell'Archiginnasio, the former seat of the University of Bologna—founded in 1088, it's the oldest university in the Western world. Dante, Petrarch, and Copernicus studied here. Inside, don't miss the Teatro Anatomico, a room entirely clad in fir wood adorned with sculptures, where medical professors gave anatomy lectures before tiers of students. The atmosphere is both scholarly and slightly macabre.

Continue to Piazza Santo Stefano, one of Bologna's most beautiful squares. The Basilica of Santo Stefano is actually a complex of several interlocking churches and cloisters, built between the 5th and 13th centuries—sometimes called "the seven churches." The medieval cloister, with its Romanesque columns and central basin, radiates absolute serenity. Take time to sit for a moment.

Pass through Piazza Nettuno to admire the Neptune Fountain, sculpted in bronze by Giambologna between 1563 and 1567. The sea god, trident in hand, dominates a basin adorned with mermaids and dolphins. Just behind stands Palazzo Re Enzo, where Emperor Frederick II's son was imprisoned for 23 years after being captured by the Bolognese in 1249.

Wander through the university quarter around Via Zamboni. With 100,000 students—a quarter of the population—Bologna pulses with distinctive energy. Independent bookshops, lively cafes, walls covered in posters: this is where you'll feel why Bologna also bears the nickname "la Dotta" (the learned one). Continue to Palazzo Poggi, the university's current seat, which houses fascinating scientific collections.

If you're seeking a quiet moment, the Baroque church of San Domenico is worth the detour. It houses the Arca di San Domenico, the monumental tomb of Saint Dominic, worked on by renowned sculptors including the young Michelangelo—three statuettes on the tomb are attributed to him. The wooden choir is also remarkable.

→

Bologna is a city best discovered by bike. The historic center is largely flat and pedestrianized, and operators offer rentals by the hour or day. It's the best way to cover the 40 km of porticoes without exhausting yourself, and to easily reach outlying neighborhoods like the Giardini Margherita, the large public park south of the center.

💡 Bike-sharing stations are available throughout the center
→

For complete immersion in Bolognese gastronomy, join a cooking class to learn how to make tagliatelle al ragù—the real ragù Bolognese, a thick and savory sauce served with egg pasta, which bears no resemblance to the "spaghetti Bolognese" found abroad. Operators offer 2- to 3-hour workshops, often in welcoming settings in the historic center.

💡 Booking essential, often full on weekends

Brisighella greets you with an unforgettable silhouette: three rocky pinnacles of selenite dominate the village, each crowned by a monument—the Rocca Manfrediana fortress, the Clock Tower, and the Sanctuary of Monticino. Ranked among Italy's most beautiful villages, this medieval hamlet in Ravenna province has remained remarkably intact. Start with the Via degli Asini, the village's most famous attraction. This covered, elevated street, built in the 12th century, originally served as a defensive rampart before housing the stables of carters who transported gypsum from nearby quarries with their donkeys. Its arched windows, wooden beams, and irregular paving make it unique in Italy.

Climb up to the Rocca Manfrediana, the 14th-century fortress that dominates the village. From up here, the view over the Lamone valley and hills covered with olive groves and vineyards is superb. The fortress houses the Museum of Man and Gypsum, which traces the thousand-year history of mining this mineral that shaped the village and its landscape. Allow 45 minutes for the climb, visit, and panorama.

Descend and take the path leading to the Torre dell'Orologio, perched on the second rocky pinnacle opposite the fortress. This 13th-century tower has a peculiarity: its clock shows only six hours, a historical detail that intrigues every visitor. The view from its summit complements that from the Rocca.

If you have time, make a detour to the Pieve del Tho (Pieve di San Giovanni in Ottavo), a Romanesque church located a few minutes from the center. Erected between the 8th and 10th centuries, it contains reused Roman fragments and ancient frescoes. It can be visited freely on Sunday afternoons; other days require booking.

→

Brisighella sits within the Regional Park of the Vena del Gesso Romagnola, a striking landscape of white gypsum ridges, caves, and forests. Several hiking trails start directly from the village—the most accessible connects the three hills in a loop of about 1h30. For the more motivated, longer paths wind through the gypsum formations with spectacular views over the Romagna hinterland.

💡 Bring good shoes; some passages are steep
→

Brisighella is also a village of flavors. Its extra virgin olive oil PDO "Brisighello," obtained from the Nostrana variety cultivated only on these hills, was the first in Italy to obtain PDO certification in 1996. Local producers offer mill visits with tastings. Pair the oil with cured meats from Mora Romagnola, a local pig breed, and a glass of Sangiovese DOC.

💡 Mills operate from November to December, but tastings are possible year-round

Dozza is a museum-village in the literal sense. Ranked among Italy's most beautiful villages, this small medieval hamlet perched on a hill between Bologna and Imola has become an open-air art gallery thanks to the Painted Wall Biennial, launched in 1960. Over 200 Italian and international artists have painted house facades, arches, and walls over the decades. The works are everywhere: look up, peer into corners, venture down alleys. Among the best-known are "The Angel of Dozza" by Giuliana Bonazza (1993) and "The Rainbow" by Alfonso Frasnedi (2000). The village consists of two parallel streets, Via XX Settembre and Via De Amicis—you can't get lost.

At the top of the village rises the Rocca Sforzesca, a fortress built in 1245 whose current appearance dates from work by Caterina Sforza starting in 1480. She added the two large round corner towers with machicolations. Inside, you'll discover rooms furnished with furniture from the Campeggi and Malvezzi families who inhabited the fortress until 1960, dungeons and oubliettes, and a picture gallery preserving sketches of the wall frescoes. From the rampart walk, the view over the valley vineyards and hills is remarkable.

In the Rocca's cellars, don't miss the Enoteca Regionale dell'Emilia-Romagna, installed here since 1970. It's the showcase for wines from across the region—over 1,000 labels from 200 producers. You can taste on-site using a self-service system, or simply buy a bottle to take home. The setting of vaulted cellars adds to the pleasure. Try the Sangiovese di Romagna, Albana DOCG (the first Italian white to obtain DOCG status), and Pignoletto.

→

If you visit Dozza in September of an even year, you'll catch the Painted Wall Biennial in full swing: over a weekend, artists paint live on the village facades, before the public's eyes. It's a unique moment when you can watch a work being born and chat with its creator. Even in odd years, the village hosts cultural events and wine tastings.

💡 Download the "Muro Dipinto" app to identify each fresco with its artist and year
→

To close this itinerary in style, treat yourself to lunch at one of the village's restaurants. Romagna cuisine takes center stage: fresh pasta, grilled meats, piadina (Romagna flatbread), and local cheeses. Tables are set on terraces as soon as good weather arrives, with views over the hills. Pair the meal with a glass of regional wine—you're in the heart of the Strada dei Vini e Sapori of the Colli d'Imola.

💡 The village is small; restaurants fill up fast on Sundays—arrive early
