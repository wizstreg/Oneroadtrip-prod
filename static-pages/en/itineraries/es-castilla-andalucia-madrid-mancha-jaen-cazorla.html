Madrid to Cazorla: La Mancha, the Sierras of Jaén, and the Forgotten Renaissance

From the Prado to Cazorla Park in 9 days: Madrid, Almagro, the Caliphate castle of Burgalimar, UNESCO sites Úbeda and Baeza, Río Borosa gorges, and the perched villages of the Sierra de Segura.

This 9-day itinerary crosses Spain's great tourist void: the zone between Madrid and Andalusia that nobody visits. Starting from the Prado and Reina Sofía museums, you'll cross Don Quixote's La Mancha to the only intact Golden Age theatre at Almagro, cross the Sierra Morena via Despeñaperros, discover the twin Renaissance towns of Úbeda and Baeza (UNESCO), and finish in Spain's largest natural park, among hilltop villages and spectacular gorges.

Key highlights (5)
  • Madrid: the Prado (8,000 paintings, Velázquez's Las Meninas) and the Reina Sofía (Picasso's Guernica, 7.76 metres wide)
  • Almagro: Corral de Comedias from 1628, the only intact Golden Age theatre still active in the world
  • Úbeda and Baeza: two UNESCO Renaissance towns, Vandelvira masterpieces among the olive groves
  • Baños de la Encina: Burgalimar Caliphate castle (968), one of Europe's best-preserved Muslim fortresses
  • Cazorla Park: 210,000 hectares, source of the Guadalquivir, Río Borosa gorges, and hilltop villages

Begin your Madrid stay along the Paseo del Arte, Europe's most concentrated museum triangle. The Prado Museum, founded in 1819 in a neoclassical building by Juan de Villanueva, houses over 8,000 paintings—only a fraction of which are on display. Velázquez's Las Meninas (1656), Hieronymus Bosch's Garden of Earthly Delights (circa 1500), Rubens's The Three Graces, and Goya's Black Paintings await you in the main galleries. Allow at least 2 hours for the major works, longer if you're a painting enthusiast. Arrive at opening time to avoid the queues—they lengthen quickly after 11am.

A ten-minute walk south along the Paseo del Prado brings you to the Reina Sofía Museum, housed in the former 18th-century San Carlos Hospital. The museum opened in 1992 and covers Spanish art from 1881 onwards. The absolute star is Picasso's Guernica (1937)—7.76 metres wide, painted in response to the bombing of the Basque village by the German Condor Legion during the Civil War. The room is always crowded, but the canvas is so immense you can see it well from anywhere. Don't miss Dalí's The Great Masturbator and Miró's canvases on the upper floors.

For a breather between museums, cross the street to Parque del Retiro—125 hectares of greenery right in central Madrid. The park was created in the 17th century as the garden of the Buen Retiro Palace, residence of Philip IV. Rent a rowing boat on the Estanque Grande, facing the Alfonso XII monument, then head to the Crystal Palace (1887), a glass-and-iron greenhouse inspired by London's Crystal Palace, which hosts contemporary art exhibitions from the Reina Sofía. Entry is free.

→

In the late afternoon, head to Plaza de Santa Ana for a tapas crawl through the Las Letras neighbourhood—the literary quarter where Cervantes, Lope de Vega, and Quevedo lived in the 17th century. The tapas bars around the square and along Calle de las Huertas serve generous portions of tortilla, croquetas, and jamón ibérico. Wash it down with a glass of Ribera del Duero or a vermut de grifo (vermouth on tap), Madrid's traditional aperitif drink.

💡 The bars along Calle de las Huertas and around Plaza de Santa Ana have the best addresses

Devote the morning to the Royal Palace, official residence of the Spanish monarchs—though the royal family no longer lives there. Built between 1738 and 1764 on the site of the former Habsburg Alcázar destroyed by fire, it's the largest palace in Western Europe by floor area: 135,000 m² and 3,418 rooms. You'll visit the state rooms—the Throne Room with its Tiepolo frescoes, the Gala Dining Room with its 140-seat table, and the Royal Armoury, one of the world's most complete collections with armour belonging to Charles V and Philip II. Allow 1.5 to 2 hours.

Leaving the palace, cross Plaza de la Armería to the Almudena Cathedral. Begun in 1883, it wasn't completed and consecrated until 1993 by Pope John Paul II—over a century of construction. The interior is surprisingly modern, with brightly painted ceilings that contrast sharply with the sobriety of the neoclassical façade. Climb to the dome for views over the palace and Casa de Campo.

Walk down to Plaza Mayor via Calle Mayor. This arcaded rectangular square, completed in 1619 under Philip III, was the scene of Inquisition autos-da-fé, bullfights, and public executions. Today, the terraces beneath the porticos are tourist traps—just admire the architecture and the equestrian statue of Philip III at the centre, then head to Mercado de San Miguel, just next door. This covered market from 1916, with its iron-and-glass structure, has become a gourmet temple: oysters, jamón ibérico, churros, vermouth, and creative tapas.

Head up to Gran Vía, Madrid's main commercial artery, cut through between 1910 and 1929. The Edificio Metrópolis (1911), at the corner of Calle de Alcalá, with its gilded dome and winged Victory, is the city's postcard image. Continue west to the Templo de Debod, an authentic Egyptian temple from the 2nd century BC, gifted by Egypt to Spain in 1968 in thanks for its help saving the Nubian temples threatened by the Aswan Dam. At sunset, the temple's reflections in the pool with Casa de Campo in the background offer one of Madrid's most beautiful views.

→

If you're in Madrid on a Sunday morning, don't miss El Rastro, Spain's largest flea market. It spreads out every Sunday from 9am to 3pm around Calle de la Ribera de Curtidores, in the La Latina neighbourhood. Antiques, vintage clothing, books, vinyl, curiosities—it's a joyful bazaar that attracts Madrileños as much as visitors. After the market, the tapas bars of La Latina are mobbed—it's Madrid's Sunday ritual.

💡 El Rastro only happens on Sundays. The rest of the week, La Latina neighbourhood remains pleasant for tapas
→

In the evening, catch a flamenco show. Madrid isn't Andalusia, but the city has a historic flamenco scene—many Andalusian artists have settled here since the 19th century. Tablaos in the La Latina and Lavapiés neighbourhoods offer intimate performances with singing, guitar, and dance. Allow 1 to 1.5 hours for a show.

💡 Book in advance, especially on weekends—the tablaos are small and fill up quickly

Two hours' drive south from Madrid and you enter the heart of La Mancha—Don Quixote's plains. Almagro, declared a Historic-Artistic Ensemble, is a complete surprise in this landscape of windmills and fields. The Plaza Mayor is its jewel: a rectangular square bordered by two stone-arcaded galleries with 81 columns, and characteristic green-painted glazed balconies—a legacy of the German Fugger bankers (los Fúcares in Spanish) who settled here in the 16th century to manage the Almadén mercury mines on behalf of Charles V.

The Corral de Comedias is the reason for your visit to Almagro. Built in 1628 by Leonardo de Oviedo in the former Mesón del Toro, it's the only Spanish Golden Age theatre preserved intact and still active in the world. Its 300 m² rectangular courtyard, its two-storey wooden galleries, its cazuela (balcony reserved for women), and its tablado (stage) transport you directly to the era of Lope de Vega, Calderón de la Barca, and Tirso de Molina. Closed in the 18th century by royal decree, it was rediscovered by chance in 1954 beneath the partitions of a boarding house. Every summer, the International Festival of Classical Theatre revives Golden Age plays there.

The Museo Nacional del Teatro, housed in the 13th-century Maestral Palace of the Knights of Calatrava, is Spain's only national museum devoted to theatre. It traces the history of performing arts from the Middle Ages: set models, period costumes, manuscripts, puppets, and posters. An entire section is dedicated to the corrales de comedias and theatrical life during the Golden Age.

The Almacén de los Fúcares—the Fuggers' warehouse—is an imposing 16th-century building that served as a grain store and mercury warehouse for the Augsburg bankers. These financiers were so powerful they lent money to Charles V in exchange for the Almadén mercury monopoly. The building, sometimes called a palace because its construction is so refined, now hosts exhibitions. Just next door, the Church of San Agustín, declared of Cultural Interest, deserves a look for its Renaissance cloister.

→

The gastronomy of Almagro and the Campo de Calatrava belongs to the heart of La Mancha. Try the berenjenas de Almagro—small round aubergines pickled in vinegar with cumin and chilli, a local speciality with its own protected designation. Add to that migas manchegas (fried breadcrumbs with chorizo), pisto manchego (a local ratatouille), and a glass of Valdepeñas wine from the neighbouring appellation.

💡 If you're in Almagro in July, the International Festival of Classical Theatre transforms the town for a month

The road south from Almagro crosses the Sierra Morena through the Despeñaperros gorge—the natural frontier between Castile and Andalusia. This narrow pass between quartzite cliffs has been the gateway to Andalusia for centuries: Roman armies, Visigoths, Moors, and Christian armies all passed through. Stop at the viewpoint for a view over the gorges, then detour to the Battle of Las Navas de Tolosa Interpretation Centre in Santa Elena. On 16 July 1212, the Christian coalition led by Alfonso VIII of Castile crushed the Almohad army here—a battle that reversed the course of the Reconquest and definitively opened Andalusia to the Christian kingdoms.

Baños de la Encina appears among the olive groves, dominated by the spectacular silhouette of Castillo de Burgalimar. This Caliphate fortress, built in 968 on the orders of Caliph Al-Hakam II of Córdoba, is one of the best-preserved Muslim fortresses in Europe. Its 14 towers and its wall of tapial (rammed earth, lime, and stone) still stand at their original height—a millennium later. A 15th tower, the Keep, was added by the Christians after the reconquest. It's called 'the fortress of seven kings' because seven monarchs passed through, from Alfonso VII to Ferdinand the Catholic. Climb the 18-metre keep: the panorama over the endless sea of olive trees is one of the most striking images in the province of Jaén.

Coming down from the castle, stroll through the streets of Baños de la Encina. Unlike Andalusia's white villages, here the façades are ashlar stone—16th and 17th-century manor houses with carved coats of arms line the streets. The Church of San Mateo (15th century), in late Gothic style, dominates the main square. Don't miss the anthropomorphic tombs carved into the rock near the Molino del Santo, vestiges of a Visigothic past.

→

Baños de la Encina sits at the heart of the world's largest olive grove—the province of Jaén alone produces about 20% of the world's olive oil. Local operators offer visits to olive groves and oil mills with tastings. You'll learn to distinguish the Picual variety (the most planted here, intense and fruity) from Arbequina, and to recognise a 'virgen extra' from a simple 'virgen'. It's an experience that permanently changes your relationship with olive oil.

💡 The olive harvest runs from November to February—it's the best time to see the oil mills in action

Baeza is one of the two towns inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 2003 for their Renaissance ensembles. But before becoming a 16th-century jewel, it was a stronghold of the Reconquest: Ferdinand III conquered it in 1227, and it became Castile's forward base facing the Kingdom of Granada for two centuries. Begin at Plaza del Pópulo, the civic heart of the town. The Fuente de los Leones (Lion Fountain), which resembles the one in the Alhambra, is actually an Ibero-Roman remnant from the ancient city of Cástulo. Around the square, the old slaughterhouse (Antigua Carnicería) and the Villalar Arch complete a harmonious ensemble.

Climb to Plaza de Santa María, the religious and educational heart of Baeza. The Cathedral of the Nativity, rebuilt in the 16th century on a former mosque, blends Gothic, Renaissance, and Baroque. Its main façade is sober, but the interior surprises with the brightness of its vaults and the richness of its chapels. Just opposite, the Palacio de Jabalquinto is Baeza's most photographed monument: its Isabelline façade, covered with carved stone diamond points, is a masterpiece of stone lacework from the 15th century. It now houses the headquarters of the International University of Andalusia.

The Old University of Baeza, founded in 1538, is an austere Renaissance building with a beautiful arcaded courtyard. The poet Antonio Machado taught French there between 1912 and 1919—his classroom has remained intact, with his desk, blackboard, and student benches. It was here that he wrote part of his Campos de Castilla. On the wall, a plaque quotes his verses: 'En estos campos de la tierra mía...' Entry is free and the emotion is real.

Finish with a walk along the Paseo de la Constitución and the ramparts, from where the view embraces the olive fields stretching to the horizon. The sunset over this green and silver sea is a daily spectacle that Baeza's inhabitants never tire of watching.

→

Baeza is a town of tapas and olive oil. The bars around Plaza de la Constitución and Calle San Pablo serve generous portions drizzled with Sierra de Cazorla PDO olive oil. Try the ochíos (olive oil bread typical of Jaén), pipirrana (cold salad of tomato, pepper, and cucumber), and revuelto de ajetes (scrambled eggs with tender garlic). Olive oil here isn't a condiment—it's a main ingredient.

💡 Baeza and Úbeda are 9 km apart—you can easily dine in one and sleep in the other

If Baeza is the ecclesiastical and university town, Úbeda is the noble and palatial one—the other half of the UNESCO inscription. It all begins at Plaza Vázquez de Molina, considered one of Spain's most beautiful Renaissance squares. Francisco de los Cobos y Molina, Secretary of State to Charles V, and his nephew Juan Vázquez de Molina, who succeeded him under Philip II, had an ensemble of palaces and chapels built here that rival anything in Tuscany. The architect Andrés de Vandelvira, a native of the region, designed most of the buildings—his influence would later be found in Latin American cathedrals.

The Sacra Capilla del Salvador, commissioned by Francisco de los Cobos as a private funerary chapel, is Vandelvira's masterpiece in Úbeda. Completed in 1559, its Plateresque façade displays extravagant sculptural richness—mythological scenes, grotesques, atlantes, the entire vocabulary of the Italian Renaissance translated into Andalusian stone. Inside, Vandelvira's sacristy is a jewel of proportion, with its caryatids and atlantes framing the windows. Berruguete's monumental altarpiece was partially destroyed during the Civil War, but what remains is impressive.

Opposite the chapel, the Palacio del Deán Ortega (16th century), now a Parador hotel, deserves a visit even if you're not staying there—go in to see the two-level arcaded Renaissance courtyard and the gallery with views over the olive groves. At the other end of the square, the Palacio de las Cadenas (Palace of the Chains), former palace of Juan Vázquez de Molina, now serves as the town hall. Its façade is a model of Spanish purist Renaissance.

Head down to the old quarter to discover the Sinagoga del Agua, discovered by chance during renovation work in 2007. This underground site comprises a prayer hall, a ritual bath (mikveh), a women's gallery, and a well—the most complete medieval synagogue ensemble found in Spain. The guided tour is fascinating and illuminates the life of Úbeda's Jewish community before the 1492 expulsion.

→

The alfarero quarter (potters' quarter) of San Millán, in the lower part of town, perpetuates a pottery tradition dating back to Moorish times. The workshops produce pieces in emerald green on a white background—Úbeda's typical style. Push open a workshop door to see the artisans at work, and leave with a dish or jar as a souvenir. It's one of the few places in Spain where traditional pottery is still a living craft, not a tourist attraction.

💡 The alfarero quarter is at the bottom of town—go down via Calle Valencia. Workshops generally close between 2pm and 5pm

The road from Úbeda to Cazorla crosses a sea of olive trees stretching as far as the eye can see—the province of Jaén has over 66 million olive trees. Cazorla is a white village clinging to a hillside at the foot of the natural park. Plaza de Santa María, one of the two main squares, is built around the spectacular ruins of the Iglesia de Santa María—a 16th-century Renaissance church bombed during the Civil War and never rebuilt. Its open-air arches, with the mountain backdrop, create a unique theatrical setting.

The Castillo de la Yedra (Ivy Castle), of Arab origin then enlarged by the Christians, dominates Cazorla from its rocky spur. It houses the Museo de Artes y Costumbres Populares del Alto Guadalquivir—an ethnographic museum tracing rural life in the sierras. From the ramparts, the view plunges over Cazorla's rooftops and the first forests of the natural park. It's also an excellent spot to watch griffon vultures soaring above the gorges.

Two kilometres from Cazorla, the village of La Iruela has one of the most spectacular sites in the province: a castle perched on a rocky pinnacle so narrow it seems to defy gravity. The adjoining ruined church, clinging to the same rock, reinforces the vertiginous impression. The climb is short but steep—the view from the summit embraces the entire Cerezuelo valley and the distant sierras.

→

Cazorla is the ideal starting point for exploring the natural park, but it's also a town where you eat well. The restaurants around Plaza de la Constitución serve game (deer, wild boar), Segureño lamb, seasonal mushrooms, and of course Sierra de Cazorla PDO olive oil on everything. Rin-ran (cod, tomato, and pepper salad) is the local speciality. Dine early—mountain restaurants close earlier than in the city.

💡 The car park at the top of the village near the castle is often full—park at the bottom and walk up

Full day in the Sierras de Cazorla, Segura y las Villas Natural Park—at 210,000 hectares, it's Spain's largest protected area and Europe's second largest. Declared a Biosphere Reserve by UNESCO in 1983, it's home to over 2,170 plant species including 34 endemics (Cazorla violet, the world's smallest narcissus, a carnivorous plant). Wildlife includes ibex, deer, wild boar, mouflon, griffon vultures, golden eagles, and the rare bearded vulture.

The Río Borosa hike is the park's flagship trail—and one of Andalusia's most beautiful. From the Torre del Vinagre visitor centre, the path follows the Borosa, the Guadalquivir's first major tributary, for about 10 km one way. You first cross the Cerrada de Elías, a narrow gorge where the path is carved into the rock above the turquoise water—wooden walkways span the torrent. Higher up, the path passes through forests of centuries-old pines before reaching two mountain lakes, Laguna de Aguas Negras and Laguna de Valdeazores. Allow 6 to 7 hours return. Bring water, food, and good shoes—there's no refreshment along the trail.

→

If you prefer a less physical option, operators offer 4x4 excursions through the park, with stops at viewpoints and wildlife observation points. The Tranco route follows the large dam and crosses pine forests with spectacular views. You can also take a boat trip on the Embalse del Tranco—the artificial lake at the heart of the park. It's from the lake that you have the best chances of seeing deer coming to drink at dusk.

💡 4x4 excursions generally leave in the morning—book the day before with operators in Cazorla

Final stop: Segura de la Sierra, ranked among Spain's Most Beautiful Villages, perched at 1,230 metres altitude in the park's northeast corner. The village of Muslim origin (conquered in 781 under the name Saqura) has preserved its steep alleyways, vaulted passages, and end-of-the-world atmosphere. The castle, built by the Almohads then transformed by the Order of Santiago in the 13th century, dominates the village from its rocky outcrop. Its 18-metre keep offers a 360° panorama over the sierras, pine forests, and olive groves. The Arab baths, discovered beneath the village, are among the best preserved in the province.

Segura is also the birthplace of the poet Jorge Manrique (15th century), author of Coplas por la muerte de su padre—one of Spanish literature's most famous poems. His father, Don Rodrigo Manrique, was commander of Segura for the Order of Santiago. The poet's birthplace, in the upper part of the village, is open to visitors. Wander the alleyways looking for details: carved coats of arms, Charles III's Imperial Fountain, the Catena Gate, and remnants of the old ramparts.

If you have time before leaving, detour to Hornos de Segura, another spectacular hilltop village 30 minutes' drive away. Its ruined castle dominates the Tranco lake—the view from the village terraces over the lake and mountains is one of the park's most photographed. The Cosmolarium, installed in the castle tower, is a small astronomical observatory and planetarium that takes advantage of the Sierra de Segura's excellent night sky quality.

→

Segura de la Sierra produces a Sierra de Segura PDO olive oil, considered one of Spain's finest. The mountain terroir (700-1,200 m altitude) and the Picual variety produce an intense oil with notes of fresh grass and green tomato. Village cooperatives offer tastings. Leave with a bottle—it's the best souvenir you can bring back from the province of Jaén.

💡 From Segura, you can continue to Albacete (2h), Murcia (2h30), or return towards Jaén/Granada (2h)
