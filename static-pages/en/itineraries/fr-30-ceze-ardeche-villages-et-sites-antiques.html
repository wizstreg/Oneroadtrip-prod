Cèze Valley and Ardèche Gorges Circuit

Medieval villages perched above the Cèze, humanity's oldest cave paintings at Chauvet, UNESCO's Pont du Gard and Nîmes arenas — 15 days through garrigue, gorges and Roman Antiquity.

From the capital of the Cévennes to Nîmes' arenas, this 15-stage circuit follows the Cèze valley from east to west through villages perched on limestone peaks, gorges carved over millennia, and some of Europe's best-preserved Roman heritage. The Aven d'Orgnac, Grotte Chauvet 2 and Pont du Gard mark a route where each day brings a different era to life.

Key highlights (4)
  • Montclus: classified medieval village in a Cèze meander, 32-meter square keep of the Counts of Sabran (1275)
  • Grotte Chauvet 2: replica of the world's oldest known cave paintings, dating back over 36,000 years, UNESCO-listed
  • Pont du Gard: world's tallest Roman aqueduct bridge (49 m), part of 50 km aqueduct supplying Nîmes from Uzès in the 1st century
  • Nîmes: 1st-century arena for 25,000 spectators, Maison Carrée temple UNESCO-listed 2023 and Jardins de la Fontaine

Alès is your gateway to this circuit — the SNCF railway station connects it directly to Nîmes (45 min) and Lyon. As the second-largest city in Gard and the capital of the Cévennes, it deserves half a day before heading towards the Cèze. Start with the so-called 'Vauban' fort — an important detail: Vauban himself never set foot in Alès. The fort was built in 1688 following plans by his engineers, on the Roque hill that had dominated the town since the 10th century. Its mission was clear: house Louis XIV's royal garrison and keep watch over the Cevenol Protestants after the revocation of the Edict of Nantes. It boasts 350 meters of ramparts, 6 bastions and 40 cannon embrasures, all facing the town — not outward, which speaks volumes about the intentions. Converted into a prison until 1990, it's now open for guided tours.

Saint-Jean-Baptiste Cathedral stands as another witness to the Wars of Religion. Louis XIII besieged Alès in 1629 — the town, a Protestant resistance stronghold, capitulated after nine days. It was Richelieu who signed the Peace of Alès on June 28, 1629, confirming religious guarantees for Protestants but stripping them of their fortified places. The church was then partially destroyed. Reconstruction stretched from the 17th to the 18th century in a Southern Gothic style — austere due to lack of funds in a still predominantly Protestant region. Its bell tower remains one of the visual landmarks of the town center.

Don't leave Alès without visiting the Colombier Museum, housed in a former mansion just steps from the train station. On the ground floor, an archaeological collection traces excavations from the Ermitage site on the town's heights; on the first floor, the Fine Arts section offers a panorama of paintings from the 16th to 18th centuries. Free admission.

→

La Mine Témoin (The Witness Mine) is Alès' quirky attraction not to be missed if you have time. Founded in 1945 to train young miners, it comprises 700 meters of underground galleries — the largest visitable mining network in France. Descend with a guide deep into the mine, wearing a helmet, to understand the working conditions of the thousands of men who extracted Cevenol coal. Theatrical evening tours are offered on certain Wednesdays. Allow 1h30.

→

In summer, the Abbey Halls in the heart of town host the food market Monday through Saturday with about forty local producers. It's the perfect opportunity to assemble a picnic for the road: Pélardon cheese from the Cévennes, chestnut honey, charcuterie and seasonal vegetables.

Montclus

Your first love-at-first-sight moment of this circuit: arriving in Montclus, the village emerges in a meander of the Cèze, clinging to its limestone spur, surrounded by vineyards and lavender. Not a single power pole visible, no modern construction — that's why Les Plus Beaux Villages de France labeled it in 2012. The Latin mention 'Castrum Montecluso' appears in a document from 1275: the name literally means 'castle enclosed by mountains,' which perfectly describes the site. The 32-meter square keep is the remnant of a castle built that same year, 1275, by the Counts of Sabran, one of the most powerful families in Provence. Climb up to it to measure the height of the cliff above the river.

Descending towards the river via the Arceau street — the medieval entrance to the castle, vaulted — look for the Beaumes site, away from the village: a vast hall carved into the rock, remnant of a 12th-century troglodyte Benedictine monastery, which later served as a chapel for the Templars. It's one of the valley's least-known curiosities. Check with the tourist office about access conditions.

The Mill Bridge, built in 1870, is the first crossing point of the Cèze since Barjac — before its construction, people crossed from one bank to the other by paid ferry, men and animals together. From the bridge, the view of the perched village with its keep and white stone houses is the one you see on postcards. It's also the access point to the Cèze's pebble beaches for a swim.

→

The Dent du Serret hike starts from the village: an accessible trail leading to one of the best viewpoints over the Cèze gorges, between forests and limestone cliffs. Allow 1h30 round trip for a 4 km loop. Easy to moderate level. On clear days, the view extends to the Ardèche reliefs.

→

The Cèze at Montclus is ideal for canoeing and swimming in summer. The waters are clear and the summer flow moderate. Operators offer equipment rental in neighboring villages — check with the Cèze Cévennes tourist office for the nearest rental points.

Barjac

Barjac is a Renaissance town classified as a 'Village de Caractère,' perched on a rocky spur between the Cèze and Ardèche gorges. Its history is marked by the Wars of Religion — the town oscillated several times between Protestantism and Catholicism, and Louis XIII stopped here on the road to the Peace of Alès in 1629. The Château des Comtes du Roure is the central monument: it actually consists of three buildings from different eras, the oldest being a seigneurial tower dated to the second half of the 10th century. The new dwelling, built between 1634 and 1639, is where Richelieu stayed. Owned by the municipality since 1982, it now houses the town hall, library and cultural halls. Go up to the terrace — the view over the Cévennes is breathtaking.

On the old main square, the Renaissance Tower is one of the village's architectural jewels: slender and narrow, it was most likely built in the 16th century, and its singular form makes it instantly recognizable. Seven other towers punctuate the tour of the old center, including that of the Bellet mansion (1580) with its spiral staircase. The ramparts from 1379 left some vestiges, and in the walls of houses, you can spot Roman sculptures salvaged from the Gallo-Roman estate of Malhac, rediscovered in the 1860s.

The Capuchin chapel, established since 1633 to bring the town back to Catholicism, and Saint-Laurent church complete the heritage tour. Don't miss the market hall and the market square with its century-old plane trees — every Friday morning, Barjac's Provençal market extends over almost the entire village, and ranks among the most renowned in northern Gard.

→

About ten kilometers from Barjac, the Aven d'Orgnac is one of France's Grand Sites: gigantic concretions several dozen meters high in chambers reaching 250 meters long. The adjoining Cité de la Préhistoire traces 350,000 years of human occupation in the valley. Allow half a day to combine both visits. Book online in high season.

→

Each year in late July, the courtyard of Barjac castle transforms into a stage for 'Barjac m'en chante,' a French chanson festival with intimate atmosphere. The antiques fairs (Easter and autumn) and the Friday organic market are unmissable appointments in local life.

Vallon Pont D Arc

The Pont d'Arc is one of France's most spectacular geological formations: a natural arch 54 meters high and 59 meters wide, carved by the Ardèche river through Urgonian limestone over approximately 124,000 years. It's the only case in France of a stone arch spanning a still-active river. From the parking areas a few hundred meters away, pedestrian paths descend to the foot of the arch. In July-August, the free shuttle from Vallon-Pont-d'Arc is the most practical solution — access roads are saturated and the site very busy. Come early morning or late afternoon to find the most beautiful light.

The Grotte Chauvet 2, opened in 2015, is the life-size replica of the Chauvet Cave discovered on December 18, 1994 by three Ardèche speleologists. The original cave, inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage list since 2014, houses the oldest known cave paintings: more than 425 drawings and paintings created over 36,000 years ago, twice as old as Lascaux. The replica occupies 15 hectares in a wooded park and faithfully reproduces the galleries. The guided tour lasts about 1h30 — absolutely book online, especially in July-August.

The tourist road through the Ardèche Gorges links Vallon-Pont-d'Arc to Saint-Martin-d'Ardèche over 28 km with 11 developed viewpoints. Even by car, this panoramic route gives the measure of the canyon: limestone cliffs plunging 200 to 300 meters above the river. Stop at the Madeleine viewpoint or the Rocher des Deux Portes for the most vertiginous views.

→

The descent of the Ardèche gorges by canoe-kayak from Pont d'Arc to Saint-Martin-d'Ardèche (24 km, 1 to 2 days) is the region's flagship activity. You pass under the Pont d'Arc right at the start, then traverse one of France's wildest canyons in a national nature reserve. The night must be spent on one of the two official bivouac areas at Gaud or Gournier — book in advance as places are limited.

Aiguèze awaits you just at the exit of the gorges, on the Gard side — its name comes from the Occitan 'aigo,' water in the rock, referring to the numerous wells in the limestone cliff. The first Gard village to enter the circle of Les Plus Beaux Villages de France in 2005, it was founded as a fortress by Charles Martel after the Battle of Poitiers in 732, to control the ford on the Ardèche. The 11th-century Saracen tower is one of the village's oldest constructions — it was intended to defend against attacks by Arab-Berber tribes whose name it bears. Connected to the 13th-century round tower and the 12th-century rectangular keep: together, these three towers perched on the Urgonian limestone blade form one of the most characteristic silhouettes of the Lower Ardèche.

The chemin de ronde (patrol path) is Aiguèze's unmissable experience: it runs along the old fortification along the cliff and passes the castle (private), the Saracen tower and the keep. The view from this path over the Ardèche gorges below and, on clear days, the distant silhouette of Mont Ventoux, is one of the finest on this circuit. Note a characteristic architectural detail: the ground floors of the houses are medieval, the first floors Renaissance — the Hundred Years' War had reduced the village to nine living inhabitants around 1382, and reconstruction happened gradually on the ruins.

Saint-Roch church, with its Renaissance portal and bell tower restored in Gothic style, deserves a stop for its neo-Gothic interior frescoes. The main street paved with Ardèche pebbles is the village's backbone — its blonde stone houses with Romanesque vaults and mullioned windows invite you to slow down and observe the sculpted details.

→

From Aiguèze, a hike towards the Castelviel rock leads to a remarkable panorama over the gorges from the heights: allow 2h round trip on a marked garrigue trail, moderate level. The view of the Ardèche meander beneath your feet is well worth the effort. Closed shoes essential.

→

Aiguèze is at the heart of the Côtes du Rhône vineyard. Operators offer tastings in village cellars or excursions to neighboring estates. In the evening, the Place du Jeu de Paume under the century-old plane trees, with its pétanque games and stone terraces, is the ideal spot to unwind.

Perched on its rocky peak above the Cèze, Cornillon is a medieval village listed in the inventory of protected sites, still little known. Its history is directly linked to the turbulence of the Avignon papacy: in the 14th century, Guillaume II Roger de Beaufort, brother of Pope Clement VI, purchased the lordship. At his death in 1380, the castle inventory revealed 40 rooms, two chapels and a 6-level keep tower. But in 1632, the Duke Henri II de Montmorency, owner of the castle, was beheaded in Toulouse for conspiring with Gaston d'Orléans against Richelieu. His property was destroyed: Cornillon castle was dismantled. The remaining walls, pierced with large windows, still dominate the valley from the village heights.

Walk along the ramparts to Place du Barry: an orientation table allows you to identify hamlets and reliefs in the verdant Cèze plain. The current church was built in one of the castle chapels whose external wall is formed by the old ramparts. Below the village, near the washhouse, you can see medieval sarcophagi found near the old church — destroyed by Protestant troops in the 16th century.

Since 2013, the festival 'Cornillon se la joue au château' takes over the courtyard each first week of August for open-air theater performances in a unique setting. Outside the season, the ruins are freely accessible from the outside — the view from the courtyard over the valley alone justifies the detour.

→

The trail that descends from the village towards the Cèze gorges (4 km loop, ~1h30, easy level) runs along abandoned agricultural terraces, dry stone walls and olive trees. The river below offers swimming spots in limestone pools — much less crowded than those at Aiguèze or La Roque-sur-Cèze.

→

A few kilometers from Cornillon, the Saint-Sauveur chapel in the hamlet of Saint-Gély (12th century) is accessible on foot via marked trails. It's characteristic of rural Romanesque art in Gard, simply built in light limestone.

Goudargues fully deserves its nickname of 'little Venice of Gard.' The La Fontaine canal that runs through the village is fed by a Vaucluse spring with constant flow (1,400 m³/h) and stable temperature at 14°C year-round. The Benedictine monks who developed the site in the Middle Ages chose their location well. Roman remains attest to human presence from the 3rd century — the village name itself comes from 'Gordanicus,' the name of a Roman villa that occupied the site. The plane trees lining the canal were planted in 1821. Sit on a terrace on the quay and watch the water flow between the plane trees: it's the most representative image of southern Gard.

The Notre-Dame-et-Saint-Michel abbey church is a surprise for those expecting a simple village church: it's a 12th-century abbey building, listed as a Historic Monument, which ranks among the most interesting Romanesque buildings in Languedoc. It developed around a Benedictine cloister — the refectory and chapter house have survived and now host cultural events. The choir and apse still date from the original Romanesque period.

Walk through Goudargues' narrow streets: the large washhouse with its emblematic bronze frog, the fountains, the bridges over the canal, the shaded squares. The village has local craft shops and tasting cellars for wines from the Gard Côtes du Rhône. In summer, the Wednesday evening market is one of the week's most pleasant moments.

→

The bike loop from Goudargues to the surrounding wine-growing hamlets (15 to 20 km, gently rolling terrain) is one of the best ways to explore the Gard countryside. The roads between vineyards and garrigue have virtually no traffic.

→

The Cèze is accessible on foot from Goudargues in 10 minutes via the path that starts from the washhouse. Swimming spots in limestone pools upstream and downstream from the village allow you to enjoy the clear water in summer, with fewer crowds than at La Roque-sur-Cèze.

Saint Paulet De Caisson

The Chartreuse de Valbonne is one of this circuit's most unusual curiosities — nestled at the bottom of a wooded valley, you reach it via a winding road through 1,500 hectares of state forest sheltering rare species for the Mediterranean region. Founded in 1204 when Guillem de Vénéjean, Bishop of Uzès, ceded to the Carthusian order a then-marshy domain, the monks cleared and drained the site, giving it the name 'vallis bona' — the good valley, origin of Valbonne. Pillaged in 1585 during the Wars of Religion, rebuilt from 1593, expropriated during the Revolution, bought back by the Carthusians in 1836, then transformed into a leprosarium from 1929 by Pastor Philadelphe Delord — who welcomed up to 400 patients and died on site in 1947 — its history spans the entire modern era.

The Chartreuse's architecture is immediately recognizable by its Burgundian-style glazed tiles covering the towers and bell tower of the conventual church — a surprising detail for a Provençal charterhouse. The great cloister measures 355 meters in perimeter; around it, the monastic cells bear letters from A to Y (Q is not used). The visit provides access to the conventual church with its vaults of complex stereotomy, work of the Franque brothers from Avignon, as well as the small cloister dating from the early 13th century. The Chartreuse has been classified as a Historic Monument since 1959. Check visiting hours before departure — the site is open from April to early October.

The Chartreuse vineyard produces organic Côtes du Rhône — a tasting is offered on site. Around the buildings, the Valbonne forest offers shaded trails with pubescent oaks, Scots pines and strawberry trees mixed with Mediterranean species.

→

The Chartreuse de Valbonne forest trail (5 to 7 km loop, moderate level, ~2h) crosses the state forest around the monastery and offers a striking contrast between the silence of the woods and monastic architecture. You encounter old dry stone walls, capitelles (shepherds' shelters) and more open garrigue areas with views over the reliefs.

A local saying sums up La Roque-sur-Cèze well: 'Who goes to La Roque gets hooked.' This Plus Beau Village de France since 2007 is perched on its rocky peak, crowned with an 11th-century castle and a 12th-century Romanesque chapel with apse adorned with cog-tooth decorations. To enter, you cross the Charles-Martel bridge, classified as a Historic Monument: built in the 13th century, it measures 155 meters long and has twelve stone arches. So narrow you must drive across alone. From the village center, climb up to the castle esplanade — an orientation table allows you to identify Mont Ventoux on clear days.

The Sautadet Cascades, below the village, are the most spectacular natural site in the Cèze valley. The river has been carving for 5 million years a series of progressive waterfalls with a total drop of 15 meters in the limestone rock, sculpting 'giant's kettles' — cylindrical cavities several meters in diameter swirling in the rocky riverbed. The nickname 'devil's leap' is well deserved: swimming is strictly forbidden due to currents and whirlpools, responsible for fatal accidents. Stay on marked trails and developed viewpoints. A little downstream, a beach allows safe swimming.

The cobblestone streets of La Roque-sur-Cèze, the steep calades, the blonde stone houses with genoises and old facades with Romanesque vaults deserve a walking exploration of at least 45 minutes. Thirteen heritage panels mark the route through the village — pick one up at the entrance to miss nothing.

→

The La Roque-sur-Cèze and Sautadet Cascades loop hike (8 km, ~2h30, easy-moderate level) links the village, the Charles-Martel bridge, both banks of the waterfalls and returns through vineyards and garrigue. It's the ideal way to combine heritage and nature in one outing. Respect the instructions: the waterfall area is forbidden for swimming.

Bagnols-sur-Cèze holds a surprise few people anticipate: one of the largest post-Impressionist art museums in provincial France, housed on the second floor of the town hall, on Place Mallet. The Albert-André Museum has a story worthy of a novel. Founded in 1859 by Léon Alègre, France's first cantonal museum, it completely changed character with the arrival in 1917 of painter Albert André as curator. A friend of Renoir, Monet and Matisse, he 'tapped his friends' — as he put it — to reconstitute the collection destroyed in a fire accidentally caused by the town's firefighters in 1924. Result: an exceptional ensemble of Renoir, Matisse, Bonnard, Signac, Marquet, Valadon, Van Dongen, Picasso, Camille Claudel, Rodin. Free admission, open almost year-round.

Place Mallet is the medieval heart of Bagnols — its arcades, old facades and private mansions tell a tale of ancient prosperity. Founded in 1223, the town has preserved some narrow streets and alleys from its old center. The Wednesday morning market is one of the area's largest.

→

The Cèze banks downstream from Bagnols offer swimming areas in limestone pools accessible on foot from town (20 minutes). Kayaking spots on the Cèze are available from Bagnols — operators offer equipment rental and return shuttles.

→

Bagnols-sur-Cèze's Wednesday market is one of the valley's culinary appointments. Wine producers from the Gard Côtes du Rhône, market gardeners and cheesemakers from the region hold permanent stalls there.

Laudun L Ardoise

Camp de César is one of the least-known and most impressive ancient towns in the Rhône corridor. Perched on the Lacau plateau at 264 meters altitude, this 18-hectare oppidum dominates the confluence of the Cèze and Tave, with views to the Alps on clear days. What we know: occupation lasted over 1,000 years. The Gauls founded a fortified oppidum in the 5th century BC, the Romans expanded it in the 1st century AD with a forum, civil basilica and tribunal — structures visible in the excavations — then the Benedictines occupied the site until the 13th century. Access is free year-round.

On the plateau, two orientation tables allow you to locate remarkable points: Rhône, Cèze, Tave, Cévennes and Alpilles in the distance. Visible remains include the Roman rampart with cart gate and vaulted pedestrian gate, the foundations of the 1st-century forum and basilica, and late-period sarcophagi. A permanent exhibition in the village presents the most significant objects from the excavations.

A few hundred meters from Camp de César, the Balouvière aqueduct is a little-known 19th-century curiosity: built in 1870 to supply Laudun with drinking water, it measures 62 meters long and 10 meters high, its limestone rubble architecture recalling its illustrious ancestor the Pont du Gard about thirty kilometers away. Both sites are freely accessible.

→

The Camp de César archaeological loop (3 to 5 km, easy level, ~1h30) starts from the marked parking on the Laudun heights and crosses the plateau following the route of the ancient ramparts. The garrigue vegetation (holm oaks, rosemary, thyme) contrasts with the cut stones of the Roman fortifications. On clear days, the viewpoint over the Rhône valley is one of the widest on the circuit.

Uzès is a revelation for those who've never been. France's first duchy since 1565 — and the oldest surviving French duchy — the town was entirely classified as a protected sector by André Malraux in 1965. Start at Place aux Herbes: lined with arcades, it hosts the Wednesday and especially Saturday market — one of the largest in Occitanie. Racine stayed here in 1661-1662. From the square, head up towards the Duchy — the castle complex of the ducal château, still inhabited by the Crussol d'Uzès family.

The Ducal Castle of Uzès is an 'architectural summary of France': its Renaissance facade, attributed to Philibert de l'Orme, superimposes the three classical orders — Doric, Ionic and Corinthian — in one of the first French applications of this architectural rule. Above stands the Bermonde tower, an 11th-century keep 42 meters high. The guided tour provides access to the flamboyant Gothic chapel, the furnished apartments and the medieval garden of 450 aromatic, medicinal and edible plants.

The Fenestrelle Tower is France's only example of a Romanesque cylindrical bell tower — built in the late 12th century, it's the sole remnant of the old Saint-Théodorit cathedral destroyed by Protestants in 1563. Standing 42 meters high with its superimposed twin bays, it irresistibly evokes Tuscan campaniles. From the nearby esplanade, the view over the Eure valley is magical.

Leaving town, look for the first vestiges of the Roman aqueduct that starts from the Eure spring at the foot of Uzès to reach Nîmes over 50 km — the same aqueduct of which the Pont du Gard is the most spectacular piece. The Eure spring is visible below the town, still active.

→

Uzès' Saturday market is the ideal time to assemble a quality picnic: black truffles from the Uzès region in season (November to March), AOP Duché d'Uzès wines, garrigue goat cheeses, olives and tapenades. Place aux Herbes is packed from 8am — plan to arrive early.

→

Uzès is 13 km from the Pont du Gard by bike via the greenway (32 km total route, Uzès–Beaucaire). It's the ideal approach to the Pont du Gard — arriving via the aqueduct through the garrigue, you better understand the ingenuity of this hydraulic work.

Vers Pont Du Gard

The Pont du Gard at 49 meters high is the world's tallest Roman aqueduct bridge. Built in the 1st century, it belongs to the 50 km aqueduct that carried by gravity 30,000 to 40,000 m³ of water per day from the Eure spring in Uzès to Nîmes — with an average slope of only 25 cm per kilometer over the entire route. The central arch of the first level measures 24.52 meters opening. Nearly 1,000 workers labored on this project completed in 5 years, for 21,000 m³ of limestone extracted from the nearby Estel quarry, without mortar. Classified as a Historic Monument since 1840 and UNESCO World Heritage since 1985, it's France's most visited ancient monument. Allow at least 1h for the self-guided visit.

The Pont du Gard Museum (left bank, in underground spaces invisible from the monument) traces over 2,500 m² the history of the aqueduct's construction and operation with multimedia technologies, virtual reconstructions and models. In summer, guided tours provide access to the upper level gallery — the conduit where water flowed.

The viewpoints on both banks of the Gardon offer very different perspectives on the three levels of arches. The right bank gives the most classic overall view. On clear days, contemplate the monument's reflection in the Gardon from the beach downstream — it's often the best photo of the day.

→

The 'Memories of garrigue' walking loop (1.4 km, ~40 min, easy level) starts from the site and crosses vineyards, olive trees and holm oaks discovering the Mediterranean landscape and buried aqueduct remains. More seasoned hikers will take the 'Roman aqueduct trail' in the Gardon gorges (8 to 10 km, ~3h, moderate level).

→

In summer, a supervised beach and swimming areas are set up 200 meters from the bridge on the Gardon. Swimming in the river at the foot of the aqueduct is the site's most memorable experience. Canoe rentals allow you to pass under the arches.

The Palais des Papes, built in less than 20 years between 1335 and 1352, is the world's largest Gothic palace: 15,000 m² of floor space, equivalent to four Gothic cathedrals. Seven French popes succeeded each other here until 1377. From the outside, the building is austere — almost menacing. Inside, the frescoes by Simone Martini and Matteo Giovanetti in the papal apartments and Saint-Martial chapel reveal the court's splendor. Book in advance — the site welcomed over 774,000 visitors in 2023.

The Pont Saint-Bénézet has a less romantic history than believed. Built in the 12th century over 900 meters and 22 arches, it was the only bridge over the Rhône for kilometers and a mandatory toll point between Spain and Italy. But the Rhône is capricious: in 1603 an arch collapsed, three more in 1605, two again in 1633, then several in 1669. Only four out of twenty-two remain. And no, people didn't dance on the bridge — they danced under the bridge, on the Île de la Barthelasse.

Avignon's ramparts form a 4.3 km belt built between 1355 and 1370 — 7 gates and 39 towers. Climb up to the Rocher des Doms garden for the view over the Rhône and the Comtat Venaissin plain. The Petit Palais Museum houses an extraordinary collection of medieval paintings and Italian primitives, including Botticelli and Carpaccio.

→

In July, the Avignon Festival is the world's largest theater festival with over 1,500 performances. The free river shuttle from Quai de la Ligne serves the Île de la Barthelasse — France's largest river island, by bike or on foot, for a different view of the Palace.

Nîmes is the logical terminus of this circuit — and the city that closes the Roman loop begun at Laudun and the Pont du Gard. The Arena, built around 90 AD, twenty years after Rome's Colosseum, measures 133 meters long, 101 meters wide and 21 meters high — one of the best-preserved ancient amphitheaters in the world. Its two levels of superimposed arcades and 60 bays are almost intact. The building could accommodate 25,000 spectators and evacuate them in 10 minutes. After the invasions, it transformed into a fortified village with streets and shops until the 19th century. It now hosts bullfights and concerts.

The Maison Carrée, classified as UNESCO World Heritage in 2023, is one of the best-preserved Roman temples in the world. Built under Augustus in the 1st century, dedicated to his grandsons Lucius and Caius Caesar, it measures 26 meters long, 13.5 meters wide and 17 meters high, surrounded by 30 Corinthian columns. The fact that it's so well preserved is due to its successive reuses: stable, church, archives, museum. Opposite, Norman Foster's Carré d'Art (1993) deliberately adopts the same proportions — a dialogue between Antiquity and modernity.

The Jardins de la Fontaine (15 hectares, created in 1745 around the sacred spring of Nemausus) are the ideal setting to end this circuit. The promenade climbs from the 18th-century basins and canals to the Temple of Diana (whose function remains debated), then up to the Tour Magne at the summit of Mont Cavalier: the last vestige of the 7 km Augustan enceinte, 32.5 meters high today, it offers a panoramic view over the entire Nîmes plain.

→

The Musée de la Romanité, opened in 2018 facing the Arena in a building by Elizabeth de Portzamparc clad in a perforated bronze veil, presents 5,000 archaeological pieces tracing 25 centuries of Nîmes history. Its rooftop offers a panoramic view over the Arena. Allow 1h30 to 2h.

→

Nîmes has a well-served TGV station (to Paris, Lyon, Marseille) and an airport, making it the ideal terminus for this circuit. The Ascension feria (May) and Harvest feria (September) transform the city into a bullfighting and music celebration for five days — if you coincide with one of these moments, book accommodation and Arena tickets well in advance.
