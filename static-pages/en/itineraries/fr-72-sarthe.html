Le Mans keeps its secrets well: behind the endurance race myth lies the Plantagenet City with its 4th-century Gallo-Roman ramparts, one of the best preserved in the entire former Roman Empire, and the cathedral where Henry II of England was baptized. From there, Sarthe opens onto ten days of authentic heritage: the Benedictine abbey of Solesmes and its Gregorian chants, Malicorne's faience workshops labeled since 1747, the russet sandstone villages of the Alpes Mancelles, and the 24 Hours of Le Mans circuit running since 1923.

  • Le Mans: 4th-century Gallo-Roman ramparts, Plantagenet cathedral, and 24 Hours circuit since 1923

  • Malicorne: artistic faience workshops since 1747, France's only capital of openwork faience

Day

Le Mans

The Gallo-Roman ramparts of Le Mans are one of the most astonishing things you'll see anywhere in France. Built in the early 4th century to encircle ancient Vindinum—the red city, as the Romans called it, referring to its blood-colored bricks—it originally ran 1,300 meters and today preserves about 500 meters, with 12 towers and 3 posterns still standing. On certain sections, polychrome geometric friezes run more than 8 meters high—a decorative richness unique in the entire former Roman Empire. Le Mans is, in fact, a candidate for UNESCO listing for these ramparts. For the best view, descend the Pans-de-Gorron staircase to the left of the bishop's palace and look up.

The Cité Plantagenêt—old Le Mans, renamed in 2003—spreads across 20 hectares atop the promontory. This is where the Plantagenet dynasty shaped medieval England's history: in 1128, Geoffrey Plantagenet married Matilda, heiress to England's throne, in the cathedral here, and their son Henry II, future King of England, was baptized in the same place. Since then, little has really changed—15th-century half-timbered houses, Renaissance mansions, and cobbled lanes so picturesque that scenes from the film Cyrano de Bergerac were shot here. Walk along rue de Vaux with its climbing roses and look for the houses called des Deux-Amis and du Drapier: their carved signs alone tell the quarter's story.

The Cathedral of Saint-Julien has dominated the city since the 11th century—in reality far longer, its foundation dating according to archives to the 4th or 5th century. Its Romanesque nave and Gothic choir completed in 1254, signed notably by Jehan de Chelles who also worked on Notre-Dame de Paris, make it one of France's most complex buildings, at 134 meters long and 5,000 m² in area. Don't miss two curiosities: the Ascension window in a side chapel, dated late 11th century and considered the oldest stained glass still in place in any cathedral in the world; and the pink sandstone menhir attached to the west facade, nicknamed Saint Julien's Stone, which legend says touching its navel promotes fertility.

The Queen Bérengère Museum, housed in three late-15th-century half-timbered houses, owes its name to Richard the Lionheart's widow, who supposedly lived in one of them until her death. Labeled Musée de France, it houses on the ground floor Sarthe bonnets and 19th-century furniture, upstairs a fine collection of medical and civil pewter, and higher up paintings depicting Le Mans life—including a large canvas by Jean Sorieul of the 1793 Battle of Le Mans.

Three kilometers from the center, the Royal Abbey of Épau is one of the finest Cistercian foundations in northwestern France. Founded in 1229 by Queen Bérengère of Navarre—who died a year after construction began and never saw it completed—the abbey houses her white marble effigy in the abbatial church. Sculpted in the style of Eleanor of Aquitaine at Fontevraud, she is depicted crowned, with a purse at her belt and a lion at her feet. The effigy had been discovered under bales of straw in 1816 then transferred to Le Mans cathedral, before returning here in 1970. The site extends through a 13-hectare park.

→

In summer, don't miss the Nuit des Chimères: every evening from July to early September, the Gallo-Roman ramparts and cathedral become giant projection screens with sound and light. Wander freely through the Cité's lanes to discover the eight visual spectacles animating the medieval facades—Le Mans heritage seen as you never imagined it.

💡 Free entry, bring warm clothes for evening
→

Stroll the Cité's lanes searching for underground passages that once connected the cathedral to other strategic points in the city. Gallo-Roman baths from the 1st century are partially preserved beneath the Beaux-Arts school—fragments of mosaics following Italian rules of the period—and the tourist office offers audioguides to explore these superimposed layers of history.

2
Day

Le Mans

The 24 Hours of Le Mans Museum traces the world's greatest endurance race, run without interruption since 1923 on the same 13.6 km circuit using departmental roads south of Le Mans. The permanent collection brings together more than 120 authentic vehicles: the 1924 Bentley 3-litre Sport that inaugurated the podium of the second edition, the Bugatti Type 57G that won in 1937, the Jaguar D-Type, the Ford GT40 victorious in 1969, the Porsche 917, the Audi R18... A showcase of 4,236 1:43 scale models represents every car entered since 1923. Note: the museum is currently closed for expansion work (reopening planned for 2026)—a temporary exhibition of 18 emblematic cars is accessible at the circuit's South Gate.

The guided tour of the 24 Hours of Le Mans circuit lets you access behind the scenes: the paddocks, starting line, the Hunaudières straight where cars reach over 350 km/h, the legendary corners of Tertre Rouge, Mulsanne, and Arnage. The permanent Bugatti circuit—4.2 km—is an integral part of the 24 Hours layout. Local operators also offer sports car tours or karting sessions within the complex.

The Tessé Museum, in Le Mans city center, holds a fine collection of paintings from the 13th to 20th centuries. Don't miss the enameled copper copy of Geoffrey Plantagenet—one of the oldest figurative representations in champlevé enamel in France—and the Egyptian archaeology collection that stands out in this context.

→

Operators offer karting or track driving sessions within the 24 Hours complex—karting on the Maison Blanche circuit, driving simulators, or sports car baptisms on the Bugatti Circuit. For serious enthusiasts, it's even possible to drive a homologated sports car on the legendary track. Book online before arrival to guarantee your slot.

💡 Closed shoes mandatory
→

The official 24 Hours of Le Mans shop, at the circuit entrance, is one of France's largest automotive merchandising stores: jerseys, caps, scale models, posters, car accessories. Even if you don't drive, it's a temple of motorsport that no enthusiast can miss.

The Prytanée National Militaire is an institution of uncommon historical density. In 1603, Henry IV donated his château of La Flèche to the Jesuits to found a college there—his declared objective: "to instruct youth and make them lovers of learning, honor, and virtue." René Descartes was one of its first students, from 1607 to 1615, and traced in the Discourse on Method the intellectual formation he received there. In 1808, Napoleon transferred the military Prytanée from Saint-Cyr here. The Henri IV quarter, 16 hectares classified as historic monuments in the heart of the city, can be visited in summer: three courtyards in succession, the Jesuit library, the halls of honor. Above all, don't miss the Church of Saint-Louis.

The Church of Saint-Louis, built between 1607 and 1621, is La Flèche's Baroque jewel. Its interior decoration was completed in 1693 and revolves around three major pieces: Pierre Corbineau's great altarpiece (1633), the organ and its gallery (1640), and the royal cenotaphs. Henry IV had promised, in his lifetime, to bequeath his heart to this church. His promise was kept—but during the Revolution, in 1793, representative Thirion had the two royal hearts thrown onto the pyre in the public square. A local surgeon collected some ashes in a glass vial, returned to the Prytanée in 1814. Today, a small gilded heart-shaped urn in the north transept arm preserves these mingled ashes of Henry IV and Marie de Medici.

The Zoo de La Flèche truly deserves a visit, and not just with children. Founded in 1946 by naturalist Jacques Bouillault—France's first private zoo—it extends over 18 hectares and gathers 1,500 animals representing 160 species. It regularly features in the top 5 most visited zoos in France and was the subject of the documentary television series broadcast on France 4. The gibbons, lemurs, and okapis alone are worth the trip. Allow at least two hours.

→

Walk along the Loir riverbanks from the Parc des Carmes, where picturesque ruins of the old château remain. The landscaped banks follow the river for several kilometers—a flat, shaded walk, perfect midday. In season, you can continue to the Monnerie lakes which offer a beach and cycling path along the water.

→

The Cré-sur-Loir marsh, a regional nature reserve about ten kilometers from La Flèche, offers several marked trails to discover the Loir valley's wetland fauna. Herons, spoonbills, and sometimes otters are regularly observed here—bring binoculars and boots if you go after rain.

💡 Binoculars recommended

Malicorne-sur-Sarthe became France's capital of artistic faience thanks to a fortunate combination: quality clay deposits at Ligron, abundant wood for kilns, and a port on the Sarthe to distribute production. In 1747, Jean Loiseau, a faience maker from Touraine, opened the first manufactory here in a former inn called the Plat d'Étain. Push open the door of Faïencerie Bourg-Joly Malicorne—the direct heir of that foundation, still in operation—and watch the artisans work. Their openwork baskets are Malicorne's signature: the technique involves cutting the raw clay piece with a pointed blade to create fine openings reminiscent of lace. In 2011, during Monaco's princely wedding, Bourg-Joly delivered 342 of these baskets to decorate the Opera reception tables.

The Museum of Faience and Ceramics, labeled Musée de France, is housed in a former 19th-century stoneware manufactory with its kilns and chimney. The tour traces the history of Malicorne faience from the 18th century to contemporary creations—decorations inspired by Rouen, Quimper, or Moustiers, and especially the famous openwork invented in 1924 by Émile Tessier. He had the idea of piercing the still-raw piece with a pointed blade before firing. Slip-casting workshops are offered for young and old—a good way to leave with a piece made by your own hands.

The Malicorne color mill houses the Arts and Crafts shop on the first floor. This is where faience makers once ground their stones under millstones to extract colored pigments. The building is worth seeing even if you don't buy anything.

→

Embark on an electric boat ride on the Sarthe. Operators offer these outings from Malicorne port—an original perspective on the village from the water, with its tuffeau facades and poplar-lined banks. Allow an hour for the short version, two hours to reach Sablé-sur-Sarthe downstream.

→

Operators offer Sarthe descents by canoe-kayak from Malicorne. Successive locks rhythm the navigation and allow stops on wild banks. Bring spare clothes—even in calm weather, lock crossings can hold surprises.

💡 Bring spare clothes

The Château de Sablé-sur-Sarthe, rebuilt in the 18th century, dominates the confluence of the Sarthe and Vègre from its promontory. It replaced the old medieval fortress of the lords of Sablé—including Geoffroy the Old, who founded in 1010 the priory that would become Solesmes Abbey. From the terrace, the view over the two rivers and city rooftops is particularly beautiful in late afternoon. Black marble extracted from Sablé quarries was used for certain exterior facings of the Palace of Versailles.

The Marble Ecomuseum traces the marble industry that made Sablé and its region wealthy from the 17th to 20th centuries, employing at its peak over 2,000 workers. Discover quarrymen's tools, extraction and polishing techniques, and some remarkable pieces from royal commissions. It's a fascinating and little-known local economic history.

The Saint-Nicolas suburb preserves some half-timbered medieval houses adorned with ironwork and ridge finials. Their winding lanes open onto the Sarthe pleasure port—the busiest in the West—where boaters travel up and down the river between May and October.

→

Operators offer river cruises from Sablé: the navigable Sarthe reveals its wooded meanders and tuffeau villages from the water. Allow two hours for a round trip to Solesmes—the view of the abbey from the river is one of the circuit's great moments.

→

The weekly market of Sablé-sur-Sarthe is one of the department's liveliest. Taste Sarthe rillettes—slowly cooked for hours in their own fat, they have a melting texture very different from industrial rillettes—accompanied by goat cheeses from local producers and shortbread cookies made according to the original recipe of the eponymous village.

💡 Check market days at tourist office

The Abbey of Saint-Pierre de Solesmes has dominated the Sarthe for over a thousand years. Founded in 1010 by Geoffroy the Old, lord of Sablé, who donated a monastery to the monks of Marmoutier, it remained a simple priory until the Revolution. It was Dom Prosper Guéranger who in 1833 made it the pinnacle of Gregorian chant that it is today. Seen from the river, the abbey's silhouette is stunning—austere and massive like a sacred fortress, in the words of a 19th-century traveler. From the bridge spanning the Sarthe, take time to contemplate this emblematic panorama before entering.

Only the abbatial church is freely visited. Once through the medieval door, the Saints of Solesmes await you in the transept: two sculptural ensembles from the 15th and 16th centuries considered masterpieces of French sculpture. The north group represents the tomb of Our Lord in flamboyant Gothic style, the south group illustrates the Assumption of the Virgin. The abbey organ, 39 stops of North European inspiration, resonates under the vaults during services. The crypt, accessible at indicated hours, houses Dom Guéranger's tomb.

Attending a service sung by the community of Benedictine monks is an experience difficult to describe. The sung conventual mass takes place at 10 a.m. every day (about 1h15, 1h45 on Sundays and feast days). Gregorian chant resonates in the abbey's particular acoustics with a depth and purity unlike anything else. Arrive early—seats in the nave fill quickly on Sundays.

→

The abbey shop, open 9 a.m. to 7 p.m. outside services, offers theology books, Gregorian chant recordings by the monks themselves, and monastic products. Solesmes recordings are an international reference in their field—buy one to extend the experience at home.

→

Continue on the pedestrian path that runs along the Sarthe beneath the abbey to Cri Cri beach. The walk is short and peaceful, perfect after the spiritual immersion of the service. You'll also pass nearby the building of Sainte-Cécile Abbey, founded in 1866, where 45 Benedictine nuns now reside.

Fresnay-sur-Sarthe has perched on a rocky spur above the Sarthe since the 9th century—William the Conqueror besieged it several times in the 11th century. Labeled Petite Cité de Caractère since 1995, it represented the Pays de la Loire region on the television show France's Favorite Village in 2021. Its absolute jewel: the carved oak door of Notre-Dame church, created in 1528 by the Dienis brothers, two local artists. The left panel illustrates the Tree of Jesse (Old Testament), the right the Twelve Apostles (New Testament). The church itself, built in warm-toned russet stone, was constructed in the second half of the 12th century by the counts of Maine.

Climb to the château park for two discoveries: the ruins of the old medieval keep and the plunging view over the Sarthe meanders and the Creusot quarter where weavers' houses still line up. These houses have very particular architecture—the weaving workshop was installed slightly underground to maintain the humidity necessary for hemp thread solidity, the living room upstairs, accessible by an external staircase. The château's postern, two 14th-century circular towers, marks the entrance to the public garden.

The Bonnet Museum exhibits 180 19th-century Sarthe bonnets—from the simple bonnette to the Bise-moi vite—restored and presented on hat stands by the local association's seamstresses. Each bonnet tells an era and social status: the most elaborate, entirely handmade in lace, could represent weeks of work. A precious ethnographic document on Sarthe women's lives.

→

Descend rue du Bourgneuf, partly paved, which runs along the Sarthe at the foot of the promontory. Typical houses open directly onto the river and benches allow you to sit and watch the water pass. It's one of the most peaceful and authentic streets in Sarthe—take your time.

→

The Coteau des Vignes, accessible from a marked path from the bottom of the village, offers a fantastic view over the Sarthe valley and its meanders. This sensitive natural site hosts rare plant species specific to limestone grasslands—its almost Mediterranean airs surprise in Maine.

Saint-Céneri-le-Gérei has been classified as one of France's Most Beautiful Villages since 1983—and seeing the ensemble from the stone bridge spanning the Sarthe, you immediately understand why. The 11th-century Romanesque church perched on its granite spur reflects in the river's peaceful water. Arrive via the RD56 to enjoy this emblematic viewpoint before entering the village. Inside the church, frescoes from the 12th and 14th centuries were covered with whitewash in 1650 then rediscovered in the late 19th century: a Virgin extending her blue mantle above a crowd, a Christ in Majesty, and the hermit Saint Céneri himself. Also look for the bee's nest carved in stone—present according to tradition since the 9th century, they would be guardians of the holy place since they attacked soldiers who threw themselves into the Sarthe to escape them.

The Chapel of Petit Saint-Céneri, dating from the 15th century, stands in a vast meadow on the other bank of the Sarthe—facing the village. Its chestnut framework forms an inverted boat hull. This is where, according to legend, founder Céneri's hermitage was located, an Italian monk who came to evangelize the Alpes Mancelles in the 7th century. Tradition holds that young women wishing to marry within the year come to plant a needle in the saint's statue's feet.

The Auberge des Sœurs Moisy, transformed into a tourist information office from April to September, was until 1908 the haunt of Impressionist and Naturalist painters who came for inspiration in the Alpes Mancelles. Corot, Courbet, Harpignies, Boudin, Mary Renard all stayed here. Go up to the first floor to see the Decapitated Room: by candlelight, visiting painters amused themselves by tracing on the whitewashed walls, in charcoal, the shadow puppet profile of their guests—sixty decapitated silhouettes still watching you today.

→

Walk the Saint-Céneri on the motif pedestrian trail, set up by the Normandie-Maine Regional Natural Park. This route leads to places appreciated by 19th-century painters for the particular quality of light on the valley—panels indicate the exact position from which such and such a painting was made. A playful booklet is available at the tourist office.

💡 Booklet available at tourist office
→

Hikes in the Alpes Mancelles from Saint-Céneri offer landscapes of cliffs, rocks, and wild river that surprise in this corner of France. The Haut-Fourché and Toyères cliff sectors offer marked circuits of 2 to 4 hours in the Regional Natural Park. Local operators also offer Sarthe descents by canoe from the village.

💡 Marked circuits available at Alpes Mancelles tourist office

The Halles aux Grains of Mamers, built in 1818 replacing an older building, still host Monday, Friday, and Saturday markets today. Countess de Ségur mentioned them in Memoirs of a Donkey, published in 1860. These halls fit into a monumental triptych around Place Carnot: facing them, the Italian-style theater—one of the region's rare examples—occupies the ground floor of the festival hall building; behind, Saint-Nicolas church built between the 13th and 14th centuries raises its remarkable tower-belfry and Renaissance portal classified as a historic monument.

On Place de la République, look up to spot Mamers' Eiffel Tower: a 1:100 replica, built in 1889—the same year as the Paris Tower—by a local tinsmith who had worked on the Parisian construction site. Restored in 1997, it sits on a roof and remains probably the oldest known copy of the Eiffel Tower in the world. The story says it was commissioned to attract officers from the local garrison.

The Convent of the Visitation, founded in 1630 by Marie-Augustine Davoust, was confiscated at the Revolution to install the town hall and state prisons. Graffiti from royalist prisoners are still visible on the walls of the former cells. Also stroll through the coulées mamertines—these paved passages so narrow they can't be distinguished from the square—and along rue de Cinq-Ans with its hemp weavers' houses.

→

Tasting Sarthe rillettes is an obligation in Mamers. The Brotherhood of Knights of Sarthe Rillettes, founded here in 1968, organizes the annual National Contest of Best Rillettes each year. Maine rillettes are slowly cooked in their own fat—melting texture, concentrated and slightly caramelized pork flavor. Market butchers will offer you several producers to compare.

→

The Maine Saosnois Greenway, a former railway line converted to a gentle path, extends 12 kilometers from Mamers through the Sarthe Perche countryside. Accessible on foot, by bike, or stroller—flat, natural, and with views over bocage. Operators rent bikes at the start.

The kitchen garden of Bonnétable is one of Sarthe's most pleasant surprises. Laid out in 1885 over 13,000 m² by brothers Denis and Eugène Bühler—the landscape architects who also designed Thabor Park in Rennes—it was commissioned by the Duke of La Rochefoucauld-Doudeauville for his Château de Bonnétable. Surrounded by walls covered with old roses and clematis, it houses a heritage orchard, heirloom vegetables, a Mediterranean garden, a medicinal herb garden, and magnificent greenhouses. At the back of the park, a Bollée windmill from the late 19th century—a wind turbine invented in 1868 by Ernest Sylvain Bollée to pump water—is one of the finest examples still standing in France. Free access during tourist office hours.

The Château de Bonnétable, built in the 15th century then remodeled in the 19th for the Dukes of Doudeauville, dominates the village with its re-dug moats and park. The old stables, with their preserved stalls and saddlery, now house the tourist office and a temporary exhibition hall.

The Church of Saint-Sulpice, whose origins date back to the 11th century, preserves a wooden organ from 1850 and beautiful 19th-century stained glass windows illustrating the life of Saint Sulpice. The 1856 covered market, on Place du Vieux Château, deserves a look for its campanile with two clocks.

→

The Transvap is a tourist railway operated by passionate volunteers who run steam locomotives and vintage railcars on the 17 km of the former Mamers-Saint-Calais line—nicknamed the Dukes' line because it was originally financed by the region's château owners. The journey between Bonnétable and Beillé crosses the Jousse valley through the Sarthe bocage. Trains run in summer on Sundays and holidays. Check the calendar before coming—the Transvap sometimes has technical closures.

→

The Forest of Bonnétable extends over several hundred hectares north of the village. Marked walking and cycling circuits allow you to stroll beneath centuries-old oaks and beeches. It's an ideal outing to finish this circuit gently, away from the bustle, before rejoining Le Mans 30 km away.
