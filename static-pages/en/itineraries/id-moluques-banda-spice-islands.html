Moluccas: Spices, Forts and Diving in the Banda Islands

Follow the trail of the most valuable commodity of the medieval world: nutmeg, produced exclusively in the Banda Islands until the 19th century. Fort Belgica (1611) on Banda Neira and the Parigi Rante well bear witness to the 1621 genocide that wiped out 14,000 Bandanese to preserve the Dutch monopoly. Neighboring Run Island was traded for Manhattan in 1667. Beneath these same waters, among Indonesia's richest reefs, 871 fish species patrol the lava flows of Gunung Api, which last erupted in 1988.

  • Banda Neira: Fort Belgica (1611), Parigi Rante well (44 chiefs executed in 1621), nutmeg plantations still in production

Your day-by-day itinerary

1
Day

Ambon

Ambon, capital of Maluku Province, was the nerve center of the Dutch spice empire for two centuries. The Portuguese were the first Europeans to set foot here in 1513, drawn by cloves; the Dutch wrested Fort Victoria from them in 1605 and made it the VOC headquarters in the eastern Indies. This fort at the mouth of Ambon Bay has been partially restored and now houses a military museum — look for the plaque commemorating the Amboyna Massacre of 1623, when the Dutch executed ten English merchants accused of conspiracy, an incident that poisoned Anglo-Dutch relations for decades.

The Siwa Lima Museum, housed in a building designed in the rumah adat (traditional house) style of Maluku, holds the most comprehensive ethnographic collection in the Moluccas: gold jewelry from indigenous aristocracies, tifa musical instruments, ceremonial textiles and 17th-century Dutch maritime charts. This is the place to understand the cultures you're about to encounter — the Alfurs of Seram, the Bandanese exterminated by Coen, the Christian Kei. Allow 90 minutes.

The old town of Ambon around the Pelabuhan (harbor) preserves a handful of 18th-century Dutch colonial facades between the modern buildings — spot the old Protestant church dating from 1817, one of the oldest in eastern Indonesia. The church was built at the request of the Ambonese Protestants whom the Dutch had converted from the 16th century onward, creating a Christian community that played a distinctive role in the region's colonial history.

→

Dine at the warungs around Pasar Mardika, Ambon's main market, for a first taste of Moluccan cuisine: ikan kuah kuning (fish in turmeric broth), sagu lempeng (sago flatbreads), rica-rica (the fiery spice paste that's a Maluku signature). The city is divided between Christian and Muslim neighborhoods — since the intercommunal violence of 1999–2002, a tense but real coexistence has been re-established.

2
Day

Ambon

Fort Duurstede, on the island of Saparua — a 2-hour ferry ride from Ambon — is the site of a founding episode of Indonesian history. On May 16, 1817, Thomas Matulessy — known as Kapitan Pattimura — led hundreds of Moluccan fighters in an assault on the fort, killing the Dutch resident Van den Berg and the entire garrison (only Van den Berg's 5-year-old son was spared). Pattimura had served in the British army during their brief occupation of the Moluccas; when the Dutch returned in 1816 with their clove monopoly and taxes, he took up arms. The Dutch retook the fort in August 1817, captured Pattimura through betrayal in November and hanged him on December 16 in Ambon. His face now appears on the 1,000-rupiah banknote. The fort's ramparts still stand — take in the panorama over Saparua Bay from the machicolations.

Liang Beach (Pantai Hunimua) on the north coast of Ambon Island: 3 km of white sand in a sheltered bay, 40 minutes from Ambon. The water is remarkably clear and coral reefs start just meters from shore. If you return from Saparua in the mid-afternoon, a stop here at the end of the day is a must.

The village of Haria, a few kilometers from Fort Duurstede on Saparua, is the birthplace of Thomas Matulessy (Pattimura) in 1783. A monument depicts him in British military uniform — he had served as sergeant-major under the English during their brief occupation of the Moluccas (1810–1816). A smaller statue represents Martha Christina Tiahahu, the 17-year-old woman who fought at his side and died in deportation a few years later. She too appears on an Indonesian banknote.

→

Diving or snorkeling in Ambon Bay — the bay is famous in the diving world for its gently sloping seabeds populated by nudibranchs, pygmy seahorses and mimic octopuses. Local operators run 2-dive outings on the bay's wrecks and reefs. Visibility is typically 15 to 25 meters.

3
Day

Banda Neira

The fast ferry from Ambon to Banda Neira takes 6 to 7 hours — early morning departure, arrival in the early afternoon. During the crossing, Gunung Api (640 m) takes shape on the horizon well before you arrive: this perfect smoking cone rising from a flat sea sets the tone. Banda Neira is a village of a few thousand people on a 3 km² island, encircled by a handful of pastel-green Dutch colonial houses and nutmeg palms. Drop your bags and head immediately to the Rumah Budaya, the historical museum founded by Des Alwi — the painting at the entrance depicts the execution of the 44 orang kaya (Bandanese chiefs) by the VOC's Japanese mercenaries on May 8, 1621. It's the only known image of the massacre.

The colonial houses in central Banda Neira deserve an hour of wandering: these 17th- and 18th-century residences belong to descendants of the perkeniers (Dutch nutmeg planters) mixed with populations imported as slave labor after the 1621 genocide. Nutmeg still grows in the gardens — take a ripe fruit in your hands and peel it open: the reddish-brown seed (the nutmeg), wrapped in scarlet mace, carries the same scent that got thousands of people killed four centuries ago.

→

First reconnaissance dive in the waters around Banda Neira after arrival — the reefs are accessible directly from the village jetty. Banda's waters hold 310 reef-building coral species and 871 recorded fish species. Four seamounts around the islands shelter yellowfin tuna, hammerhead sharks and manta rays in the deeper zones.

4
Day

Banda Neira

Fort Belgica, on the hill overlooking Banda Neira, is the largest VOC fort still standing in Indonesia. Built from 1611 on orders from the future Governor-General Jan Pieterszoon Coen — the man who would order the Bandanese genocide ten years later — this black-basalt pentagon commands the entire bay and the neighboring islands. Its five star-shaped bastions allowed 360° fire coverage. In 1621, Coen landed here with 1,655 European soldiers, 100 Japanese rōnin mercenaries and 300 Javanese prisoners. Of a Bandanese population of roughly 15,000, barely 1,000 survived. From the ramparts on a clear day, you can see Run to the west — the island that was traded for Manhattan.

Fort Nassau, older and less well preserved than Belgica, dates from 1609 — it's the first Dutch fort on the Banda Islands. Beside the fort stands the Parigi Rante well, surrounded by a monument inaugurated in 2003: a bronze plaque bears the names of the 40 orang kaya executed here on May 8, 1621. According to tradition, they descended from their cells to purify themselves before being beheaded by the rōnin in front of Coen. A second monument recalls that Mohammed Hatta and Sutan Syahrir, future founding fathers of Indonesian independence, were exiled here by the Dutch from 1935 to 1942 — the house of their forced residence can be visited.

A nutmeg plantation on Banda Neira — several estates offer guided tours. Nutmeg (Myristica fragrans) is the tree whose seed quite literally changed the course of world history. In medieval Europe, a pound of nutmeg was worth more than a pound of gold; it was believed to protect against the Black Death. It was to maintain the absolute monopoly on this spice that the VOC committed the 1621 genocide. Today the same trees still produce, in the same gardens, and the scent of fresh nutmeg is everywhere on the island.

→

Diving on the "Lava Flow" around Gunung Api — when the volcano erupted on May 9, 1988, the lava destroyed all corals in its path and killed 3 people. But the cooling lava created a mineral substrate of exceptional fertility: within 5 years of the eruption, 120 coral species had already colonized the flows. Today the reefs around Gunung Api are among the most diverse in the world, with growth in 30 years that normally takes 70 years elsewhere. It's one of the rare opportunities to dive on volcanic lava covered in corals.

5
Day

Banda Neira

Ascent of Gunung Api (640 m) — departure at 4 AM from Banda Neira, a 5-minute boat ride to the foot of the volcano, then a 3- to 4-hour climb over volcanic scoria slopes with no mature vegetation (the trees haven't had time to regrow since 1988). At the summit, fumaroles hiss and sulfur crystallizes on the rocks. On a clear day, the panorama reveals the entire Banda archipelago and, on the horizon, the outline of Seram. Gunung Api is one of the best-documented volcanoes in history (first recorded eruption in 1586) thanks to the logbooks of VOC ships that passed it on their trade routes.

Boat excursion to Pulau Run, 30 km west of Banda Neira — count on 45 minutes each way. This island, 3 km long and 1 km wide, is the single most philosophically dizzying spot in the Moluccas: in 1667, under the Treaty of Breda that ended the Second Anglo-Dutch War, the English ceded Run to the Dutch in exchange for Manhattan — which the Duke of York had renamed New York three years earlier. The Dutch reckoned that Run, the only island in the nutmeg-producing archipelago they didn't yet control, was worth New Amsterdam. Nutmeg trees still grow on Run. The village has about 2,000 inhabitants. No roads, no cars.

→

Snorkeling around Pulau Ai, Run's neighbor: the waters between Ai and Run are among the richest in marine life in the Banda Sea. Whitetip reef sharks and Napoleon wrasse (Cheilinus undulatus) patrol the reefs. Ai was the scene of a savage battle in 1615: the English retook the island by night after a Dutch defeat, killing 200 Dutchmen — then the Dutch besieged it for a month until the defenders, out of ammunition, surrendered. The Dutch renamed the fort "Fort Revenge."

6
Day

Banda Neira

Banda Besar (Lontar), the largest island in the group — 10 minutes by boat — is where Jan Pieterszoon Coen launched his main attack on March 11, 1621. Nutmeg groves still cover much of the island — some trees are over 200 years old. Fort Hollandia, built in 1624 on Coen's orders to oversee the Lontar plantations, is reachable on foot from the landing. From its ramparts you can see the "Mini Palace" of Banda Neira across the water, once the Dutch administrator's residence. Some of the old perkens (plantation concessions) are still inhabited by descendants of the perkeniers, mixed with the populations of imported slaves.

The Welvaren Gateway, a 17th-century stone arch at the entrance to a former plantation on Banda Neira, is one of the most photographed architectural remnants on the islands. It marks the entrance to a perken's gardens — each 1.2-hectare nutmeg plantation was allotted after 1621 to a former VOC soldier or employee. With the monopoly, nutmeg fetched 122 times on the European market what the VOC paid the perkeniers. The planters nonetheless built villas furnished with imports from Europe.

→

Full day of diving on the Banda Sea seamounts — four underwater mountains around the islands harbor concentrations of pelagic life that few places in the world can match: schools of yellowfin tuna, banded sea snakes spiraling through the water, mandarin fish (Synchiropus splendidus), reef sharks. Visibility rarely drops below 25 m and often reaches 40 m. Surveys in 2001–2002 recorded 871 fish species in these waters. Reserve 2–3 dives for this penultimate day on the Banda Islands.

→

Tasting nutmeg and mace in every form on Banda Neira before departure: nutmeg jam, nutmeg tea, nutmeg coffee, mace syrup. The Dutch perkeniers grew rich reselling nutmeg at a 6,000% markup in Europe. Today the small-scale growers sell directly from their gardens.

7
Day

Ambon

Ferry back to Ambon — 6–7 hours of sailing across the Banda Sea. The crossing is a chance to watch Gunung Api disappear over the horizon and reflect on what you've just seen: for two centuries, these few islands produced in nutmeg what some historians estimate at a third of the global GDP of the era. On arrival in Ambon in the mid-afternoon, the city feels almost modern after the isolation of Banda.

Namalatu Beach, 12 km south of Ambon toward Latuhalat, is the best snorkeling spot accessible from the capital. Fringing reefs start 50 m from the beach with healthy hard corals and parrotfish, surgeonfish and Napoleon wrasse. It's also a good sandy beach to rest on before tomorrow's flight.

The ANZAC War Cemetery at Tantui, 5 km from Ambon, holds the graves of 1,931 Australian, British and Dutch soldiers who died in Japanese camps between 1942 and 1945. Almost all were prisoners of war captured when Ambon fell in February 1942. The cemetery, maintained by the Commonwealth War Graves Commission, is one of the most significant in the Pacific — and one of the least visited. The presence of these graves is a reminder that Ambon was a major theater of the Second World War, nearly erased from European memory.

→

Free evening in Ambon — enjoy local cuisine at the waterfront restaurants. Moluccan food is little-known but excellent: kohu-kohu (smoked tuna salad with grated coconut), ikan asar (coconut-wood-smoked fish), rujak colo (spicy fruit salad). The Passo night market, 20 minutes from the center, is lively on weekday evenings.

8
Day

Tual

Flight Ambon–Tual (Kei Kecil) — about 1.5 hours. The Kei Islands form an archipelago of 150 islands south of the Banda group, on the edge of the Arafura Sea. Tual and Langgur are the two main towns, linked by a bridge. On arrival, take an angkot (shared minibus) to Ngurbloat: 3 km of white sand of flour-like fineness, which National Geographic named the softest sand in Asia. The ultra-fine quartz that makes up these beaches emits a faint matte sound when you walk on it, like talcum powder. Arrive in the afternoon to see the evening light on the coconut palms and the turquoise hues of the water.

Ohoidertawun, the fishing village north of Pasir Panjang, maintains a nearly intact Ohoi (traditional Kei community) culture. The Kei are 90% Catholic — a rare distinction in predominantly Muslim eastern Indonesia — and apply the Larvul Ngabal customary law, an unwritten legal code governing social relations, property and fishing. In the evening, watch the fishermen return with their catch.

→

Snorkeling from Pasir Panjang beach — the coral reefs begin about 600 m from shore toward the drop-off zone. Visibility ranges from 15 to 40 m depending on the season. There are 200 hard coral species in these waters, and green and hawksbill turtles are sighted on 80% of snorkeling outings. Don't miss the local phenomenon called meti kei (Oct.–Nov.): the sea retreats by dozens of meters, revealing habitable sandbars.

9
Day

Tual

Pulau Bair, reachable in 30 minutes by boat from Tual, is a small cluster of limestone rocks enclosing a turquoise lagoon of staggering clarity. The water is so clear from the surface that you can see corals at 15 m depth with the naked eye. The Bair underwater cliff drops to over 40 m — one of the best dive sites in the Kei for experienced divers, with currents, gorgonians and giant groupers. For non-divers, swimming in the lagoon itself is exceptional.

Hawang Cave shelters an underground lake with turquoise, cold waters lit by light piercing through the stalactites. This site is sacred to the Kei villagers and hosts rites of passage and ceremonies — ask permission before entering and follow the guardians' instructions.

→

Diving or snorkeling on the Ngur Sarnadan reef — a site renowned for the sea turtles (green and hawksbill) that come to feed here. Sea turtles have been protected in the Kei under Ohoi customary law long before the Indonesian state intervened: each clan manages its fishing zones and sets no-catch periods. The result: turtle populations in far better health than elsewhere in Indonesia.

10
Day

Tual

Final morning at Pasir Panjang — walk toward the quieter, cleaner northern section at Ohoililir. At low tide, the white sandy bottom extends over 100 m toward the open sea and the waters shift from pale green to sapphire blue. Collect a few shells, but leave the inhabited ones: the Kei communities have strict customary rules on harvesting marine resources.

Langgur market before the airport — the Kei local markets are one of the rare places in eastern Indonesia where you'll find exotic fruits you won't see in Bali: papeda (sago porridge), kenari (Moluccan nuts, similar to almonds), fresh seafood landed that morning. The Kei canoe fishermen who sell their catch directly from their boats operate on a village-set communal pricing system.

→

Final morning snorkeling from the beach or a boat trip to the small islands around Kei Kecil before heading to the airport by midday. Tual Airport (Dumatubun) serves Ambon, Makassar and occasionally Jakarta directly — check connections as schedules are irregular depending on the season.
