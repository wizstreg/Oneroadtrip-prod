Alpes-Maritimes Road Trip: From the French Riviera to Perched Villages

French Riviera, Monaco, Mercantour and the Vallée des Merveilles: 13 days in France's most contrasting département.

📅 Best period May, June, September, October

The Alpes-Maritimes is France's only département where you can swim in the Mediterranean in the morning and hike at 2,500 meters altitude in the afternoon. This 13-day road trip descends from Nice to Monaco along the Riviera, climbs into the Roya and Var valleys to the Mercantour's edges, before descending toward Grasse, Saint-Paul-de-Vence and Cannes. Niçoise Baroque, Bronze Age carvings, the world capital of perfume and modern art collections unfold in a territory the size of two Swiss cantons.

Key highlights (4)
  • Nice: Promenade des Anglais and UNESCO-listed Old Nice, Baroque palaces and Niçoise cuisine
  • Vallée des Merveilles: 50,000 Bronze Age rock carvings on Mont Bégo's slopes
  • Grasse: UNESCO-inscribed world capital of perfume, 3,500 m² museum and creation workshops
  • Fondation Maeght: 13,000 works by Miró, Giacometti and Chagall in a 1964 architectural jewel

Nice

The Promenade des Anglais stretches for seven kilometers along the Baie des Anges, backed by Belle Époque palace hotels. It owes its name to British aristocrats who funded its construction from 1822 onward — Reverend Lewis Way launched the fundraising drive to employ local workers during a period of hardship. Settle into one of the blue chairs: designed in 1948 by artisan Charles Tordo, they've become so emblematic of Nice that they're now displayed in the MAMAC.

Lose yourself in Old Nice, a UNESCO World Heritage site since 2021: laundry hangs from windows, green shutters clatter against ochre and yellow facades, narrow streets twist toward hidden squares. The neighborhood owes its color palette to Genoese and Piedmontese influence — you'll sometimes feel you've stepped into Genoa itself.

Palais Lascaris is one of France's most discreet Baroque treasures — tucked into the narrow Rue Droite, so narrow you might pass by without glancing up. Built from 1648 for Jean-Baptiste Lascaris, a descendant of Byzantine aristocracy, it now houses one of France's two most important collections of early musical instruments: over 500 pieces donated by Nice notable Antoine Gautier in 1904, including a 1618 Baroque guitar and a flute by the inventor of the clarinet. Admission is free — don't walk past.

On Place Rossetti, the Cathédrale Sainte-Réparate displays its 17th-century Baroque facade. Inside, side chapels compete in gilding and polychrome marble in the Niçoise Baroque style. The square itself, with its cafe terraces lively until late in the evening, is one of Old Nice's most vibrant spots.

Climb the Colline du Château — the elevator from the waterfront is free, or take the 400 steps if you prefer to earn the view. At the top, a wooded park overlooks the entire city, an artificial waterfall murmurs, and the panorama sweeps from the Baie des Anges to the snow-capped Pre-Alps. The castle itself was razed by the Savoyards in 1706 — only the ruins and the sailors' cemetery remain.

Port Lympia, dug in the 18th century at the mouth of the Paillon River, displays red and yellow facades in Piedmontese Baroque style. It's one of Nice's most photogenic spots in late afternoon — reflections of the facades on the water, colorful boats and yachts create a unique atmosphere somewhere between fishing village and chic marina.

On Cimiez hill, the 2nd-century Roman arena sleeps beneath centuries-old olive trees. Nearby, the Matisse Museum occupies a red 17th-century villa — the collection traces the painter's entire oeuvre, who lived and died in Nice. In the neighboring Franciscan monastery rests Henri Matisse, alongside Raoul Dufy.

→

Stroll the Cours Saleya first thing in the morning: flower market stalls explode with color, socca vendors call to each other, the scent of tapenades and spices fills the air. On Mondays, antique dealers replace the florists — a different atmosphere, equally captivating.

→

The Promenade du Paillon is Nice's answer to New York's High Line: a 12-hectare green corridor runs through the city from Old Nice to the Museum of Modern Art. Children run through the water mirror's 3,200 jets, locals gather at all hours. Perfect for a break between visits.

→

Place Masséna, with its bright red buildings and black-and-white checkerboard ground, is the gateway to the Avenue Jean Médecin shops. At the center, seven illuminated statues represent the seven continents — an installation by Jaume Plensa that lights up after dark.

→

Don't miss the Russian Orthodox Cathedral of Saint Nicholas, whose five colored domes rise unexpectedly behind the train station. Erected between 1903 and 1912 at the request of Tsar Nicholas II, it's the largest Orthodox church outside Russia. The icon-adorned interior testifies to the Russian colony that wintered in Nice during the 19th century.

→

End the day on the pebbles of the Promenade des Anglais with a swim. Waters are generally calm in summer, but bring water shoes — the pebbles are treacherous on entry. Private beaches offer sun loungers if you prefer more comfort.

→

Sample Niçoise cuisine: golden socca sizzling on the griddle at Cours Saleya, pissaladière melting in your mouth, Swiss chard pie playing sweet and savory. For dinner, Old Nice restaurants serve pan bagnat, Niçoise daube and herb ravioli — specialties distinct from Provençal cuisine, inherited from the County of Savoy.

The Citadelle Saint-Elme has guarded the roadstead entrance since 1557, commissioned by Duke Emanuel Philibert of Savoy to defend his duchy against Barbary raids. Cross the drawbridge: the five-pointed star fortress houses shaded gardens, small municipal museums and the town hall today. The Italian-style military architecture, with its thick walls and moats, is remarkably well preserved.

The Chapelle Saint-Pierre, known as the Cocteau Chapel, is one of Jean Cocteau's major works on the French Riviera. This Parisian poet, filmmaker and artist had stayed for years at the Hôtel Welcome facing the chapel — he undertook its decoration in June 1956 as a gesture of friendship toward the fishermen who had entrusted him with the building. The decor was inaugurated on June 30, 1957, with Charlie Chaplin in attendance. Five fresco panels illustrate the life of Saint Peter, the gypsies of Saintes-Maries and the demoiselles of Villefranche. Listed as a historic monument in 1996.

The Rue Obscure is one of the French Riviera's most singular passages: 130 meters of 13th-century vaulted gallery plunged in darkness, running along the coast beneath the old town's houses. This former patrol path served as a shelter for residents during World War II bombardments. Enter from Place du Conseil to experience the atmosphere.

The Église Saint-Michel, perched atop the old town's stairs, unfolds its 17th-century Baroque splendor beneath imposing vaults. The historic organ in the gallery dates from the 18th century. Have a look at the polychrome wooden recumbent Christ — a remarkable piece of Niçoise Baroque.

Continue to the Darse, the former military port of the Kings of Sardinia: the quiet basin where remnants of the rope factory remain contrasts with the bustle of the marina. It's a calm spot, often overlooked by tourists, ideal for watching sailboats and traditional pointus.

→

Spread your towel on Plage des Marinières: fine sand and calm waters, sheltered by the natural roadstead — one of the deepest in the Mediterranean at 55 meters. The view of the Saint-Jean-Cap-Ferrat peninsula is superb. Arrive on a weekday if possible; weekends fill up quickly.

→

Settle on a terrace along Quai de l'Amiral Courbet: pink and orange 18th-century facades reflect in the water, fishing boats return in the morning with their catch, cafe terraces come alive in the evening. This is classic Villefranche — cartoon-like and utterly real at once.

→

Walk the ramparts along the Chemin de Ronde: the walls plunge straight into the sea, the view of the roadstead is striking, and late-afternoon light transforms the facades into painting. Allow 20 minutes for a leisurely walk.

→

The Villefranche roadstead is one of the deepest in the Mediterranean: the seafloor attracts divers and freedivers, with often excellent visibility. Operators offer diving baptisms departing from the port — a memorable experience in this sheltered roadstead.

→

Drive up to the Basse Corniche for panoramas: the view of Villefranche roadstead and the Saint-Jean-Cap-Ferrat peninsula, framed between hills and blue sky, is the emblematic French Riviera postcard. If you have time, continue to the Moyenne Corniche which climbs even higher.

Monaco

The village of Èze clings to its rocky pinnacle at 429 meters altitude — driving the Moyenne Corniche, you spot it from afar silhouetted against the sky. Pass through the medieval gate and lose yourself in the cobbled streets lined with ceramics boutiques and artisan perfumeries. Don't miss the sea-side lookout: on clear days, the view extends all the way to Corsica.

The Jardin Exotique d'Èze crowns the medieval castle ruins: century-old cacti, giant aloes, rare euphorbias and contemporary sculptures embedded in the rock. The 360° view of the Mediterranean and the Alps beyond is one of the coast's most spectacular. Wear good shoes — the paths are steep and uneven.

The Rock of Monaco, an 800-meter-long by 300-meter-wide rocky outcrop, has hosted the principality's old town since the 13th century. The neo-Romanesque Cathédrale Sainte-Dévote, consecrated in 1911, houses the tombs of the Grimaldi princes including Grace Kelly, who died in 1982. The 16th-18th century streets exude a surprising calm just steps from Monte-Carlo's bustle.

Witness the Changing of the Guard before the Prince's Palace: every morning at precisely 11:55 a.m., the carabinieri in Napoleonic uniforms execute the ceremony with military precision. The Palace itself has been inhabited by the Grimaldi family since 1297, when François Grimaldi took the fortress disguised as a Franciscan monk — a ruse that became Monaco's emblem.

The Oceanographic Museum has plunged into the abyss since 1910, founded by Prince Albert I, who devoted his fortune and life to ocean exploration. The collection of 6,000 marine species is one of the world's most comprehensive; the great Lagoon recreates a living coral reef at the museum's heart. Allow at least 2 hours — the deep-sea exhibition alone is worth the trip.

The Monte-Carlo Casino, designed by Charles Garnier in 1879 — the same architect as the Paris Opera — was inaugurated by Sarah Bernhardt before 400 workers who had labored day and night for six months. The onyx entrance hall, 28 marble columns and symbolist-painted salons are visible even without gambling. Note that Monégasques aren't allowed to enter: this rule dates back to Prince Charles III, who wanted to prevent his subjects from squandering their fortunes.

→

Descend the Nietzsche Path from Èze-Village to Èze-sur-Mer: 2 kilometers of steep trail through Mediterranean vegetation. Philosopher Friedrich Nietzsche walked it during his stays in the region, and he's said to have conceived ideas for "Thus Spoke Zarathustra" here. Allow 45 minutes descending, then return by train or bus.

→

Èze's perfumeries offer creation workshops: copper alembics, cold maceration secrets, shops with intoxicating scents. You compose your own perfume by blending essences — one hour's workshop is enough to leave with a personalized bottle.

→

Stroll through Monte-Carlo: a star-studded quarter of sumptuous villas, palatial hotels like the Hermitage or Paris, and yachts exceeding 60 meters moored at Port Hercule. The Formula 1 circuit passes through here in May — you'll see permanent safety rails in the tightest corners on the calendar.

→

The Princess Grace Rose Garden perfumes 4,000 rose bushes in a 3,500 m² park: this garden created in 1984 in memory of actress Grace Kelly, who died in 1982, shelters varieties from around the world. The roses sometimes bear celebrity names: come in spring to see them in full bloom.

→

Swimming at Larvotto Beach, Monaco's only public beach: fine sand and crystal-clear water directly at the foot of Monte-Carlo's high-rises. The beach is equipped and supervised in summer.

The Basilique Saint-Michel Archange has dominated the old town since its 17th-century construction: its ochre and yellow facade with two asymmetrical bell towers is Menton's most photographed image. Inside, glittering gold and polychrome marble illustrate Niçoise Baroque in full power. The pebble mosaic pavement is itself a work of art.

Lose yourself in Old Menton: cobbled lanes climbing toward the basilica, pastel facades pressed against each other, turquoise and pale yellow shutters, staircases transforming into shaded squares. The architecture recalls Ligurian villages across the border — Italy is just 2 km from here.

The Musée Jean Cocteau — Séverin Wunderman Collection is one of the French Riviera's most surprising museums: 1,800 works assembled by an American entrepreneur and Cocteau fan in a luminous contemporary building inaugurated in 2011. Cocteau had a strong connection with Menton — he decorated the town hall's marriage room in 1957-1958, just after Villefranche.

Le Bastion, a 17th-century fortress perched directly on the water at the old port entrance, was the first place dedicated to Cocteau's work during his lifetime. He decorated it himself in 1957. The terrace offers a view of the sea and the town — an intimate spot that crowds overlook.

Climb to the Vieux-Château cemetery: terraces stacked on the rocky promontory offer spectacular views over ochre-tiled roofs and the shimmering Mediterranean. Writer William Webb Ellis, inventor of rugby, rests here alongside Grand Tour travelers who came to die beneath Menton's sun.

The covered Halles market stalls overflow with local flavors: goat cheeses from the hills, truffle-perfumed tapenades, golden olive oil from the backcountry groves, and especially Menton lemons in every form — the local variety is sweeter, less acidic than ordinary lemons. The market runs every morning.

→

Wander the Jardins Biovès: flowered paths, pools and sculptures at year's start transform these gardens into the stage for the Lemon Festival — a festival held every February since 1934, where giant citrus constructions (145 tons of citrus in 2023) invade the paths.

→

The Promenade du Soleil unfurls its kilometer and a half bordered by palm trees between pebble beach and restaurants facing the azure. It's France's sunniest promenade — Menton claims 316 days of sunshine per year, a national record due to its position in a basin sheltered from winds.

→

Bring home a lemon souvenir: tangy jams, amber limoncello, perfumed soaps, lemon jelly in specialized shops around the market. Menton's citronella holds AOP status — check the label to ensure authenticity.

→

The coastal path toward Cap Martin winds 6 km between umbrella pines and secret coves, turquoise sea below. The trail passes Empress Eugénie's former villa, now a private residence. Walking shoes recommended for rocky passages.

→

Succumb to Menton lemon tart on a terrace: local pastry works Menton lemons with a fruity intensity ordinary lemons can't match. Accompanied by a limoncello spritz, it's the town's essential afternoon ritual.

The Pont-Vieux has spanned the Bévéra since the 13th century: Sospel's emblem, it once housed a toll station on the Salt Road linking Nice to Turin via the Tende Pass. The central tower pierced by an arch served as a toll booth. If you arrive in late afternoon, the light on the trompe-l'œil facades of riverside houses is particularly beautiful.

The Cathédrale Saint-Michel raises its 11th-century Romanesque bell tower at the old town's heart: it's one of the region's oldest and contrasts with the Baroque nave added in the 17th century. The interior houses a retable from the 15th-century Niçoise school attributed to François Bréa — don't miss the Virgin and Child in the side chapel.

Fort Saint-Roch is one of the alpine Maginot Line's most impressive fortifications: built between 1931 and 1933, it mobilized 5,000 m³ of concrete and 385 tons of steel. Its 2,000 meters of galleries carved 50 meters underground constituted a true subterranean city for 250 soldiers autonomous for three months. A 149 mm cannon weighing 13 tons remains. Bring a sweater — it's 14°C inside year-round.

Fort de l'Agaisen, built from 1932 to 1936 on Mont Agaisen, completes the defensive system around Sospel. Equipped with powerful artillery protecting neighboring forts, it offers guided tours in season led by passionate volunteers. The orientation table at the summit lets you locate all the valley's fortified structures.

The trompe-l'œil facades of the old town display ochre and pink hues characteristic of neighboring Liguria: Renaissance doorways, wrought-iron balconies, squares shaded by century-old plane trees. Sospel has preserved an authentic alpine town atmosphere, far from the coast's bustle.

→

Lace up your boots for the Bévéra valley: the GR 52 trail climbs toward the Col de Turini through chestnut forests and alpine pastures. In good weather, the Mediterranean and Mercantour peaks share the horizon from the viewpoints. The Col de Turini (1,607 m) is known to rally racing fans — it's a historic stage of the Monte-Carlo Rally.

→

Local operators offer electric mountain bike outings on Mercantour circuits: gentle routes for families in the valley or demanding single tracks for experienced riders. It's an excellent way to discover the backcountry without excessive effort.

→

The Gorges du Piaon are the area's canyoning paradise: rappel descents, natural slides, pools of fresh water in a setting of rock and forest. Supervised operators offer half-days accessible to beginners and families from age 8.

→

Thursday mornings, Place des Platanes comes alive for the weekly market: goat cheeses from alpine pastures, honey from Mercantour hives, olive oil from the hills, garden vegetables. An authentic village market, far from mass tourism.

The Franciscan monastery of Saorge is one of the region's architectural rarities: founded in 1633 by Recollect friars in the Roya valley, it presents a unique blend of sober Baroque typical of Franciscans — no marble or ostentatious gilding, but 17th-century frescoes of exceptional finesse. The cloister houses 23 frescoes illustrating the life of Saint Francis of Assisi, and a remarkable series of nine sundials painted around 1760. From the esplanade, the view of the amphitheater village and Roya gorges is striking.

Push open the door of Église Saint-Sauveur in the village: built in the 15th century then enriched in the 17th, it preserves an 1847 Italian organ transported by mule from Nice — a remarkable logistical feat for an instrument this size. The twisted-column Baroque decor and walnut woodwork testify to the care lavished on the valley's religious buildings.

Saorge's lanes constitute a visit in themselves: the "pountin" — vaulted passages typical of Ligurian architecture — wind between fountain squares and wash houses, tall houses stack up until they tumble into the valley. The entire village is classified as a historic monument. Three fortified castles once watched over this "Roya lock," a reputedly impregnable stronghold.

The violet flagstone roofs, extracted from local mountain quarries, give the village a unique color in the Niçoise backcountry. The tall houses — sometimes seven stories — pressed against the slope recall Ligurian perched villages. Take time to search for painted sundials on the facades: Saorge counts about twenty.

→

Parc National du Mercantour is within hiking distance from Saorge: the trail toward the rock carvings of the Vallée des Merveilles begins a few kilometers away, with representations of animals and figures from the Bronze Age 3,000 to 4,000 years old. If you don't have time for the trip to the Vallée des Merveilles, inquire at the Musée des Merveilles in Tende (next stop).

→

Board the Train des Merveilles on the Nice-Tende line from Breil-sur-Roya: tunnels carved through rock, viaducts suspended above gorges, dizzying panoramas over the Roya. This section built between 1906 and 1928 is one of France's most beautiful — a contemplative alternative to the road.

→

Cool off at the Bain du Sémite, accessible on foot from the village: a crystalline water spring nestled in a verdant setting, a natural pool formed by rock where residents have bathed for generations. A secret spot tourist guides rarely mention.

→

Follow the Baroque route from village to village in the Roya valley: each church harbors its treasure — historic organs, Niçoise school retables, 15th-century frescoes. The Menton-Riviera & Merveilles tourist office offers themed guides to explore this heritage trail.

→

Sit down at one of the village's rare restaurants to savor Roya valley cuisine: wild herb ravioli and local goat cheese feature prominently on menus. The valley's cuisine blends Niçoise and Piedmontese influences — a specialty found nowhere else.

The Musée des Merveilles is the essential gateway to the Vallée des Merveilles: designed by architect Jean-Michel Wilmotte, it presents archaeological collections across 1,200 m², with casts and photographs of rock carvings from Mont Bégo dating to the Bronze Age (2,000 to 1,500 BC). Over 50,000 carvings dot the massif's slopes — a unique open-air museum in Europe. If you plan to hike to the carvings, get information here on access conditions.

The medieval village of Tende climbs in an amphitheater up the hill: 4-to-6-story houses in local green schist, topped with flagstone roofs, squeeze into cobbled lanes. A former major stop on the Salt Road between Nice and Piedmont, Tende was only attached to France in 1947 — Italian identity remains very present in architecture and cuisine.

The Collégiale Notre-Dame de l'Assomption blends an 11th-century Romanesque bell tower with a 17th-century Baroque interior: this mix well illustrates Tende's history, a cultural crossroads between Ligurian, Piedmontese and French influences. The building's richness testifies to the town's past prosperity on the Salt Road.

Climb to the ruins of Château des Lascaris and the Tour de l'Horloge to overlook the village: the panorama embraces the Roya valley up to Mercantour peaks exceeding 3,000 meters. Ideal in late afternoon when golden light illuminates the flagstone roofs.

→

The Vallée des Merveilles is the unmissable excursion from Tende: over 50,000 Bronze Age rock carvings cover glacier-polished rocks around Mont Bégo. Independent access is free but an approved guide is strongly recommended to spot the carvings. Allow a full day for the hike from Castérino. Snow can make the site inaccessible before July.

→

Reach the hamlet of Castérino by car from Tende: this small hamlet at 1,550 m altitude is the starting point for hikes toward the Vallée des Merveilles and Vallée de Fontanalba. Along the way, watch for chamois, marmots and ibex that frequent the alpine pastures.

→

The Vallée de Fontanalba offers an alternative to the Vallée des Merveilles with its own rock carvings and spectacular alpine lake landscape. The hike is more accessible and less crowded. Local guides offer themed outings focused on alpine botany or wildlife.

→

Board the Train des Merveilles from Tende station: vertiginous tunnels, viaducts suspended above gorges, one of France's most spectacular railway lines built in 1928. The Nice-Tende-Cuneo line crosses alpine landscapes otherwise inaccessible.

Saint-Martin-Vésubie, nicknamed "the little Switzerland of Nice," is the tourist capital of Mercantour: at 1,000 m altitude, surrounded by peaks exceeding 3,000 meters, it offers access to 3,600 hectares of larch forests and alpine pastures. Stroll the alpine village lanes with wooden chalets, murmuring fountains and the canal running down the middle of the main street.

Centre Alpha "Le Temps du Loup" is the only structure of its kind in France: three natural habitats allow observation of wolves in semi-freedom at feeding time. Wolves were naturally reintroduced to Mercantour in the 1990s from Italy — the center helps understand the biology and behavior of these predators now at the center of debate. Book ahead in high season.

Continue to the Sanctuaire de la Madone de Fenestre at 1,903 meters altitude: this pilgrimage site accessible by winding road from Saint-Martin has been frequented since the Middle Ages. The view of the Gélas, culminating at 3,143 meters (highest point in the Alpes-Maritimes), is exceptional in good weather. The road is generally open from June to October.

→

Hike to Lac du Boréon from the hamlet of the same name at 1,473 m: pale blue waters reflecting larches, marmots whistling on rocks, ibex surveying the horizon from ridges. The lake is accessible in 2 hours of easy walking from the parking lot. Don't leave without checking the weather — afternoon storms are frequent in summer.

→

The Lacs de Prals trail links several alpine lakes in a landscape of flowered pastures and scree: a moderate-level hike of about 4 hours that rewards walkers with views of Mercantour's rocky needles. Bring a warm layer — temperature drops quickly in shade.

→

Lac des Trécolpas, 9.5 km via the Boréon valley, is one of Mercantour's most beautiful lakes: picturesque forests of Swiss stone pines and larches, rolling meadows and mountain streams mark the route. Guaranteed sightings of alpine fauna — brown trout, chamois and golden eagles.

→

Operators offer canyoning or aqua-hiking outings in the crystalline gorges around Saint-Martin: jumps, natural slides, rappel descents into turquoise pools at 8-10°C. Family formulas are accessible from age 8, "expert" outings descend technical gorges with 20-meter rappels.

The Gorges du Cians are one of the Alpes-Maritimes' most beautiful roads: the D28 plunges into a canyon where rock changes color over kilometers — white limestone at the start, then deep red schist surges at Pra d'Astier. Don't miss passages where the road skirts the torrent just meters above. Circulation is narrow — tricky crossings.

The Gorges de Daluis constitute the Alpes-Maritimes' most spectacular Regional Nature Reserve: classified for their 70 mineral species, the vertiginous walls of red pelites 250 million years old create a striking Niçoise Colorado. The Pont de la Mariée spans the void at 120 meters height, and the 17 tunnels pierced through red rock are a procession of light and shadow.

Valberg, a family resort at 1,669 meters altitude, is one of the rare resorts labeled Flocon Vert for its environmental commitment: streetlights turned off at midnight, free shuttles between hamlets, carpooling encouraged. Outside ski season, slopes transform into hiking trails and ski lifts can carry mountain bikes.

Valberg's planetary path guides young and old along a pedagogical loop representing the solar system to scale: each planet is proportionally spaced, giving a concrete sense of spatial immensities. Explanatory panels and spectacular alpine views accompany the walk.

Mont Mounier, culminating at 2,817 meters, is the emblematic peak of the Niçoise backcountry — the hike from Valberg is a full-day excursion rewarded by panoramas of the Southern Alps and the Mediterranean in clear weather. From the resort's viewpoints, even without climbing to the summit, the view over alpine pastures and larch forests is already remarkable.

→

Hiking at Mercantour's gates from Valberg: the GR 52A crosses alpine pastures dotted with génépi, rhododendrons and mountain flowers. Operators offer guided walks for all levels — from two-hour family walks to athletic ascents toward passes at 2,500 meters.

→

Mountain biking and road cycling on alpine circuits from Valberg: technical descents via forest tracks or panoramic routes on pass roads. Rental shops offer electric mountain bikes if you want to enjoy climbs without effort. The roads through the Gorges du Cians and Daluis are particularly spectacular by bike.

→

Night hike to observe stars from Valberg, labeled a "Dark Sky Reserve": far from any light pollution, local astronomy guides set up telescopes and point out constellations. A rare experience, to book on moonless nights between June and September.

Grasse has been the world capital of perfume since the 17th century: local know-how was inscribed on UNESCO's intangible cultural heritage list in 2018. Lose yourself in the old town's ochre and yellow-facade lanes, where 17th and 18th-century buildings adjoin 19th-century townhouses. The air itself is fragrant — fields of roses and jasmine surround the town from May to October.

The Musée International de la Parfumerie is the largest of its kind in the world: created in 1989 and renovated in 2008, its 3,500 m² trace 4,000 years of perfume history, from Egyptian canopic jars to Lalique bottles. Over 50,000 objects are preserved in its collections. The multi-sensory route with a hundred essences to smell is a unique experience — allow 1h30.

Visit one of the historic perfumeries — Fragonard (1926), Galimard (1747, the oldest) or Molinard (1849): copper alembics, enfleurage basins, creation laboratories and packaging rooms are accessible to visitors. Visits are free at all three houses — they naturally hope you'll buy from the boutique.

The Cathédrale Notre-Dame du Puy raises its 12th-century Romanesque walls at the historic center's heart: it preserves a painting by Fragonard (The Washing of the Feet), a Rubens and canvases by Louis Bréa. It's one of the rare places where you can see old masters' works in their original context, without display cases or protection.

Climb to the village of Gourdon, one of France's most beautiful villages perched at 760 meters on a vertiginous pinnacle: the 12th-century castle, remodeled in the 17th, is surrounded by gardens designed after Le Nôtre's model. The panorama from the terraces embraces the Mediterranean, Cap d'Antibes and — in exceptional weather — Corsica. Allow 20 minutes' drive from Grasse.

→

Perfume creation workshop at one of Grasse's perfumeries: guided by a "nose," you learn to distinguish olfactory families and compose your custom fragrance among hundreds of essences. Sessions last 1h to 2h depending on workshops — book ahead as groups are limited.

→

The Provençal market at Cours Honoré Cresp perfumes the lanes: tapenades, olive oils from Cagnes and Nice, Marseille soaps, Provençal herbs and of course products perfumed with rose or jasmine. Every morning except Monday.

→

The MIP Gardens at Mouans-Sartoux (10 km from Grasse) constitute a living conservatory of perfume plants: 700 species including May roses, jasmine, tuberoses and violets that supply Grasse perfumeries. The "Remarkable Garden" label has recognized this space since 2022 — an ideal complement to the museum.

→

Head down to Vallauris, the potters' village where Picasso settled in 1947 to learn ceramics: artisan tradition continues in dozens of workshop-galleries. The chapel decorated by Picasso with his War and Peace in 1952 is open to visitors.

Pass through the 14th-century Porte de Vence to enter the fortified village: one of the rampart cannons is the one captured during resistance against Charles V in 1536 — the Niçois came to help defend the village, and François I rewarded Saint-Paul with numerous privileges. Allow a morning to explore the village before tour buses invade Rue Grande.

Rue Grande traverses the fortified village from end to end over 300 meters: art galleries, artists' studios, beige limestone medieval facades. Picasso, Chagall and Prévert strolled here — Chagall lived his final years here and rests in the village cemetery, grave n°152 aisle C.

The 17th-century Chapelle des Pénitents Blancs was entirely decorated by Belgian artist Jean-Michel Folon between 1997 and 2008: floor mosaics, stained glass, murals and a monumental sculpture transform this space into a poetic world of winged figures and deep colors. A surprise in this medieval setting.

Climb to the 16th-century bastion for the panorama: the view of the Baous chain, Mont Chauve and the backcountry hills is one of the village's finest. Below, the cemetery where Chagall rests — a sober and touching visit.

→

The Fondation Maeght is France's first private art foundation — inaugurated on July 28, 1964, by André Malraux — and one of Europe's most important collections with 13,000 works. Miró, Giacometti, Chagall, Braque and Calder participated in designing the building with Catalan architect Josep Lluís Sert: the Giacometti courtyard, Miró labyrinth and Chagall's wall mosaics are part of the architecture itself. Allow at least 2 hours.

→

Walk the Chemin de Ronde on the ramparts: the plunging view over village rooftops and the Niçoise countryside is what photographers seek. The path makes a complete circuit of the fortifications in 20 minutes.

→

Place du Général de Gaulle comes alive beneath century-old plane trees: pétanque games here are legendary — Montand, Ventura and Lino regularly faced off in the 1960s-70s. The tradition continues with local players from morning to evening.

→

Continue to Tourrettes-sur-Loup, the "violet village" 15 minutes away: cobbled lanes wind between medieval stone houses, potter and weaver artisans have taken up residence in the town's arcades. The Fête des Violettes has been held every March since 1952 — off-season, the village is peaceful and authentic.

→

Art galleries line every Saint-Paul lane: paintings, sculptures, ceramics in spaces that were sometimes the masters' studios. The spirit of 20th-century artists still hovers — even if commerce has taken over, some galleries present high-quality works.

The Musée Picasso occupies Château Grimaldi, the former acropolis of the Greek city of Antipolis. In autumn 1946, Picasso set up his studio there at the invitation of curator Dor de la Souchère: he worked solidly for two months and left 23 paintings and 44 drawings on deposit when leaving for Paris. Among them, La Joie de Vivre and Le Gobeur d'Oursins. The museum officially became the Picasso Museum on December 27, 1966 — the world's first dedicated to the artist.

Old Antibes is organized around its ancient ramparts: Provençal lanes, pastel-colored facades, colorful shutters and the Cathédrale de l'Immaculée-Conception whose 17th-century carved wooden doors are remarkable. The Provençal market at Cours Masséna runs every morning except Monday — one of the French Riviera's finest.

Fort Carré, built in the 16th century on a rocky peninsula, raises its four bastions 26 meters above the sea. Napoleon Bonaparte was briefly imprisoned here in 1794. The panoramic view from the ramparts embraces the Baie des Anges, Nice and the Pre-Alps — even more spectacular from the north bastions.

Stroll Port Vauban, one of the Mediterranean's largest marinas with 1,650 berths: yachts over 50 meters are moored at the "billionaires' quay." The architecture of Belle Époque buildings facing the basin contrasts with the ultra-modern lines of boats — a spectacle in itself.

The Safranier quarter, wedged between ramparts and pedestrian lanes, has proclaimed itself a "free commune" since 1966: humorous signs claim autonomy for this neighborhood of a few dozen flower-covered houses. A bohemian, relaxed atmosphere steps from luxury boutiques.

→

The Sentier du Tire-Poil traces Cap d'Antibes for 5 kilometers between limestone cliffs and turquoise sea: secret coves accessible by scrambling over rocks, sumptuous villas hidden in vegetation on the road's other side. It's one of the French Riviera's most prized coastal walks. Arrive early — parking fills quickly in summer.

→

Swimming at Plage de la Gravette, Old Antibes' only free sand beach: directly at the ramparts' foot, sheltered by the port, waters here are generally calm and crystalline. A rarity on this stretch of coast where private beaches dominate.

→

Climb to the Phare de la Garoupe: a hundred steps to the lantern, then 180° panorama over sea, Mercantour and the Estérel massif. The neighboring Chapelle Notre-Dame de Bon Port houses a collection of fishermen's ex-votos from the 16th to 20th century — touching and popular paintings.

→

Discover Villa Eilenroc at Cap d'Antibes: neoclassical château designed by Charles Garnier in 1867 — the same architect as the Paris Opera — surrounded by 11 hectares of remarkable gardens. The villa opens to walkers on Wednesdays from September to June, and the sea-side gardens are accessible on fine days.

La Croisette unfurls its two legendary kilometers between fine-sand beaches, Belle Époque palace hotels and luxury boutiques. The boulevard was developed in the 19th century and owes its name to the calvary that stood there then. Walk on the sea side to avoid pavement crowds and observe gigantic yachts moored offshore.

Climb to Le Suquet, the medieval quarter perched on its hill: cobbled lanes rising toward the 12th-century Tour du Mont Chevalier offer the most authentic view of Cannes bay. The Musée de la Castre, installed in the castle, presents archaeological and ethnographic collections from around the world.

The Palais des Festivals raises its modernist architecture facing the sea since 1982: climb the famous steps where stars parade every May during the Cannes Film Festival, founded in 1946. Search for celebrity handprints sealed in concrete on the Allée des Étoiles.

Cannes' painted walls pay homage to cinema throughout the city: giant frescoes celebrating great film posters in different neighborhoods. A pedestrian circuit lets you discover them following the map available at the tourist office.

Stroll the Vieux-Port quays: colorful fishing boats alongside ferries to the Lérins islands, lively terraces, daily spectacle of sailboats and traditional pointus. This is locals' Cannes, far from La Croisette's glamour.

→

Head to the Îles de Lérins, accessible in 15 minutes by boat from the Vieux-Port: Île Sainte-Marguerite houses Fort Royal where the mysterious Man in the Iron Mask was imprisoned between 1687 and 1698 (his identity remains unknown), and Île Saint-Honorat has welcomed Cistercian monks since the 5th century. Regular boats depart all day — plan Île Saint-Honorat for Wednesday morning when monks chant lauds.

→

Swimming on La Croisette's fine-sand beaches or in the wilder coves of the Lérins islands: waters are generally calm and translucent. La Croisette's private beaches offer sun loungers but Pointe Croisette's public beaches are just as pleasant.

→

Marché Forville is Cannes' true belly: cut flowers, regional fruits and vegetables, alpine cheeses, locally-caught fish, marinated olives. Every morning except Monday. This is where palace chefs stock up from dawn.

→

In the evening, La Croisette lights up: the Carlton, Majestic and Martinez palaces unfold their terraces, haute couture boutiques gleam behind their windows. Even if you're just looking, it's a show in itself — and a cafe terrace facing the sea at twilight is one of the most pleasant ways to end this road trip.
