Welcome to Manado, capital of North Sulawesi and gateway to some of Indonesia's richest underwater treasures. The city's name likely derives from the Portuguese manadas, meaning "cluster of offshore islands" — a hint at its seafaring calling since the 15th century. The Portuguese landed here in 1511, followed by the Spanish, the Dutch and the British, all vying for control of this trading post over two centuries. Present-day Manado is home to around 460,000 people and proudly calls itself the "City of a Thousand Churches" — no exaggeration: Christian places of worship line every other intersection, a legacy of the Dutch Protestant mission in the 17th century. Stroll along the Boulevard, the city's waterfront esplanade, to soak in the local atmosphere before diving into its streets.

The Ban Hin Kiong Temple, in Manado's Chinatown district, is recognised as the oldest Taoist temple in eastern Indonesia. Founded in 1819 during the Qing dynasty, it was originally built from bamboo before being rebuilt in stone. Its dragon statues, pagodas and monks in orange robes make it a peaceful cultural stop amid the city's bustle. Come early in the morning to catch the surrounding market in full swing and a more contemplative atmosphere.

The North Sulawesi State Museum (Museum Negeri Provinsi Sulawesi Utara) showcases prehistoric artefacts, ceremonial costumes of the Minahasa people, traditional musical instruments, and objects from the Dutch colonial era and the Japanese occupation during World War II. If you only visit one thing in Manado before heading out to the islands, this is the place — it puts everything you're about to discover into context.

→

In the evening, head to Pasar Jengki, the night market known as "Manado's kitchen". This market, rooted in local life for generations, brims with fresh seafood, spices and classic Minahasa dishes: tinutuan (rice porridge with mixed vegetables), cakalang fufu (wood-smoked tuna) and fiery preparations made with rica-rica (red chilli paste). Minahasa cuisine is reputed as one of the spiciest in all of Indonesia — fair warning if your chilli tolerance is low.

Depart by 8 AM from Bahowo or Molas pier towards Bunaken Island, a 30-minute boat ride away. Bunaken National Marine Park was officially established in 1991 — one of Indonesia's earliest marine parks. It covers 890 km², of which 97% is marine environment, and encompasses five islands: Bunaken, Manado Tua (an extinct volcano rising in a perfect cone over 600 m), Siladen, Mantehage and Nain. The park harbours 390 coral species and around 2,000 fish species — more than 70% of all known fish species in Indonesian waters. The geological peculiarity: the absence of a continental shelf means the seabed plunges directly from a few metres to over 1,800 m. These sheer vertical walls of 25 to 50 metres are what made Bunaken famous.

Manado Tua, the volcanic silhouette standing out on the horizon from the boat, is worth the trip on its own. The island has been inhabited for generations by fishing families whose ancestors fled the volcano during an ancient eruption — hence its name "Old Manado". The Muka Gereja dive site (literally "facing the church") at the foot of the volcano is one of the most spectacular in the park: massive coral heads, schools of parrotfish and a strong current suited to experienced divers.

→

Diving or snorkelling on the Lekuan Walls (Lekuan I, II and III) — Bunaken's most iconic sites. These vertical walls drop over 100 metres and are covered in giant sponges, sea fans and colourful hard corals. Lekuan I alone hosts about a dozen green turtles that divers regularly encounter between 5 and 30 metres. Napoleon wrasse (up to 2 m), eagle rays, whitetip reef sharks and schools of barracuda patrol these walls. For beginners or snorkellers, Lekuan III offers the same species in shallow areas. Operators based on the island offer packages of 2 to 3 dives per day with full equipment.

Spend your second day at Bunaken exploring the less crowded sites. Mandolin Point, named after the "harmony" divers feel here, is an outstanding wall that reveals something different at every depth — spectacular at 5 m just as much as at 35 m. Fukui Point is the only site in Bunaken that offers a gently sloping reef instead of a vertical wall: ideal for snorkelling and for watching schools of bumphead parrotfish grazing on hard corals near the surface. In 1997, it was in the lava tubes of Manado Tua that the coelacanth — a "living fossil" that first appeared 400 million years ago — was filmed in situ for the first time anywhere in the world. If you're lucky during deep dives around Manado Tua, you're in its habitat.

Siladen Island, the smallest and least known of the park, lies 8 km east of Bunaken. Its south-western wall is one of the most colourful in the park, home to swarms of damselfish, rare nudibranchs and schools of snappers. The shallow reef surface is perfect for calm snorkelling with crystal-clear visibility. If you're staying overnight on Bunaken, Siladen is typically the afternoon destination.

→

Night dive on Bunaken's fringing reef. The nocturnal scene reveals an entirely different cast of creatures: octopuses hunting in the open, coral crabs, moray eels on patrol and pygmy seahorses hiding in whip corals. The bioluminescence of certain corals makes Bunaken's night dives especially photogenic. Bring your own torch or check that your operator provides one.

→

Take a walk through Bunaken village to observe the daily life of fishermen and families living within the park. Much of the population still depends on traditional fishing and seaweed farming — some have since shifted to dive tourism. The sunrise from the island's beach, facing the cone of Manado Tua, is one of the most striking views of the entire trip.

The Tangkoko-Batuangus Nature Reserve, at the foot of Mount Dua Saudara (1,361 m), covers 8,718 hectares of lowland rainforest — one of the largest intact forests in the Minahasa region. The reserve was established in 1919 to protect its endemic species. Today it shelters at least 127 mammal species, 233 bird species and 104 reptile and amphibian species, the vast majority of which are endemic to Sulawesi. The most endangered include the crested black macaque (Macaca nigra), with only around 5,500 individuals remaining across the entire island, and the spectral tarsier, the world's smallest primate. Between 1978 and 1993, hunting had driven the macaque population down by 75% — this reserve is their last viable refuge.

The black volcanic beach of Batuputih, at the reserve entrance, is one of the region's most unusual landscapes. The dark sand contrasts sharply with the dense forest and the turquoise waters of the gulf. Flights of knobbed hornbills (Rhyticeros cassidix) — the emblem of South Sulawesi province — regularly sweep across the canopy above the beach. If you're here at sunrise, macaque troops often descend to the shoreline to forage in the shallows.

→

Trek through the reserve with a local guide — mandatory for park access. The late-afternoon outing (departing around 3:30 PM) is the best option: you first observe black macaques in full social mode — troops can number up to 100 individuals, and some are so accustomed to researchers that they'll let you approach within a metre — then search for tarsiers as dusk falls. The spectral tarsier measures 10 to 15 cm and weighs 80 to 100 grams: its eyes, larger than its brain, allow it to see in total darkness. Never shine a torch directly into their eyes. Local guides from Batuputih Bawah village will accompany you for a 2- to 3-hour walk.

→

If you stay overnight at Tangkoko, the early-morning trek the next day offers the best chances of spotting knobbed hornbills in the canopy and macaques returning from the coast. Some operators also run traditional canoe trips through the mangroves to observe herons, kingfishers and monitor lizards. Ask at your accommodation the evening before.

Tomohon, nicknamed the "City of Flowers", is a highland town nestled between two active volcanoes — Lokon (1,580 m) and Mahawu (1,324 m) — at roughly 800 m elevation. The air is cool, the gardens are lush and the views across the Minahasa highlands are stunning. Start with the Pasar Extrem, the most unusual — and most controversial — market in Sulawesi. This traditional Minahasa market sells fruit, vegetables, spices and local treats like cucur (sweet fritters made with palm sugar). It earned its nickname from the exotic meat section — wild boar, bats, snakes and other local animals — reflecting the ancestral food traditions of a mountain-dwelling people. Come with an open mind and a ready stomach.

Lake Linow, a few kilometres from Tomohon, is a volcanic lake whose colour shifts throughout the day: emerald green, Prussian blue, milky white and sometimes deep red. This phenomenon is caused by changing sulphur levels and lighting conditions. Several lakeside cafés let you sit back and watch these colour changes unfold over a cup of highland coffee. The steam rising intermittently from the surface is a reminder that the geology here is very much alive.

→

Hike to the crater of Mount Mahawu. The ascent from the car park takes about an hour on a concrete-step trail — suitable for beginners. At the summit, a green crater lake sits amid dense tropical vegetation. On a clear day, the view stretches to Manado, Bunaken, Lembeh Island and the Minahasa volcanic chain. Leave early in the morning for the best visibility before clouds build up.

→

In the late afternoon, drive to Lake Tondano, the largest lake in North Sulawesi, about 30 minutes from Tomohon along a mountain road. Sitting at 600 m elevation inside the caldera of the ancient Burpa volcano, its shores are lined with fish restaurants serving nike (a species endemic to the lake). Order grilled nike fish with rice and chilli sauce while watching the sunset over the mountains — the most local table you'll find anywhere in the region.

Top hotels
10 Kasuari Homestay by Ipoeng RB&B

Gorontalo, capital of the province bearing the same name, is a city often overlooked by travellers rushing to reach the Togean Islands. That's a mistake. The town grew around a federation of five pre-colonial kingdoms before attracting Portuguese interest in the 16th century. Otanaha Fortress, perched on a hill overlooking the bay, is one of the few surviving traces of that presence: three round stone towers built in the 15th century by the Portuguese, with panoramic views over the city and Lake Limboto below. Climb the steps in the late afternoon for the golden light.

Lake Limboto, visible from the fortress tower, stretches across the heart of Gorontalo province. From the Tower of Majesty viewpoint (Menara Keagungan), the vista over the lake and surrounding fishing villages is especially beautiful at sunset. The lake is shallow (2 to 4 m) but rich in local aquatic biodiversity, and its banks are home to communities of traditional fishermen.

→

Snorkelling or diving at Olele Marine Park, a 30-minute drive from town. This site, still largely unknown to international tourists, is often compared to a surrealist Salvador Dalí canvas underwater: its bizarrely shaped coral formations, giant sponges and canyon-like reefs are unique in the entire region. Visibility is excellent and currents are generally mild. Local operators run trips from Olele beach.

→

In season (mainly November to April), the whale sharks of Botubarani are the region's most extraordinary attraction. At Botubarani, 20 minutes east of town, whale sharks — the largest fish in the world, reaching up to 12 metres — come to feed at the surface just a few dozen metres from shore, drawn in by shrimp brought by local boats. The experience involves swimming alongside them from a boat or from the beach. Their presence is not guaranteed — check with local operators the day before.

Spend the morning exploring Gorontalo city before the evening departure. The floating Baiturrahim Mosque, built on a platform at the water's edge, is one of the city's most photographed landmarks. Its neo-classical white-and-green architecture reflects beautifully in the waters of Tomini Bay on a clear day. Dress respectfully if you wish to enter.

If time allows, a boat trip to Saronde Island, about an hour north of Gorontalo, offers a day on a white-sand beach surrounded by turquoise water with very few visitors. Alternatively, head to the famous Pulo Cinta (Love Island), a heart-shaped island perched on a sandbar in Tomini Bay — one of Sulawesi's best-known romantic attractions. Local operators run morning transfers from Gorontalo harbour.

→

In the evening, board the KM Tuna Tomini ferry from Gorontalo harbour. The ferry departs on Tuesdays and Fridays around 6 PM bound for Wakai in the Togean Islands. The crossing takes approximately 12 hours — bring a hammock, a light sleeping bag and some food. The overnight passage across Tomini Bay is an experience in itself: the Sulawesi sky, far from any light pollution, is breathtaking. Arrival at Wakai in the early morning hours.

Arrival at Wakai, on Batudaka Island, in the Togean Islands — an archipelago of 56 islands and islets in the middle of the Gulf of Tomini, designated a National Park in 2004. Sailors describe the Gulf of Tomini as one of the calmest deep-water bays in the world, sheltered by the two arms of Sulawesi that flank it. The Togean Islands feature all three types of coral reef within a single area — fringing reefs, barrier reefs and atolls — a combination found nowhere else on Earth according to marine biologists. The archipelago supports roughly 262 coral species and 596 fish species. Settle into one of the small coastal bungalows on Kadidiri or Togean Island — accommodation is basic but the reefs are accessible right from the beach.

Lake Mariona (also known as Jellyfish Lake) on Togean Island is one of the most improbable experiences you can have in Indonesia. This ancient marine lake, formed in a limestone sinkhole flooded by seawater, is home to two species of jellyfish that have lost their stinging cells through evolution — having no predators left in this enclosed lake. You can swim (without fins, to avoid harming them) among millions of translucent jellyfish drifting slowly around you. It is one of the very few places in the world where this is possible.

→

Snorkelling or diving on the Togean reefs. Unlike Bunaken, diving here is still largely wild and uncrowded. The best-known site is the wreck of an American B-24 bomber from World War II, which went down on 3 May 1945 during a mission over Sulawesi. The 17-metre aircraft rests intact at 18 m depth on a sandy bed, its machine guns still visible. Hundreds of bigeye trevally swirl around it constantly. Operators on Kadidiri Island arrange dive trips to this site and other reefs.

Don't leave the Togean Islands without visiting a Bajo village. The Bajo (or Badjao) are known as the "sea gypsies" — a semi-nomadic people, 25,000 of whom live in the Togean Islands in stilt houses connected by wooden walkways above the water. They are renowned for their extraordinary breath-holding abilities, inherited from generations of open-water fishing. Pulau Papan, connected by a 1.3-km bridge, is the most accessible Bajo settlement in the archipelago. Approach with respect: avoid photographing without permission.

Una Una Island, off the beaten tourist track, is the wildest in the archipelago. Its active volcano forms a perfect cone visible from afar. The dive sites around Una Una are among the most diverse in the Togean Islands: giant sponges, massive sea fans, dense schools of trevally and barracuda, and reefs less affected by coral bleaching than sites in the south of the archipelago. Few operators service Una Una — ask around as soon as you arrive at Wakai.

→

Free day on the Togean beaches. The white-sand beaches of the surrounding small islets — reachable in 15 to 30 minutes by boat from Kadidiri — are among the most pristine in Indonesia. Bring your own provisions as there's nothing on the islets. Ask your accommodation to arrange a custom boat tour to the most remote reefs and beaches. This is the place to switch off completely — some lodges have no Wi-Fi and few have electricity around the clock.

Palu, capital of Central Sulawesi, is a transit city between the Togean Islands and the rest of the island. Take the return ferry to Ampana or Gorontalo depending on available schedules, then reach Palu by road or by flight from Gorontalo. The city sits at the head of a long, narrow bay — one of Indonesia's most distinctive micro-bays, ringed by mountains that block rainfall and give it one of the lowest precipitation rates in the country. Palu was severely hit by the 2018 earthquake and tsunami — the scars are still visible in some neighbourhoods. A sober walk along the bay shore helps convey the brutal volcanic geology of Sulawesi.

Lore Lindu National Park, about an hour southeast of Palu, is a UNESCO Biosphere Reserve spanning 217,000 hectares of montane tropical forest. It shelters enigmatic megaliths — anthropomorphic stone statues dating from the Bronze Age whose origin and purpose remain a mystery — along with 77 endemic bird species, Tonkean macaques and Dian's tarsiers. If you have time in Palu, a half-day excursion to the megaliths of the Bada Valley is one of the most otherworldly experiences in Central Sulawesi.

→

Evening stroll on Taman Ria beach. This Palu waterfront is the locals' promenade, lined with street-food vendors, fish barbecues and a laid-back atmosphere. Try the local ikan bakar — whole fish grilled over coals, served with rice and chilli sauce — before getting ready for tomorrow's flight to Makassar.

Makassar (formerly Ujung Pandang) is Indonesia's fifth-largest city and the capital of South Sulawesi — a maritime port city that wrote much of the archipelago's seafaring history. Fort Rotterdam is the city's most iconic landmark: this sea-turtle-shaped fort (Benteng Penyu) was originally built in 1545 by King Tunipalangga of the Gowa dynasty — the tenth ruler of the kingdom. The current outer walls of 6-metre-high volcanic stone were rebuilt by Dutch Admiral Cornelis Speelman after his conquest in 1667, and named after his hometown in the Netherlands. The fort later served as a colonial prison: this is where Javanese national hero Prince Diponegoro was imprisoned for 25 years until his death in 1855. Inside, the La Galigo Museum displays prehistoric artefacts, Bugis costumes and objects from the great Bugis-Makassar oral epic tradition.

Pantai Losari is Makassar's waterfront promenade: 1 km of seafront with no actual sandy beach, but plenty of street-food vendors, the floating Masjid Amirul Mukminin mosque, and crowds of locals gathering every evening to watch the sunset over the Makassar Strait. It's the social heartbeat of the city. Plan to be there around 5:30 PM for the golden hour.

Paotere Harbour, 3 km north of Fort Rotterdam, is the traditional port of the pinisi — the tall twin-masted sailing ships built for centuries by the Bugis shipwrights of Sulawesi. These wooden schooners still carry goods across the Indonesian archipelago today. Come early in the morning to see crews loading and unloading, brightly painted hulls lined up along the quay.

→

Dinner at one of the Losari waterfront restaurants or in the alleys behind the fort. Makassar's specialities are not to be missed: coto makassar (thick, spicy beef and offal soup served with ketupat — pressed rice cakes), konro bakar (grilled beef ribs in a dark spicy sauce), and es pisang ijo (green banana ice cream, the city's signature dessert). These are dishes you won't find at the same level outside Makassar.

Day trip to Rammang-Rammang, 40 km north of Makassar in the Maros district. This is the second-largest karst landscape in the world — after Guangxi in China — with black limestone towers of 50 to 300 metres surging from rice paddies and mangroves. Traditional boats take you along the Pute River as it winds at the foot of sheer, vegetation-draped cliffs. The atmosphere feels like a fantasy film set. A network of paths runs through Rammang-Rammang village, home to families of fishermen and farmers, with no other tourism in sight.

The Leang-Leang caves, within the same Maros karst massif, hold some of the oldest cave paintings in the world. In 2021, researchers dated one of the warty pig depictions in Leang Tedongnge cave to at least 45,500 years ago — the oldest known figurative painting to date. Hand stencils and animals in red ochre are still visible on the walls. A guide is essential and some caves require special access permission.

→

Return to Makassar in the evening with a stop at Bantimurung — dubbed the "Kingdom of Butterflies" by naturalist Alfred Russel Wallace, who worked there between 1856 and 1857. This national park is home to at least 250 butterfly species, including 20 that are protected. Wallace, who developed his theory of natural selection independently of Darwin, described Bantimurung as one of his favourite study sites in Asia. An on-site museum traces his time there.

Arrival in Rantepao, the main town of Tana Toraja, after a roughly 6-hour mountain drive from Makassar — a magnificent route through terraced rice fields, bamboo forests and the highlands of South Sulawesi. The Toraja are an Austronesian highland people numbering over one million. The word toraja comes from the Bugis to riaja: "people of the uplands". The Dutch colonial government officially gave them this name in 1909. Their ancestral belief system, Aluk To Dolo (the Way of the Ancestors), structures all social life around the cult of the dead. Most Toraja are Christian today, but the ancestral rites continue with undiminished intensity.

Visit Kete Kesu village, 4 km from Rantepao — one of the best-preserved and most accessible tongkonan villages in the region. Tongkonan are the ancestral houses of the Toraja: built on stilts, their upward-curving roofs — shaped like an upside-down boat — are instantly recognisable. Tradition holds that this design recalls the vessels on which the Toraja ancestors first arrived in Sulawesi. The walls are covered with pa'ssura — geometric motifs carved and painted in black, red, white and yellow, the four sacred Toraja colours, which "read" the family's history and cosmology. Facing the tongkonan stand the rice granaries (alang) of the same shape, forming an architectural ensemble found nowhere else in the world.

→

Walk through the terraced rice fields around Rantepao at sunset. The Toraja rice paddies, carved into steep hillsides, are farmed using ancestral methods. This is also when the villages come alive — families returning from the fields, children playing in the lanes between the granaries. If you're invited to share a meal in someone's home, don't turn it down: Toraja cooking (pa'piong — meat cooked in bamboo — and highland Toraja arabica coffee) is outstanding.

Lemo is the most spectacular and most visited burial site in Tana Toraja. On a vertical limestone cliff face several dozen metres high, scores of burial niches have been carved by hand by to pande batu — specialist stone carvers hired by families to suspend their dead in the rock. The work could take months. In front of each tomb, a balcony holds the tau-tau: a wooden statue sculpted and painted in the likeness of the deceased, dressed in fresh clothes and gazing downward — toward the living. Toraja tradition holds that the dead are not truly gone until the funeral ceremony has been completed — until then, the body is kept in the ancestral house, dressed and fed as if merely ill. These customs can delay the ceremony by months or even years after biological death.

The Londa burial cave, a few kilometres from Lemo, is a complex of natural caverns where generations of Toraja families have been laid to rest. You enter with a kerosene lamp held by a local guide. Inside: ancestral coffins stacked one on top of another, skulls lined up on stone ledges, scattered bones. From the base of the cliff, a group of life-size tau-tau watches over the entrance from a carved wooden balcony. It is at once moving and profoundly unsettling to Western eyes.

Climb to the Buntu Pune or Batutumonga viewpoint for a single sweeping panorama of the entire Tana Toraja landscape: endless terraced rice fields, orange-and-red tongkonan scattered across the valleys, and clouds clinging to the forested peaks. This is the image you'll carry home from this trip.

→

If you're here between June and August, you may be able to attend a Rambu Solo (or Rambu Tuka) funeral ceremony. This is the funeral season — after the rice harvest and before the new planting. These ceremonies can last from 3 days for a modest family to over two weeks for a noble one. Dozens to several hundred water buffaloes (karabao) are sacrificed — the horns, mounted on the front of the family tongkonan, testify to status and wealth. Thousands of people may attend. Foreign visitors are generally welcome provided they bring a symbolic gift (cigarettes, sugar, coffee) and dress modestly. Don't photograph without asking.

The tree burial site of Kambira follows a unique custom: babies who die before cutting their first tooth (considered too "pure" to be buried in the ground) are placed in cavities hollowed into the trunks of living ficus trees. The tree gradually closes the wound around the small coffin, "absorbing" the child into its sap. Several dozen trees here have been used this way over multiple generations. The practice still continues today in some families.

Palawa village, 12 km north of Rantepao, is considered one of the best-preserved tongkonan villages in all of Toraja. The front of the main ancestral house is covered in rows of buffalo horns from sacrifices spanning several generations — the backbone of Toraja social status. Some façades display hundreds. The village has been listed among official tourist sites since 1987.

→

Toraja arabica coffee tasting at one of the highland plantations around Rantepao. Selayar is a north-south elongated island of 2,000 km² in South Sulawesi, separated from the mainland by a narrow strait. The island sees few foreign tourists and offers a rural, peaceful side of Sulawesi: rice fields, coconut palms, fishermen and early-morning local markets. The capital, Benteng, concentrates the administrative life and the handful of hotels, but it's the marine environment that makes the island's reputation.

Off the coast of Selayar lies Takabonerate Atoll — the third-largest coral atoll in the world and the largest in Asia, covering 220,000 hectares of reef. Its waters harbour some of the most diverse corals in the Flores Sea. Boat excursions from Selayar allow for a full day of snorkelling and diving in waters of exceptional clarity. Count on about 2 hours each way by boat from Benteng.

→

Snorkelling on the fringing reefs close to the island, accessible from Baloiya beach or from the coastal fishing villages. Selayar's reefs are far less crowded than those of Bunaken or Wakatobi, and some remain completely intact. It's your chance to dive in near-total solitude alongside clownfish, stingrays and schools of blue surgeonfish.

Welcome to Wakatobi — the name is an acronym of the four main islands: Wangi-Wangi, Kaledupa, Tomia and Binongko. This national marine park covering 1.39 million hectares was established in 1996 and designated a UNESCO Biosphere Reserve in 2012. Explorer Jacques Cousteau reportedly called it an "underwater nirvana". The numbers are staggering: over 750 coral species, 942 recorded fish species — roughly five times the coral diversity of Hawaii — and 50 charted dive sites. Average visibility is 35 metres. With no rivers or streams carrying sediment, the water stays perfectly clear year-round. Wakatobi boasts Indonesia's largest barrier reef — the second-largest in Asia after Australia's Great Barrier Reef.

The Bajo stilt villages in Wangi-Wangi Bay are one of the archipelago's most powerful cultural images. The Bajo (or Bajau) people, often called "sea gypsies", have lived in these houses suspended above the water for generations. They are celebrated in the scientific world for their extraordinary breath-holding ability — some can hold their breath for over 13 minutes and dive to depths beyond 60 metres. A 2018 study found that their spleens are naturally 50% larger than those of their land-dwelling neighbours, enabling them to store more oxygen.

→

First dive at Roma Reef, one of the most photographed sites in Wakatobi. This pinnacle coral site features two giant 300-year-old coral disc formations known as "The Rose" — two massive coral flowers several metres across. Around them, a dense parade of life: leopard groupers, eagle rays, schools of fusiliers and blue surgeonfish. The site is suitable for all levels. Book as soon as you arrive with operators on Wangi-Wangi.

Tomia, the third island of the archipelago, is widely regarded as offering the best diving in Wakatobi. Sites like Fan Garden — a wall draped in huge sea fans and soft corals, swept by schools of bumphead parrotfish — and Magnifica — a gently sloping reef ideal for drift diving among snappers, skipjack and giant barracuda — are regularly cited among the ten best dive sites in Southeast Asia. Tomia is home to an internationally renowned dive resort that provides exclusive access to certain sites.

Kaledupa hosts the atoll of the same name — one of the most pristine coral formations in the park. A boat tour around the atoll offers chances to spot dolphins (bottlenose, Risso's and spinner dolphins are regulars in these waters) and, with luck, sperm whales and pilot whales migrating through the depths of the Banda Sea.

→

For experienced divers, The Blade — a cluster of seamounts spread across 200 metres in a rarely visited area — is one of Wakatobi's most intense dives. The site is only accessible in good weather and favourable current conditions. Hammerhead sharks, reef sharks, dense schools of giant trevally and rare nudibranchs make every dive here a different experience. Check with operators on the day's conditions.

→

In the evening, a night dive in Wakatobi's shallow corals to witness the mating ritual of the mandarinfish (Synchiropus splendidus) — a ceremony that takes place every evening at sunset on specific sites. The mandarinfish is among the most colourful in the world — electric blue, green and orange — and only emerges in the darkness. Operators who know these specific sites will guide your night outing.

If you have time before your return flight, Binongko — the fourth and most remote island of the archipelago — is the least touristic of all four. It's nicknamed "The Island of Blacksmiths" because its artisans have been practising traditional knife and tool forging for centuries. A visit to a local smithy lets you watch techniques passed down through generations. Binongko also has hard-to-reach white-sand beaches and pristine reefs.

Final dive or snorkelling session from Wangi-Wangi's fringing reef. This so-called "House Reef", accessible by wading from shore, drops off into a vertical wall just 50 m from the coast. Green turtles, stingrays, leopard groupers and nudibranchs of every kind congregate here in a single dive. The sunset over the Banda Sea from your accommodation's terrace wraps up the journey.
