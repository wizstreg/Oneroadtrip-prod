Sixteen stops between Vauban citadels and lavender villages: Entrevaux with its ramparts suspended above the Var, Castellane gateway to the Verdon, the lavandin fields of the Albion plateau seen from Simiane-la-Rotonde, and Moustiers-Sainte-Marie with its star suspended between two cliffs. Between high alpine valley and secret Provence, this circuit crosses one of France's most authentic departments.

Key highlights (4)
  • Entrevaux: medieval fortified town by Vauban in 1693, classified as one of France's Most Beautiful Villages in 2023
  • Castellane: gateway to the Verdon, dominated by its 184-meter limestone rock
  • Moustiers-Sainte-Marie: faience capital since the 17th century, star suspended between two cliffs
  • Haute-Provence Observatory: discovery site of the first exoplanet in 1995 (2019 Nobel Prize)

Entrevaux

+2

9 La Maiouneta

Cross the drawbridge over the Var — two towers flank the Royal Gate built in 1658 — and you'll enter one of the few intact medieval fortified towns in Provence. Entrevaux has been a royal town since 1541 (François I granted it total tax exemption that year) and was classified as one of France's Most Beautiful Villages in 2023. Wander the narrow lanes between tall houses, fountains, and medieval shops: every stone tells three centuries of fortified life.

The Cathedral of Notre-Dame de l'Assomption deserves a visit inside: built between 1604 and 1667, it's integrated into Vauban's fortifications through the Italian Gate. The exterior is austere, but the Provençal baroque interior surprises — carved decorations, gilded altarpieces, and a contemplative atmosphere. Don't leave without seeing the old dungeons right at the town's entrance.

Climb to the citadel — allow 30 minutes for the walk up the cobbled zigzag path that Vauban had built starting in 1693 (before him, soldiers climbed by ladder!). The fortress itself dates to the 11th century, reinforced between 1683 and 1702 on Louis XIV's orders: 150 meters above the village, the 360° panorama over the Var valley and surrounding ridges justifies the effort. During World War I, the citadel served as a prison for German officers — graffiti from that period remain visible on the walls.

→

The ideal arrival is by the Train des Pignes from Nice or Digne-les-Bains: the Chemins de Fer de Provence line (151 km, narrow gauge) is itself a spectacle with its viaducts and tunnels. Entrevaux station, on the right bank of the Var facing the ramparts, gives a striking first view of the fortified town. Take time to linger on the platforms to photograph the fortifications from below.

→

From the opposite bank of the Var, a marked trail leads to the two old 18th-century mills (flour and oil) — one is still operating, which is rare in Provence, and both are open to the public. The path offers the best outside views of the fortified town: plan 45 minutes round-trip.

Annot sits at 700 meters altitude at the foot of a rocky chaos classified as a Sensitive Natural Area since 1921 — sandstone blocks formed 35 to 40 million years ago by underwater avalanches, then fractured during the formation of the Alps. The village itself is medieval: its 11th-century ramparts are still clearly visible, and the houses in local sandstone give the lanes a particularly warm hue. Look for carved lintels, corbelled houses, and vaulted passages — every facade detail tells of a trade or a date.

The Grès trail (starting from the station, 6 km loop, 350 m elevation gain, about 3h) is the highlight of the day. It first crosses the King's Gardens — overhanging formations — then reaches the King's Chamber, a cave in the cliffs where, according to legend, a lord and his princess hid until a traitor betrayed them. The natural arch of Les Portettes marks the descent; the Espaluns viewpoint offers the best plunging view of the village. Hiking boots required — the trail is sometimes slippery.

→

The Grès d'Annot cliffs are considered the reference for traditional climbing in France: no artificial holds, the climber places their own protection in the bare rock. Local operators offer beginner initiations or guided outings on equipped routes. Even if you don't climb, watching climbers from below in this forest of century-old chestnut trees is worth the detour.

→

If you're passing through between May and October, check the schedule of the Steam Train des Pignes: volunteers from GECP (Study Group for Provence Railways) run a steam locomotive on certain weekends. Annot is the terminus for these heritage trains — boarding or simply watching the engine maneuver at the station is a special experience.

Castellane

Top hotels
9.7 Entre Ciel et Verdon
9.1 Camping La Ferme de Castellane
9.2 Maison Castellane Chambre d'Hotes B&B
9.2 Appartement en centre-ville

Castellane is dominated by a 184-meter limestone outcrop — the Roc — which gave the village its name: Petra Castellana, the 'fortified rock'. The old medieval village established itself there in 977 CE after Saracens had ravaged the original settlement of Salinae in the plain. Today, Castellane is the gateway to the Verdon Gorges and a lively small town with its Wednesday and Saturday morning market on Place Marcel Sauvaire. Walk the circuit of ramparts and spot the 14th-century pentagonal tower, remnant of fortifications that resisted the Wars of Religion — notably the 1586 siege repelled thanks to Judith Andrau, still celebrated every January 31st with the Pétardier festival.

The Church of Saint-Victor shouldn't be missed: built between the 12th and 18th centuries and classified as a Historic Monument in 1944, it blends late Romanesque and Gothic styles. Its bell tower dates from 1455. Push open the door — the interior is simple and peaceful, reflecting this town that knows how to resist tourism's excesses.

Climb up to Notre-Dame du Roc — 30 to 45 minutes of walking from the town center via the Way of the Cross (14 19th-century oratories mark the ascent). The chapel dominates at 911 meters altitude; the oldest parts date back to the 12th century, but it was rebuilt several times (in 1703, then 1861). In 2023, restoration of painted decorations discovered on this occasion revealed remarkable murals. From the terrace, you have two orientation tables and a view over the Verdon and the pre-gorges. Nearby, the ruins of Petra Castellana (explanatory panels in 3 languages) merit 30 additional minutes.

→

Castellane is the capital of Verdon whitewater sports: operators offer rafting, canoeing and canyoning on the Verdon, from beginner to advanced levels. If you have energy after climbing to the Roc, book an afternoon outing — the first gorges downstream from Castellane are accessible without prior experience.

Colmars Les Alpes

+5
Top hotels

9.6 Les Terrasses de Labrau
9 Appartement Hauteur Barcelonnette Vue Exceptionnelle

Colmars-les-Alpes sits at 1,250 meters altitude in the Upper Verdon valley, on the border between alpine and Provençal influences: medieval ramparts, flowered fountains, sundials on facades, and tall houses with colorful shutters. Founded by Romans under the name Collis Martis, the town received its first fortifications in 1382 when the neighboring village of Allos passed to the Duchy of Savoy. Classified as one of France's Most Beautiful Villages in September 2025, first stroll its lanes and the 500-meter rampart walk that encircles the town.

Fort de Savoie is the landmark monument: built between 1693 and 1696 by engineer Creuzet de Richerand (following Vauban's plans, though Vauban never visited the site), it guarded the border with Savoy. Classified as a Historic Monument since 1923, it houses an immersive exhibition with projections and reconstructed scenes about Colmars's history. Guided tours last about 1h30 — offered in July-August. Fort de France, on the downstream side, can't be visited but is clearly visible from the ramparts.

In the rampart walk house-museum, a reconstructed 19th-century bourgeois apartment brings you into the daily life of the fortified town. And if you have 20 minutes, head to the Apothecary's Garden: dedicated to the 17th-century village apothecary, it brings together aromatic and medicinal plants from the Upper Verdon — a peaceful and fragrant garden just steps from the ramparts.

→

The Lance waterfall is reached on foot from the village in 20 minutes (easy marked trail, accessible to families). Its natural pools are suitable for swimming in summer — the stream is cold and the spot remains uncrowded. On the way back, take the Adroit Canal trail (4 km loop, 2h, interpretive signs about fortified heritage) for a balcony view of the ramparts and Mercantour peaks.

→

Colmars stands at the gates of Mercantour National Park: in summer, half-day hikes lead to alpine pastures and high-altitude lakes. Operators also offer canyoning outings in the nearby gorges — a fine option if the weather is warm.

+3

9.6 Superbe Chalet Cabane Chabanon

Seyne-les-Alpes reveals itself from the Col de Maure pass: the Vauban citadel on its rocky spur of La Robine suddenly appears, crowning the village. It's a former Gallic oppidum that became a frontier town with Savoy under Louis XIV. The Grande Tour from the 12th century (Seyne was then called 'Seyne la Grande Tour') stands at the very heart of the fortress — restored and classified as a Historic Monument, it offers from its terrace a unique panorama over the Blanche mountain chain and the valley. Allow 1h30 for the complete visit, including the stalactite cistern, cannons, and old bread oven.

The citadel proper was built in 1693 by Niquet and Vauban — its narrow, elongated 'shark' shape follows the spur's relief. In addition to the permanent exhibition on military history, you'll find a contemporary art installation in free access: The donjon of the sleeping bear, by American artist Mark Dion, classified Viapac. An escape game in the fort's underground passages (180 m²) is offered depending on the season.

Descend into the old village for the pedestrian crossing from the Porte de Provence to the Place d'Armes: washhouses, half-timbered houses, flowered squares, and small museums of the forge, the bugade (traditional laundry), and the tailor mark the route. The Church of Notre-Dame-de-Nazareth, crowning its historic gardens, and the Penitents Chapel with its rare three-faced bell tower warrant a stop.

→

A few kilometers from the village, the Nordic area of Ratery (1,700 m altitude) offers hiking and, in winter, cross-country skiing and snowshoeing. In summer, trails around Seyne in the Haute-Provence Geopark offer varied routes between larch forests and ridges. If you have time, the hamlet of Chardavon houses the Maison du Mulet — a small museum on the mule-breeding tradition of the Pays de Seyne, surprising and endearing.

Cruis is a discreet village perched on the south face of Montagne de Lure — the 'little sister of Mont Ventoux' dear to Jean Giono. Its large Church of Notre-Dame-et-Saint-Martin is actually the former church of an abbey founded around the year 1000 by Augustinian canons. Around it, vestiges of the cloister and traces of the abbey recall that the village owes its development to this institution, which was very wealthy until the Hundred Years' War and the Great Plague ruined it in the 15th century. Wander the stone lanes, calm and cool in summer — the tall houses with carefully crafted lintels betray the past prosperity of peddler-druggist families.

A few kilometers north of Cruis, the Abbey of Notre-Dame de Lure (12th century) nestles at the bottom of the Morteiron valley, beneath century-old walnut and linden trees. It was one of the required stops for peddler-druggists who traveled the mountain to gather medicinal plants — lavender, wormwood, thyme — and resell them as far as the Beaucaire fair. This trade was so developed here that the Pays de Forcalquier counted 45 peddler-druggists in 1779 (versus 3 in 1707) — their descendants became pharmacists in the 19th century.

→

Three marked routes 'On the path of plants of Montagne de Lure' allow discovery of aromatic and medicinal plants in their natural environment (Graves site, Lure ridges). The discovery guide is available at the Forcalquier tourist office. If you're there between June and August, the Ronde des Jas (10 km, 4h30, starting from Cruis) reaches the alpine pastures and high-altitude shepherds' huts with exceptional views of the Alps.

→

A tasting of essential oils and Provençal herbs at local producers in the Pays de Forcalquier is a good introduction to the region's flavors — some estates near Cruis offer visits to lavender distilleries in season.

Lurs

+2
Top hotels
9.6 Le Mas des Meras - Gite Côté mas
9.3 Domaine Ribiera, Hotel 5 Etoiles, SPA & Golf - Forcalquier
9 Studio au calme les hirondelles
9.7 La Magnanerie en Provence

Lurs perches at 612 meters on a rocky spur above the Durance. Consecrated as Episcopal Land by Charlemagne in 812, the town was the summer residence of the bishops of Sisteron until the Revolution — this past is visible everywhere: castle of the prince-bishops, Clock Tower (whose bell dates from 1599, the oldest in the department), seminary built between 1738 and 1742. Enter the village via the Path of Scripts, inaugurated in 2010: 42 steles trace the evolution of Latin writing and typography, an idea born right here, as Jean Giono and typographer Maximilien Vox founded the International Encounters of Lure in 1952 — they still take place every last week of August.

Don't leave without taking the Bishops' Promenade: from the castle, an avenue lined with 15 oratories dedicated to the Virgin (built in 1864) leads to the Chapel of Notre-Dame de Vie (1552, with columned entrance). From the terrace, the view is 360° over the Durance valley, Valensole plateau, Montagne de Lure, and the Luberon — in clear weather, the Alps chain on the horizon. It's one of the finest views in Haute-Provence.

The Lurs olive grove is one of Provence's five most beautiful — often multi-century-old olive trees cascade down toward the Durance. The local Aglandau variety produces PDO oil. Descend for a walk among the trees via the calade path.

→

The Roman Bridge loop (3h15, 270 m elevation gain) crosses the village, the Bishops' Promenade, a forest, and reaches a bridge built between 121 and 145 CE on the Via Domitia — the Roman road connecting Italy to Spain passed at the foot of Lurs. On a corner block in large stonework, a phallus deeply carved into the stone has intrigued archaeologists for a long time. The climb returns via a trail bordered by dry-stone walls and olive groves.

Mane

Top hotels
9.5 Le Couvent des Minimes, un Hôtel & Spa L'Occitane en Provence
9.2 Le Mas Du Pont Roman
9.4 Le Sens des Merveilles
9.9 Ferme La Clavière, Maison de vacances Lou Mas

Mane is a village of blonde stone perched on a safre hill (local ochre rock). Its name may come from Mana Genita, Roman goddess of funerals — the site has been inhabited since prehistory and the Via Domitia passed below. The 12th-century citadel, built on its peak, is the only feudal fortification remaining intact in Haute-Provence. Wander the cobbled lanes and observe the pointed cabanons (bories) scattered across the territory: dry-stone constructions without mortar, built between 1850 and 1950, witnesses to a vanished peasant know-how.

The Salagon Priory is the key visit of the stop. Classified as a Historic Monument, this vast 50 x 30 m complex groups a 12th-century Romanesque church (barrel vaults, carved capitals, rare 15th-century mural fresco), a 15th-century priory lodging, and 6 hectares of ethnobotanical gardens. The interior surprise: a series of six contemporary stained-glass windows by Aurélie Nemours (installed in 1998) bathe the nave in intense red — a striking contrast with Romanesque austerity. The gardens present more than 1,700 plants, including a medieval garden and a poisonous plant garden. The museum traces 2,000 years of site occupation, from the Neolithic to monastic life.

Less than a kilometer from Salagon, the Château de Sauvan (18th century) is a replica of Versailles's Petit Trianon — an architectural curiosity amid Provençal scrubland. Period furniture and classical gardens, guided tours in season.

→

Walk the route of calades and fountains through the town, then reach the medieval Roman bridge over the Laye that once led pilgrims to Salagon Priory with dry feet. The round-trip walk from the village takes about 45 minutes and offers lovely views of Mane's peak.

Saint Michel L Observatoire

Top hotels
9 Charmant petit havre de paix

9.3 Studio climatisé au centre du village
9.2 Le Mas Du Pont Roman

Saint-Michel-l'Observatoire is a Provençal village of pale houses — it owes its name and worldwide fame to the Haute-Provence Observatory (OHP), founded in 1937 by CNRS after measurements taken as early as 1925 revealed 260 to 280 nights per year of excellent sky above the plateau. The OHP's coup: in 1995, astronomers Michel Mayor and Didier Queloz discovered the first confirmed exoplanet (51 Pegasi b) using the T193 telescope (1.93 m mirror) and its Élodie spectrograph — a discovery rewarded with the 2019 Nobel Prize in Physics. Today 13 domes are scattered through the oak forest. Guided tours of the main dome are organized regularly; tickets are sold online or at the Astronomy Center, not on site.

The village itself merits a tour: the Church of Saint-Michel dates from the 12th century, ramparts and a Romanesque tower (Tour de Porchères) recall its medieval past. Seven educational displays mark the 'Path of Stars' on the hill overlooking the town — a discovery trail about astronomy accessible to all and permanently open.

→

The Astronomy Center (at the Moulin plateau, a few minutes from the village) hosts year-round activities for the public: telescope observation evenings, planetarium sessions (64 seats), solar observation. In summer, the Été Astro festival brings together more than 10,000 visitors. Book your seats in advance — observation evenings fill quickly in July-August.

→

The Observatory walk (6 km loop, 2h, easy) follows the oak forest around the scientific site and offers beautiful views of Montagne de Lure and the Valensole plateau. A country stroll between terraces, ruined mills, and country chapels.

Simiane-la-Rotonde perches at 650 meters on an advanced promontory of the Albion plateau, at the center of a Ventoux-Lure-Luberon triangle. The village owes its name to its exceptional keep: La Rotonde, a dodecagonal tower (12 sides) built at the end of the 12th century by the Simiane-Agoult family. Its interior is the real surprise — on the first floor, a 5-meter-high ribbed dome whose capitals adorned with strange and grotesque human masks fascinate historians. Some compare it to the Holy Sepulcher of Jerusalem brought back by crusaders. Classified as a Historic Monument as early as 1841, it's visited from February to November. In August, an international early music festival (Les Riches Heures Musicales de la Rotonde) holds its concerts there since 1982 — the acoustics are remarkable.

Then stroll the lanes to reach the 16th-century covered market: beneath its columns, a panoramic view of the lavender fields below opens like a painting. These halls were immortalized by Henri Cartier-Bresson in 1971. Nearby, the birthplace of writer Ponson du Terrail (1829-1871), author of the Rocambole saga, whose title gave rise to the French adjective 'rocambolesque' (far-fetched).

5 km from the village, Valsaintes Abbey houses a rose garden of 600 varieties and permaculture gardens around Cistercian ruins. The ideal period to visit is rose blooming, late May - early June.

→

Simiane houses the SCA3P cooperative (founded in 1979), the largest lavender and lavandin cooperative in France: 330 members, 5,000 hectares of fine lavender and 15,000 of lavandin, representing 90% of world lavandin production. If you're there between mid-June and mid-July, the flowering Albion plateau is a photographic spectacle not to be missed. A visit to the cooperative or a local farm completes the day nicely.

Dauphin sits on its hilltop between the Luberon and the Valensole plateau, 9 km from Forcalquier. The village established itself there in the Middle Ages for protection from marauders, leaving below the vestiges of Gallo-Roman villas and Via Domitia mosaics that crossed the commune. Pass under the Pourcheirieu gate — sole remnant of the fortified walls — and climb to the old castle viewpoint: to the north, the Observatory domes of Saint-Michel, the village of Mane, and the Forcalquier citadel; to the south, the wooded Luberon. The view is one of the most complete over the Pays de Forcalquier.

The Church of Saint-Martin, a former castle chapel that became parochial in 1524, houses an exceptional baroque organ: restored and enriched with multiple stops by organ builder Michel Deluz, it's renowned for its acoustics and attracts organists each summer for concerts (Bach, Buxtehude, Handel, Frescobaldi). Concerts take place from July to September. If you're not there for a concert, the nave is worth visiting for its 19th-century canvas paintings by Esprit Michel Gibelin and its mullioned windows.

In December, the church displays one of the oldest nativity scenes in the Pays de Forcalquier: 11 dressed santons from the 18th century in their original state, whose clothing allows dating the crèche to the July Monarchy period.

→

The village has active craftspeople — potters, ceramicists, art blacksmiths, wood turners. A late-morning stroll through the cobbled lanes often allows entering their workshops. The Iron and Fire festival (last weekend of March) is the main event celebrating these crafts.

→

The Via Domitia runs along the commune: from the village, it's easy to join the GR 653 (Way of St. James) that follows the old Roman route. A 2-hour walk through olive groves and oak forests, heading north toward the Forcalquier plateaus, offers beautiful panoramas.

Montfuron (200 inhabitants) is one of the most secret villages of the Luberon. Its name comes from Latin Monsfurnorum — 'mountain of ovens' — referring to the gypsum that was cooked and ground there to make plaster. The village possesses one of only two still-functioning windmills in Alpes-de-Haute-Provence: the Saint-Elzéar mill, built in 1640 under Louis XIV. Restored with support from Luberon Regional Natural Park (1980, then 2000), it preserves part of its original wooden mechanism and its 13-meter-long sails. It still produces flour during the mill festival every first weekend of August. Guided tours take place Saturday afternoons in season.

Climb to the Castelas viewpoint (650 m), on the ruins of Jean Garnier's Renaissance castle, sacked during the Wars of Religion. You'll find the stone compass rose, with Provence's 32 winds engraved by direction (Tramontane, Mistral, Sirocco…). The orientation table names the mountains visible 360°: Sainte-Victoire, Mont Ventoux, the Luberon, Montagne de Lure, and the Alps as far as Italy in clear weather.

→

The Windmill loop (starting from village, 2h30) crosses the forest, passes the gypsum mill ruins, climbs along ridges with panoramic views, and reaches the windmill. The trail is family-friendly and not technical. Right next to the mill, the Butterfly Garden (7,000 m² of shrubby scrubland, 3,000 planted vegetation) shelters 60 species of diurnal butterflies — a peaceful spot to see depending on season.

Saint Martin De Bromes

Top hotels
9.6 L'Establoun
9.2 Campaneta
9.5 Chez Gilles Math
9.5 Sous Les Chênes

Saint-Martin-de-Brômes is a village of endearing simplicity: Jean Giono nicknamed it 'the curled-up fox'. It's built in an amphitheater on the hillside of Castelleras, at the confluence of the Colostre and Pontet rivers. The village emblem is the Clock Tower — former keep built by Boniface de Castellane in the 14th century, 24 meters high with machicolations and arrow slits. Classified as a Historic Monument since 1921, it has housed since 1972 one of France's smallest museums (10 m²): on the ground floor, a 4th-century Gallo-Roman sarcophagus with a 300 kg lead lid and intact skeleton, discovered in 1972 during road works, and a milestone of Emperor Carus (282-283 CE) that marked the Roman road between Aix-en-Provence and Riez.

The Romanesque Church of Saint-Martin (12th-14th centuries), classified as a Historic Monument, is known for its 19th-century murals by Esprit Michel Gibelin — student of Cézanne's workshop — depicting the lives of Saint Martin and the Virgin in the nave's blind arcades. Also look for the statuette of Saint Anthony the Hermit in gilded and polychrome wood, patron of truffle hunters, the oldest in the church.

→

7 km away, Lake Esparron-de-Verdon opens onto turquoise water — an artificial reservoir created in 1967, but set in a landscape of limestone gorges that gives it a wild appearance. Operators offer pedal boats, canoes, and electric boats for rent. It's one of the best swimming spots in the region, ideal for an afternoon.

→

The GR4 crosses the commune and offers beautiful ridge trails above the Colostre — views of the Valensole plateau on one side and the emerging Verdon gorges on the other. A 2-hour loop suffices for the most interesting viewpoints.

At the entrance to Riez, in a field on the left of the road, you'll come upon four monolithic columns in gray granite from Estérel, 5.90 meters high, topped with white marble Corinthian capitals. This is all that remains of a tetrastyle temple dedicated to Apollo, erected in the 1st century CE — classified on the first Historic Monuments list of 1840. Because Riez is no ordinary village: under the name Colonia Julia Augusta Reiorum Apollinaris, it was one of the 13 Latin law cities of Narbonensis. At its peak in the 2nd century, the town spread over 20 hectares, with two thermal baths, a theater, and several necropolises.

The early Christian baptistery (5th century) is one of the rare buildings of this type preserved in Provence. Behind its austere square facade hides an octagonal room with eight granite columns, Corinthian marble capitals, and an immersion basin — baptismal, used one night per year during the Easter ceremony. Comparable to the Fréjus baptistery, it has been classified as a Historic Monument since 1840.

In the old town, enter through the Saint-Sols gate to reach the Grande-Rue and its Renaissance facades. Look for the Hôtel de Mazan (1523): its staircase with four twisted columns is adorned with gypseries — these molded and sculpted plaster decorations that make Riez's specialty. There was no ashlar in the vicinity, so Riez residents covered everything with sculpted plaster, on facades, chimneys, ceilings, and staircases. Then climb to the Chapel of Saint-Maxime (17th century, on the hill) for a panoramic view over three valleys.

→

The Wednesday and Saturday morning market is one of the largest in the region — held for centuries under plane trees on the central square. In winter (December-February), a truffle market takes place Wednesday morning. The transhumance festival (third or fourth Sunday in June) is a major event: sheep cross the village heading up to alpine pastures.

→

Riez sits at the heart of Verdon Regional Natural Park: Lake Sainte-Croix (2,200 hectares, the largest of the Verdon lakes) is 14 km away. If the weather is favorable, an afternoon on or beside the water is a fine option before heading to Moustiers the next day.

Moustiers-Sainte-Marie is built between two limestone cliffs — the first view from the road resembles a life-sized nativity scene. A five-pointed golden star (1.25 m diameter, suspended from a 135-meter chain between two rocky peaks) watches over the village since the Middle Ages. Legend, transmitted by Frédéric Mistral, attributes this chain to a knight Blacas, prisoner of Saracens during the Crusades, who promised this ex-voto if he returned alive. Classified as one of France's Most Beautiful Villages since 1981, the town is divided by the Adou torrent. Don't miss the Church of Notre-Dame de l'Assomption: 12th-century Lombard bell tower in tuff, and inside an altar carved from a 5th-century sarcophagus. Louis XIV himself was a fan of Moustiers faience — when he decided to melt down the court's golden tableware to refill coffers, he ordered Moustiers ceramics instead.

The faience museum (inaugurated in 1929) traces nine centuries of ceramic production through more than 300 pieces. The 17th-18th century golden age, with its hunting, grotesque, and bird decorations that made the workshops' worldwide reputation, is very well represented. Allow an hour.

Climb up to Notre-Dame-de-Beauvoir by climbing the 262 steps of the Way of the Cross (allow 20-25 minutes ascent). The chapel dates from the 12th and 16th centuries — the 14 Stations of the Cross are adorned with faience tiles made by Simone Garnier. From the terrace, the view over the village rooftops and Lake Sainte-Croix below is striking. Shoes with grip required — the steps are slippery.

→

5 km from the village, Lake Sainte-Croix (2,200 hectares, 10 km long) is fed by the Verdon. The entrances to the Verdon gorges on the Moustiers side are accessible by kayak or canoe — operators offer rentals from the shore. In July-August, book early morning: lake access is very busy in high season.

→

A free stroll through the village's faience workshops and shops — about fifteen remain active — allows seeing the faience-makers' gestures and understanding differences between styles (17th-century blue decorations, 18th-century polychromy, contemporary production).

Digne-les-Bains, prefecture of the 04 department, is the terminus of the Train des Pignes that has accompanied this entire circuit from Nice. It's also the birthplace of the very first UNESCO-labeled Global Geopark, created in 2000 — 300 million years of geological history beneath your feet. Start with the Ammonite Slab: an emerged ancient seabed fossil site, at the town's exit toward Barles, on which 1,550 fossils of ammonites, nautiluses, and crinoids are visible over 320 m². The site is freely accessible on foot from the road.

The Saint-Benoît Museum-Promenade is the Geopark House — a geology museum nestled in a park with Japanese gardens, butterfly garden, and contemporary artworks by Andy Goldsworthy, Herman de Vries, and other international artists. The museum traces 300 million years of Earth's history through an impressive fossil collection. Allow 2 hours for museum and gardens.

Digne is also the city of Alexandra David-Néel (1868-1969) — explorer, orientalist, and first Westerner to enter the forbidden city of Lhasa (Tibet) in 1924. Her foundation houses her collections, oriental library, and travel mementos. A fascinating portrait of an extraordinary woman.

→

The thermal baths of Digne (springs naturally flowing between 22 and 42°C since Roman Antiquity) were voted France's best thermal spa in 2024 by Le Figaro. The Spa de Haute-Provence offers a half-day accessible to the public with thermal water pool, sauna, and hammam — an ideal way to conclude this long circuit through the Alpes-de-Haute-Provence.
