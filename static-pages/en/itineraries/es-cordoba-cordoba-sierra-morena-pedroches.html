Córdoba and the Sierra Morena: From the Caliphate to the Dehesas of Los Pedroches

From the Mezquita to the dehesas: UNESCO Córdoba, Medina Azahara, Almodóvar castle, Iberian ham of Los Pedroches, Belalcázar, and red Montoro.

This 7-day itinerary explores the two faces of Córdoba province: the caliphal city with four UNESCO inscriptions (Mezquita, historic center, Medina Azahara, Festival of the Patios), then the little-known territories to the north—the spectacular castle of Almodóvar del Río, the lynx forests of the Sierra Morena, the granite villages of Los Pedroches with their jamón ibérico de bellota, and Montoro, the 'red village' set in a meander of the Guadalquivir.

Key highlights (5)
  • Córdoba: the Mezquita with 850 columns (UNESCO 1984) and Medina Azahara, 10th-century caliphal city (UNESCO 2018)
  • Almodóvar del Río: 8th-century Arab castle overlooking the Guadalquivir, Game of Thrones filming location
  • Belalcázar: 47-meter Keep, the tallest in the Iberian Peninsula
  • Valle de los Pedroches: the largest continuous dehesa in Europe, birthplace of DOP jamón ibérico de bellota
  • Montoro: red sandstone village in a Guadalquivir meander, Historic Ensemble since 1969

The Mezquita-Cathedral of Córdoba is, without exaggeration, one of the most extraordinary monuments on the planet. Begun in 785 by Abd al-Rahman I on the site of a Visigothic basilica, enlarged three times over two centuries, then transformed into a cathedral after the Reconquest of 1236, it layers civilizations within a single 23,400 m² space. Upon entering, you're seized by the forest of more than 850 columns of marble, jasper, and granite—some salvaged from Roman and Visigothic ruins—connected by red-and-white bicolor arches that unfold as far as the eye can see. Push on to Al-Hakam II's mihrab: its golden and blue mosaics, created by artisans sent by the Byzantine emperor, rank among the finest in Islamic art. Then discover the Renaissance cathedral planted in the middle of the mosque—Charles V, who had authorized it, reportedly regretted upon visiting the works: 'You have destroyed what was unique to build what can be seen everywhere.' Arrive at 8:30 AM for free entry (until 9:30 AM)—after that, the queue grows quickly.

Leaving the Mezquita, lose yourself in the Judería, the medieval Jewish quarter that's part of Córdoba's UNESCO listing. Its whitewashed lanes so narrow you can touch both walls with outstretched arms, its patios glimpsed through half-open doors, its pots of geraniums clinging to facades—this is the image of Córdoba you'll take home. Don't miss the Calleja de las Flores, a tiny dead-end alley that opens onto a perfect perspective of the Mezquita's bell tower framed by flowers. The synagogue (14th century), one of only three preserved in Spain along with those in Toledo, can be visited in 15 minutes—it's small, but the Mudéjar stuccowork is remarkable.

The Alcázar de los Reyes Cristianos, built in 1328 by Alfonso XI on the ruins of a Moorish palace, is especially famous for its terraced gardens—rectangular pools bordered by clipped hedges, cypresses, and orange trees that descend toward the Guadalquivir. This is where Ferdinand and Isabella planned the conquest of Granada and where Christopher Columbus presented his project for a voyage to the Indies in 1486. The interior is sober—vaulted halls, Roman mosaics, and Arab baths—but the gardens at sunset are worth the visit.

Cross the Puente Romano, Córdoba's Roman bridge—331 meters, 16 arches, built in the 1st century BC under Augustus, rebuilt several times since. In the middle, pause before the statue of the Archangel Raphael, patron of the city. On the other side, the Torre de la Calahorra (12th century), a former Arab fortified gate, houses a small museum on the Córdoba of three cultures (Muslim, Jewish, Christian). But above all, turn around: the view of the Mezquita and the historic center from the bridge, especially in late afternoon, is the finest in Córdoba.

→

In the evening, settle into the taverns of the San Basilio neighborhood or the Plaza de la Corredera for a tapas crawl. Córdoba is a city of generous tapas: salmorejo (cold tomato soup thicker than gazpacho, served with ham and hard-boiled egg), flamenquines (breaded ham and pork roll), berenjenas con miel (fried aubergines with honey), and a glass of Montilla-Moriles, the local fortified wine that resembles sherry but with its own character. The Plaza de la Corredera, the only enclosed square plaza in Andalusia (17th century), is perfect for starting.

💡 The bars around Plaza de la Corredera and Plaza del Potro are the most authentic—avoid the tourist restaurants in the Judería

Devote the morning to Medina Azahara (Madinat al-Zahra), the caliphal city that's been a UNESCO site since 2018. Eight kilometers west of Córdoba, at the foot of the Sierra Morena, Abd al-Rahman III ordered in 936 the construction of an entire palatine city to serve as the seat of the Caliphate of Córdoba. Over 75 years, 10,000 workers built a city of 112 hectares on three terraces—royal palaces at the top, gardens and administration in the middle, mosque and dwellings at the bottom. Then, in 1010, the civil war that ended the caliphate reduced it to ashes. It remained buried for a millennium, until its rediscovery in 1910. Today, only 10% has been excavated, but the Salón Rico (official reception hall), with its arches and carved stone panels of insane delicacy, conveys the measure of what this 'Andalusian Versailles' once was. The museum at the base of the site is excellent—start there to understand what you're about to see. Allow at least 2.5 hours.

Back in the city in the afternoon, visit the Palacio de Viana, nicknamed the 'Museum of Patios.' This aristocratic palace built between the 14th and 18th centuries has 12 patios and a garden—each with its own style, fountains, columns, and vegetation. It's the best way to understand Córdoba's patio culture, inscribed on UNESCO's intangible heritage list since 2012 (Festival of the Patios, in May). If you're there in May, the private patios of the San Basilio and Alcázar Viejo neighborhoods open to the public—it's a spectacle of flowers, colors, and popular pride.

Push on to the Roman Temple on Calle Claudio Marcelo—the eleven Corinthian columns of this 1st-century temple, dedicated to imperial cult, recall that Córdoba was the capital of Roman Baetica and the birthplace of Seneca and Lucan. Just beside it, the remains of the Roman theatre (the largest in Spain in its time) are visible in the cellars of the Archaeological Museum.

→

If you're in Córdoba between May and June, don't miss the flamenco shows in the peñas flamencas of the Santiago and San Lorenzo neighborhoods—more intimate and authentic venues than the tourist tablaos. Córdoba's flamenco guitar tradition is distinct, inherited from Paco Peña and Vicente Amigo.

💡 The peñas flamencas are often open on weekends—check with the tourist office for dates

Twenty minutes west of Córdoba, the castle of Almodóvar del Río appears from afar, perched on a 252-meter hill above the Guadalquivir. This fortress of Umayyad origin, built around 760, is one of the best-preserved in Spain. Its crenellated towers—the Cuadrada, the Redonda, and the Keep—stand out against the sky like an illustration from a medieval book. The interior can be visited: courtyards of arms, furnished halls, armor, dungeons, and oubliettes where Peter I the Cruel supposedly imprisoned his enemies in the 14th century. The building was restored between 1901 and 1936 by the Count of Torralva, which explains its remarkable condition. If the place seems familiar, that's normal—scenes from Game of Thrones (Season 7, Highgarden and Casterly Rock) were filmed here. Allow 1.5 hours for the visit.

In the afternoon, push toward the Sierra de Hornachuelos Natural Park, 30 km northwest. It's one of the least-visited natural areas in Andalusia—and one of the wildest. The Mediterranean forest of holm oaks and cork oaks shelters one of the last populations of Iberian lynx, black vultures, Spanish imperial eagles, and deer. The village of Hornachuelos, listed among Spain's Most Beautiful Villages, overlooks a spectacular gorge of the Río Bembézar. From the village's mirador, the view over the gorges and the Sierra Morena is breathtaking.

The Monastery of San Jerónimo de Valparaíso, on the edge of the park, deserves a stop if timing permits. Founded in the 15th century, this is the monastery where Gonzalo Fernández de Córdoba—the Gran Capitán, the most famous Spanish general of the Renaissance—spent his final years. The cloister and church preserve Gothic and Mudéjar elements.

→

Operators in the Hornachuelos park offer guided hikes with wildlife observation—the Fuente del Valle trail (8 km, 3 hours) crosses a dense cork oak forest with good chances of seeing deer and raptors. In season (autumn-winter), the deer rut is spectacular in these forests. Kayak excursions on the Bembézar reservoir are also available.

💡 The park is vast and wild—bring water and provisions. Not all trails are marked; a guide is recommended

The route northward crosses the first dehesa landscapes—those pastures dotted with centuries-old holm oaks that are the birthplace of the Iberian pig. You're entering the Valle de los Pedroches, the largest continuous dehesa in Europe, extending over 3,600 km² in northern Córdoba province. First stop: Belalcázar, whose Sotomayor y Zúñiga castle can be seen from kilometers away. Built in the 15th century, it has the tallest Torre del Homenaje (Keep) in the entire Iberian Peninsula—47 meters of granite flanked by seven massive towers. The castle's interior unfortunately cannot be visited (under restoration), but its silhouette seen from below is one of the most impressive in Spain. Walk around via the path that circles the hill for the best perspectives.

The Convento de Santa Clara de la Columna, in the village of Belalcázar, is the second most important religious monumental complex in Córdoba province after the Mezquita. Founded in the 15th century, it's a cloistered convent still inhabited by nuns. Guided tours (often dramatized) allow you to discover the cloister, the artesonados (coffered wooden ceilings in Mudéjar style), and the chapel. The nuns sell artisanal pastries through a rotating hatch—a typically Spanish tradition.

Continue 9 km east to Hinojosa del Duque. Its church of San Juan Bautista is nicknamed 'the Cathedral of the Sierra'—and rightly so. Built in Pedroches granite between the 15th and 16th centuries, its 56-meter tower is the work of Hernán Ruiz II, the same architect who transformed the Mezquita's minaret into a bell tower in Córdoba and remodeled the Giralda's campanile in Seville. The interior houses a remarkable baptistery and Baroque altarpieces. The ethnological museum, in the village center, traces rural life in Los Pedroches with agricultural tools, antique toys, and even an old cinema projector.

→

The gastronomy of Los Pedroches revolves around the Iberian pig. The jamón ibérico de bellota DOP Los Pedroches is one of the most renowned in Spain—the black pigs graze freely in the dehesa and feed on holm oak acorns during the montanera (October to February). Regional operators offer visits to ham-curing facilities with tastings and explanations of the aging process. It's a first-rate gastronomic experience. But Los Pedroches isn't just about ham: also try the goat cheeses, rejones (local pastries), and salmorejo serrano.

💡 The montanera (Oct-Feb) is the best period to see the pigs under the oaks. Book curing facility visits in advance

Pedroche is the village that gave its name to the entire comarca. Perched on a hill, it's the former capital of the Siete Villas de los Pedroches—the seven villages that governed themselves in the Middle Ages. The landmark monument is the Torre de Pedroche, a 56-meter-high bell tower that dominates the entire landscape. Begun in the 15th century, it was completed by Hernán Ruiz II—the same architect as at Hinojosa, the Mezquita, and the Giralda. Four superimposed sections (square, octagonal, square, cylindrical) rise above the church of El Salvador (Gothic-Mudéjar). Climb to the top if access is open—the view embraces the entire dehesa as far as the eye can see. Around the Plaza de las Siete Villas, the narrow lanes with granite houses preserve the atmosphere of a medieval Sierra Morena market town.

Drive 15 minutes east to Dos Torres, whose historic center is classified as a Property of Cultural Interest. This village was born from the merger of two medieval settlements—Torrefranca and Torremilano—in 1839. The Pedroches granite architecture is best preserved here: manor houses with dark stone façades highlighted with white lime, carved coats of arms, wrought-iron balconies. The Centro de Interpretación de la Arquitectura Popular explains local construction techniques. Don't miss the Plaza de Toros integrated into the urban fabric and the Pozo de la Nieve (snow well), where ice was stored in winter.

Push 10 minutes south to Añora, the smallest village in Los Pedroches. Its dark granite houses striped with white bands create a unique architectural style in the comarca. The church of San Sebastián (16th century) is considered one of the purest examples of Mudéjar art in Los Pedroches. The village is tiny—30 minutes suffices to tour it—but the end-of-the-world atmosphere is total.

→

The Valle de los Pedroches is classified as a Starlight Reserve by UNESCO for its night sky quality—very little light pollution in this rural area. If you stay a night in Pedroche or Pozoblanco, take advantage of it to observe the stars. Operators offer astronomical observation sessions with telescopes, especially in summer.

💡 The best nights for observation are moonless nights in summer. Pozoblanco is the largest town in Los Pedroches—you'll find the most services there

En route southeast, cross the Sierra de Cardeña y Montoro Natural Park—the highest in Córdoba province. The Mediterranean forest of holm oaks and cork oaks is dense, and the wildlife rich: Iberian lynx (reintroduced), wolf (last Andalusian populations), otter, Spanish imperial eagle. The Río Yeguas trail crosses spectacular granite rock landscapes. Allow 2 to 3 hours for a hike in the park before descending toward Montoro.

Montoro is a surprise. This village of 10,000 inhabitants, classified as a Historic-Artistic Ensemble since 1969, sits on a promontory encircled by a spectacular meander of the Guadalquivir—almost a peninsula. What strikes you first is the color: the monuments are built in piedra molinaza, a local red sandstone that gives the church, ducal palace, and manor façades terracotta hues. The church of San Bartolomé (15th century), in the center on Plaza de España, has a red sandstone bell tower nicknamed 'the Red Giralda' for its resemblance to the Sevillian campanile. Opposite, the Ducal Palace of the Casa de Alba (16th century), now the town hall, presents Mudéjar elements in piedra molinaza.

Descend toward the Puente de las Donadas, built in 1498 by Enrique Egas—one of the finest bridges in the province. Its name ('bridge of the women who gave') comes from the fact that the women of Montoro sold their jewelry to finance its construction, and the king granted them in exchange the privilege of no longer having to lodge or feed troops. From the bridge, the view of the Guadalquivir's meander encircling Montoro is the village's emblematic image. Before leaving Montoro, seek out the Casa de las Conchas—a house whose façade and interior patios are entirely covered with seashells, 45 million in total, accumulated over 50 years by a passionate local.

→

Montoro is at the heart of Córdoba's olive-growing zone—the surrounding olive groves produce DOP Montoro-Adamuz olive oil. Cooperatives offer visits and tastings. Local gastronomy reflects the terroir: ajo sopeao (tomato, pepper, and garlic stew), tortilla de faisanes (bean omelet), perdices en salsa (partridge in sauce), and tortas de manteca for dessert.

💡 Calle Corredera is the main street—shops, bars, and local crafts

Return to Córdoba for the final day, devoted to the less touristy neighborhoods. Start with the Plaza del Potro, mentioned by Cervantes in Don Quixote—the inn bordering it is where Sancho Panza supposedly lodged. The square houses the Museo de Bellas Artes (in the former Hospital de la Caridad, 15th century) and the Museo Julio Romero de Torres, dedicated to the most popular Córdoban painter of the early 20th century, famous for his portraits of Andalusian women with intense gazes.

Head up toward the San Lorenzo neighborhood, one of the most authentic in Córdoba. The church of San Lorenzo (13th century), with its Gothic rose window and arcaded portico, is one of the 'Fernandine churches' built after the Reconquest of 1236—Ferdinand III had 14 parishes constructed in the reconquered city. The neighborhood is calm, residential, with flowery patios you'll only glimpse by craning your neck through half-open doors—this is the Córdoba of Córdobans, far from the tourist groups of the Judería.

Finish with the Palacio de la Merced (18th century), an imposing Baroque building that now serves as the seat of the Provincial Council. Its two-level cloister, with pink marble columns and azulejos, is one of the finest Baroque spaces in Córdoba. Entry is free and the place is rarely mentioned in guidebooks.

→

For your final evening, treat yourself to dinner in the Plaza de las Tendillas or San Miguel neighborhood, where the tapas bars are frequented by locals rather than tourists. Order a salmorejo (the Córdoban version is the most authentic in Andalusia), rabo de toro (braised oxtail, Córdoba's emblematic dish), and a glass of Montilla-Moriles amontillado. From Córdoba, you can take the AVE back to Madrid, Seville, or Málaga, or continue by car toward Jaén (our 'Madrid to Cazorla' itinerary) or toward Granada.
