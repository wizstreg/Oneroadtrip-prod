Melfi, Venosa, Castelmezzano, Craco, Aliano, Matera, Maratea, and Pollino: 9 days off the beaten path through Basilicata.

Basilicata is one of Italy's least known and most fascinating regions. This 9-day itinerary traverses the entire territory, from the Norman-Swabian castles of Vulture to the troglodyte dwellings of Matera, via ghost villages clinging to clay badlands, the vertiginous spires of the Lucanian Dolomites, and the ancient forests of Pollino. This is a rural, wild, and authentic Italy, far from the crowds.

Key highlights (5)
  • Melfi: castle with 10 towers, site of Frederick II's promulgation of the Constitutions in 1231
  • Venosa: birthplace of Horace, Abbey of the Trinity and unfinished 11th-century basilica
  • Castelmezzano: village embedded in the Lucanian Dolomites, Volo dell'Angelo at 120 km/h
  • Craco: ghost village abandoned in 1963, set for The Passion of the Christ
  • Matera: UNESCO-listed troglodyte Sassi, inhabited since the Paleolithic

Your day-by-day itinerary

1
Day

Melfi

Arrive in Melfi, gateway to northern Basilicata, nestled at the foot of Monte Vulture, an extinct volcano. The Norman-Swabian castle dominates the town from atop its volcanic hill. With its 10 towers—7 rectangular and 3 pentagonal—and a moat once crossed by a drawbridge, it's one of the largest castles in southern Italy. Built by the Normans in the 11th century, it was expanded by Frederick II between 1221 and 1225, who constructed the Tower of the Seven Winds and made it his summer residence. Here, the emperor promulgated the Constitutions of Melfi (Liber Augustalis) in 1231, the first organized legal code of the Middle Ages, drafted with the help of Pier delle Vigne—whom Dante would later place in Hell.

Inside the castle, the National Archaeological Museum of Melfese displays finds from the Vulture region. Don't miss the Sarcophagus of Rapolla (2nd century AD), a Roman marble sarcophagus from Asia Minor discovered in 1856—one of the most important in southern Italy. The rooms also showcase Daunian pottery, bronze armor, and gold and amber jewelry dating from the 8th to 4th centuries BC.

Descend into Melfi's medieval borgo, still encircled by its ramparts. Pass through the Porta Venosina, one of the best-preserved medieval gates, and climb the narrow streets lined with noble palaces featuring sculpted stone portals. The cathedral, founded by Robert Guiscard in the 11th century, hosted no fewer than five papal councils between 1059 and 1137—including the 1089 council where Pope Urban II preached the First Crusade.

→

In late afternoon, drive 20 minutes south to reach the Laghi di Monticchio, two volcanic lakes nestled in the ancient craters of Monte Vulture. The water is a deep green, surrounded by dense forests of oaks and beeches. On the shores of Lago Piccolo, the Abbey of San Michele, founded by Benedictine monks in the 8th century, clings to the cliff above the water. Allow an hour for a stroll around the lakes.

💡 Sunset over the lakes is spectacular—arrive around 5 PM

2
Day

Venosa

Thirty minutes east and you're in Venosa, ranked among Italy's most beautiful towns. Founded as a Roman colony (Venusia) in 291 BC, it's the birthplace of the poet Horace (65-8 BC)—a statue depicts him in the central square. The "House of Horace," reconstructed using experimental archaeological techniques on the foundations of a 2nd-century AD domus, can be visited. This is also where Hannibal ambushed two Roman consuls in 208 BC.

The Archaeological Park of Venosa comprises the remains of ancient Venusia: a Roman amphitheater, baths, a domus with mosaic fragments, and sections of the Via Appia that crossed the city. But the crown jewel is the Abbey of the Trinity complex. The old church (5th-6th century) is built on the ruins of a Roman temple and houses the tombs of the Hautevilles—the Norman conquerors of southern Italy, including Robert Guiscard. The church is still active, run by the Trinitarian Order.

Just behind it, the Incompiuta (the Unfinished) is a striking sight. Begun in the late 11th century to replace the old church that had become too small, this basilica was never completed. You walk between massive stone walls and pillars that support nothing—the sky is the ceiling, grass the floor. The columns and capitals recycled from Roman, Lombard, and Norman monuments create a unique architectural blend. It's one of the most moving sites in all of Basilicata.

In central Venosa, the Castello Pirro del Balzo (1470) imposes itself with its square plan flanked by four cylindrical towers and its drawbridge over the moat. It houses the National Archaeological Museum with coins, pottery, and funerary elements. If you have time, visit the Jewish catacombs (3rd-7th century), testimony to one of Italy's oldest Jewish communities.

→

Enjoy the evening tasting Aglianico del Vulture, Basilicata's great red wine. This grape variety grown on Vulture's volcanic soils produces tannic, deep wines often compared to Piedmont's Barolo. Several wineries around Venosa and Rionero in Vulture offer tastings. Pair your glass with soppressata, caciocavallo podolico, and taralli with fennel seeds.

💡 Book the day before—many wineries only receive visitors by appointment

3
Day

Castelmezzano

Leave Venosa heading south. After 40 minutes' drive, stop in Acerenza, perched at 833 meters altitude on a tuff spur. Horace already mentioned it as "the eagle's nest of high Acerenza." The cathedral, dedicated to Santa Maria Assunta, was built in 1080 on a Cluniac model and remodeled in 1281 in Romano-Gothic style. Its choir with ambulatory and radiating chapels is rare in southern Italy. The Renaissance crypt (Cappella Ferrillo, 1524) houses a bust of Emperor Julian the Apostate from the 4th century. From the Belvedere Torretta next door, the view plunges over the Vulture hills.

Resume the road south (about 1 hour) to reach Castelmezzano. The village of 800 souls is embedded in the sandstone spires of the Lucanian Dolomites. Its name comes from the Latin Castrum Medianum. Classified among Italy's "Most Beautiful Villages," it preserves traces of the Normans, Swabians, and even the Templars, whose cross is visible on the walls of the church of Santa Maria dell'Olmo (13th century). Climb the Gradinata Normanna, 54 steps carved into living rock that led to the castle's watchtower. The ascent is short but vertiginous—the visit can only be done with a licensed guide.

→

The Volo dell'Angelo (Flight of the Angel) is the star attraction of the Lucanian Dolomites. Hooked to a steel cable, you fly over the valley between Castelmezzano and Pietrapertosa at 400 meters altitude, covering 1,452 meters distance, at speeds reaching 120 km/h. Two lines exist (one in each direction). The ticket includes both flights and the shuttle between villages.

💡 Operates May to October. Book online. Min. weight 35 kg, max. 120 kg
→

For hikers, the Path of the Seven Stones connects Castelmezzano to Pietrapertosa through the valley (1.5 hours one way). Seven stations marked by sculptures and poems evoke local legends. You can also attempt the via ferratas Salemm or Marcirosa, of moderate difficulty, with a 72-meter Nepalese bridge suspended 35 meters above ground.

💡 Good shoes required. For via ferratas, arrange guide and equipment

4
Day

Aliano

Leave Castelmezzano in the morning heading southeast (1.5 hours' drive). Craco appears in the distance, perched on its spur like a phantom apparition amid the clay hills. This medieval village founded in the 8th century was abandoned in 1963 due to a landslide, then definitively evacuated after the 1980 Irpinia earthquake. The guided tour (mandatory, 60-90 minutes) departs from the Museo Emozionale in the former San Pietro convent. Helmeted, you enter the secured ruins: the Chiesa Madre San Nicola (14th century) with a saint's mummified body, the Norman tower, the Maronna and Grossi palaces. Craco served as a set for Mel Gibson's The Passion of the Christ and appears on the World Monuments Fund Watch List.

Continue to Aliano (30 minutes' drive). The village is perched on a ridge of white clay amid a lunar landscape. This is where Carlo Levi, a Turin physician and painter and anti-fascist, was confined from October 3, 1935 to May 20, 1936. This experience produced Christ Stopped at Eboli. Visit the Carlo Levi Picture Gallery (photos and paintings from exile), his reconstructed house, and the House of Malocchio—a dwelling built in the shape of a human face to ward off evil spirits. At the cemetery, Levi's grave: he wanted to be buried here, in this village he had come to love.

→

In late afternoon, walk to the Calanchi viewpoint. These geological formations—deep gullies in the clay, sculpted by erosion over millions of years—create a landscape of sharp ridges, whitish pinnacles, and desert-like valleys. The silence is total, broken only by the flight of black kites. It's the exact setting described by Levi in his book. Marked trails cross the Regional Natural Park of the Calanchi—bring good shoes and avoid rainy days.

💡 Free access year-round. The clay becomes very slippery in wet weather

Morning drive from Aliano to Matera (1 hour 15 minutes). The arrival is spectacular: the Sassi—troglodyte districts carved into the limestone of a canyon—appear suddenly. Drop your bags and devote the afternoon to a first exploration of Sasso Barisano, the larger and more restored of the two troglodyte quarters. Its dwellings, carved into calcarenite (soft limestone), have been occupied since the Paleolithic and inhabited continuously until 1952, when the government evacuated 15,000 people living in deplorable conditions. Since the 1990s, the district has been rehabilitated—the caves have become hotels, restaurants, and workshops.

Climb to the Cattedrale di Matera (13th century), in Apulian Romanesque style, which dominates the Sassi from the summit of Civita, the rocky promontory between the two quarters. Its facade features a beautiful 16-ray rose window. From the parvis, the view plunges over Sasso Barisano below—it's one of the city's most iconic images.

→

Enjoy sunset over the Sassi. The best spot is the Belvedere Murgia Timone, on the other side of the Gravina (the canyon), accessible by car from the SP3 road toward Laterza (10 minutes). From this natural viewpoint, the frontal view of the entire city illuminated by the low light is one of the most photographed panoramas in southern Italy. In summer, stay until the Sassi light up at dusk—the spectacle is magical.

💡 Arrive 30 minutes before sunset to have time to settle in

Second day in Matera, devoted to Sasso Caveoso (south side), wilder and less touristy. Here you'll find the most impressive rock churches. The Chiesa di Santa Maria de Idris, carved into a rocky outcrop in the 13th century, preserves Byzantine frescoes from the 12th to 17th centuries. Below, the crypt of San Giovanni in Monterrone is even older. Allow a good hour to explore this maze of caves, staircases, and passages carved into stone.

Visit a Casa Grotta (reconstructed cave house) to understand how Sassi inhabitants lived until the 1950s: a single room for the entire family, livestock housed in the alcove at the back, an ingenious rainwater collection system via channels carved in the rock. The constant humidity and absence of natural light had created a catastrophic sanitary situation.

For contemporary art lovers, MUSMA (Museum of Contemporary Sculpture) is installed in the caves of Palazzo Pomarici, in Sasso Caveoso. Contemporary sculptures exhibited in cavities carved into rock millennia ago. The contrast between the prehistoric setting and modern works is striking.

→

Keep the rest of the day free to wander the Sassi at your own pace. Matera is a city that rewards exploration: the streets are sloped, the staircases countless, and beauty reveals itself in details—a half-open cave door, a carved limestone balcony, an unexpected view of the canyon. Get deliberately lost in Sasso Caveoso, push open the doors of artisan workshops, settle on a terrace in a cave-bar for coffee or aperitivo facing the tuff rooftops.

💡 Late afternoon, when the light is golden and tourist groups have left, Matera reveals its true beauty
→

If you have the energy, cross the Gravina on foot to reach the Parco della Murgia Materana. The trail leads to rock churches lost in the scrubland, including the Chiesa della Madonna delle Tre Porte with its 12th-century frescoes. Allow 2 hours round trip. This isn't essential if you enjoyed the viewpoint the previous day, but the reverse perspective—seeing Matera from afar—is unique.

💡 Optional. Trail unmarked in places—bring GPS. Avoid in midsummer during hot hours

Transition day: the morning is devoted to the drive from Matera to the Tyrrhenian coast (about 2.5 hours via the Basentana and A2). You pass from the mineral world of the Sassi to the blue of the Mediterranean. Arrive in Maratea around noon, lunch on fresh fish at the port, and begin visits in early afternoon. Maratea is nicknamed the "pearl of the Tyrrhenian"—it's Basilicata's only access to the western sea, on 32 km of wild coastline.

Climb to the summit of Monte San Biagio (623 m) to see the Cristo Redentore. Standing 21.20 meters tall with an arm span of 19 meters, this statue in Carrara marble and cement was created by Florentine sculptor Bruno Innocenti between 1963 and 1965. It's the third-tallest statue of Jesus in Europe. Distinctive feature: unlike Rio's Christ from which it draws inspiration, its arms are slightly folded backward, giving it an almost floating appearance. The view from the base embraces the entire coast.

Below the statue, the Basilica of San Biagio has preserved the relics of Maratea's patron saint since 732 AD. Descending, you'll pass through the ruins of Maratea castle, destroyed by the French in 1806. The path offers spectacular viewpoints at every turn.

Descend to the historic center (Il Borgo), a labyrinth of medieval alleyways. Maratea is called the "city of 44 churches"—an impressive number for 5,000 inhabitants. Wander between Piazza Vitolo and Piazza Buragli, enter the baroque chapels you encounter.

→

Finish on one of Maratea's beaches. The Spiaggia Nera (Black Beach), with dark volcanic sand, offers a striking contrast with the turquoise water. For more tranquility, head to the cove of Cala Jannita or Acquafredda beach. Operators offer boat trips to explore sea caves inaccessible by land.

💡 In summer, arrive early—the coves are small and fill up quickly

8
Day

Rotonda

From Maratea, reach the western slope of Pollino National Park (about 1 hour 15 minutes of mountain driving). With its 192,000 hectares spread between Basilicata and Calabria, it's Italy's largest national park. The massif culminates at Serra Dolcedorme at 2,267 meters. Base yourself in Rotonda, a small gateway town to the park—it has nothing touristy in itself, but it's the best base camp for exploring Pollino, with accommodations, restaurants, and the visitor center.

Drive to San Costantino Albanese (30 minutes from Rotonda), one of the last Arbëreshë (Albanian) villages in Basilicata. Founded in the 15th century by Albanian refugees fleeing the Ottoman invasion, it still preserves the language, traditional costumes, and Byzantine rite. The church of the Madonna della Stella celebrates its services in Old Albanian. During certain festivals, inhabitants wear gold-embroidered costumes centuries old.

→

In the afternoon, hike toward the loricato pines (Pinus heldreichii), Europe's oldest trees. Some Pollino specimens are over 900 years old—twisted trunks, bleached by sun and wind, growing on ridges between 1,800 and 2,200 meters altitude. The trail from Piano Ruggio (1,550 m) is the most accessible: allow 2 to 3 hours round trip to reach Serra di Crispo, nicknamed the "Garden of the Gods." From the summit, the view simultaneously embraces the Tyrrhenian coast to the west and the Ionian coast to the east.

💡 Start early, bring water and windbreaker—weather changes quickly at altitude. Trail is marked but rocky
9
Day

Rotonda

Explore Pollino's eastern slope. Drive to Terranova di Pollino (30 minutes from Rotonda), a lost village at 926 meters altitude, departure point for trails to the summits. The medieval village, clinging to the rock, has preserved its stone alleyways and mountain water fountains. It's one of Basilicata's least populated municipalities—you'll encounter practically no one.

Descend to Viggianello, one of Pollino's "Most Beautiful Villages of Italy." The ancient Castrum Byanelli, mentioned in 1079, preserves a Sanseverino castle transformed into a noble residence in the 16th century. The centro storico's alleyways, lined with Byzantine churches (including San Sebastiano, remodeled in the 15th century) and noble palaces, offer a concentrate of Lucanian medieval architecture. The surrounding beech and fir forests are among the densest in southern Italy.

→

If you're athletic, descend the Raganello Gorges, on Pollino's Calabrian side (1 hour drive from Rotonda, via Civita). This 17 km canyon, up to 700 meters deep in places, is traversed by canyoning with a licensed guide. The lower route (3 hours, accessible to beginners) crosses passages of crystal-clear water between vertiginous limestone walls. Civita, the Albanian village above the gorges, is worth a stop for its architecture and panoramic bridge suspended over the void.

💡 Book in advance with park-licensed guides. Bring swimsuit and water shoes
→

For a more contemplative end to your trip, opt for a walk in the beech forests of Pollinello or a horseback ride in the high-altitude meadows. Several agriturismi around Rotonda and Viggianello offer equestrian outings. In the evening, taste the peperone di Senise IGP (dried pepper, base of Lucanian cuisine) and Pane di Matera IGP.

💡 Pollino's agriturismi often serve the best meals—book dinner on-site
